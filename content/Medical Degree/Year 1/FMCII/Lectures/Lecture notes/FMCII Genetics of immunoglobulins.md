## 1. The problem: one gene, one protein?
- We have a huge repertoire of receptors, a receptor for each different antigen: theoretically billions ("hundreds of billions").
- The old dogma "one gene, one protein" cannot explain it: the human genome has about **20,000 protein-coding genes**, but there are millions to billions of different antibodies, each a different protein. [Slides p3] ~20,000 protein-coding genes vs **10^7-10^11 different antibodies**. [Lucía] Same.
- **Anecdote, the Spanish Christmas lotteries:**
  - The Christmas lottery needs **one ball for every possible number**: almost **100,000 balls** in a big drum. Equivalent: one gene for each antibody.
  - The lottery right after Christmas uses **five small drums of 10 balls each**: one ball from each drum gives each digit. With **50 balls** it generates the same numbers. Equivalent: the Ig genes.

## 2. Structure of Ig genes
- Ig molecule: **two identical heavy chains + two identical light chains** (light chain **kappa or lambda**) [Slides p6]. So at least one gene for the heavy chain and one for the light chain.
- Two solutions:
  1. **Each type of chain is encoded on a separate chromosome.**
  2. The gene is **not used as a whole**: by **recombination, only parts of the genes** are used [Slides p6: "By using recombination, PARTS of genes are used to create different possible antibodies"].
- **Chromosomes** [Slides p7]: each set of gene families is on a separate chromosome. Human **chr 2, chr 22, chr 14**; mouse **chr 6, chr 16, chr 12**. Which chain is on which chromosome is labelled in the figure (not in the slide text): **see the slide**.
- **Gene segments** [Slides p8]: **variable (V), diversity (D), joining (J), constant (C)**. **D segments only in heavy chains.** Light chains: V, J, C. Heavy chain: V, D, J, C. "Tightly regulated machinery controls the recombination processes."
- Principle: from each collection of segments **one is chosen**, and they are joined by **recombination**, like one ball from each drum. Each combination gives a different protein.

## 3. Light chains
### 3a. CDRs and where they come from
- Recall from [[FMCII Immunoglobulins structure and isotypes]]: specificity comes from the three **hypervariable regions = CDRs** of each chain ("fingers"); the six together (light + heavy) form the **idiotype**.
- **CDR1 and CDR2: inside the V segment. CDR3: the V-J junction** [Slides p9: "V: CDR1 y CDR2; V-J: CDR3"]. [Lucía] Same.
- So **CDR3 has the highest variability**: CDR1 and CDR2 come from one chosen V segment, while CDR3 depends on which V is joined to which J.

### 3b. Kappa [Slides p10]
| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| Germline DNA (example) | **35 V**, **5 J**, **one C (Cκ)**: an undifferentiated B cell has every segment in its DNA |
| 1. Recombination (DNA) | One V is chosen and joined to one J; **everything in between is eliminated**. Example: **V32-J4** |
| 2. Primary transcript (RNA) | Transcribed from the rearranged DNA |
| 3. Mature mRNA | **Introns removed** (V32 J4 Cκ), then translated into the kappa chain |

- **35 V x 5 J = 175 combinations (kappa)** [Slides p10]. [Lucía] 175.
- Textbooks give different numbers of V and J segments depending on how old they are, but the potential combinations come out about the same.

### 3c. Lambda [Slides p11]
- Different organization: instead of one C joined to the VJ product, **each J is paired with its own C (J-C pairs)**: the chosen V joins a J that brings its own C, "in a block".

| λ locus [Slides p11] | Humans | Mice |
|---|---|---|
| Functional Vλ segments | ~30-33 | only 3 |
| Functional Jλ-Cλ pairs | 4-5 | only 3 |
| Possible combinations | ~120-132 | ~4-6 useful |
| **Serum κ:λ ratio** | **60:40** | **95:5** |

- **Why the ratio differs:** "just by chance, by statistics". The number of segments available decides how often each chain is produced: humans have many more λ combinations than mice, so λ chains are easier to generate in humans.
- [Lucía] About 30 Vλ genes and 7 JCλ pairs, of which **four** are functional **[check]**: slides say 4-5 functional pairs.

## 4. Heavy chain [Slides p12-14]
- All four segment types: **V, D, J, C**.
- **CDR1 and CDR2: in the V segment. CDR3: spans V-D-J** (the D segment is small, so CDR3 covers the junctions) [Slides p12: "V-D-J: CDR3"]. So heavy-chain CDR3 is "very, very, very variable", and together with the light chain the number of idiotypes becomes enormous.
- Segment numbers:

| Segment | Lecturer | [Slides p13] | [Lucía] |
|---|---|---|---|
| VH | 40-50 | 45-50 functional | about 40 |
| DH | 20-30 | 23-30 | (not given) |
| JH | 6 | 6 | (not given) |
| CH | **8** | **8**, encoding the different isotypes | 8 |

- **Not a single C:** each isotype has its own constant region: **Cμ = IgM, Cδ = IgD**, and the others for the IgG subclasses and IgA [Slides p14: order of the C segments in the figure, see the slide].
- **Two recombination events** (light chains have one) [Slides p14]:
  1. **D-J** first.
  2. Then a **V** is joined to the DJ. Example: **V2-D3-J6**.
- The rearranged DNA **still contains all the C regions**. So the VDJ **fixes the specificity** of that B cell, but **not the isotype**: the cell can still make any isotype with that single specificity.
- **Transcription chooses Cμ** first: **IgM**. Then mRNA maturation removes the introns; translation gives the μ heavy chain. [Lucía] "The first selection... will always correspond to IgM".
- **45 V x 23 D x 6 J ~ 6,000 combinations (heavy chain)** [Slides p14]; lecturer: "in the order of 6,000".
- **Student questions:**
  - *Does recombination happen twice, between the primary transcript and the mature mRNA?* No: that step is **not recombination** but normal **mRNA maturation** (splicing): introns are transcribed but excised, and only the remaining sections are translated. This happens in many mRNAs.
  - *How is the constant region chosen?* Later, by **isotype switching** (after Christmas).

## 5. Mechanism of V(D)J recombination [Slides p15-17]
- **Very tightly regulated**, because it uses enzymes the cell normally uses to **repair DNA**. Cells must avoid mutations, but B cells (and T cells, next lecture) use some of these enzymes "not to repair but to cut and paste" DNA fragments. [Slides p15] Proteins also involved in DNA repair: **RAG-1/2, TdT**.
- **Recombination signal sequences (RSSs)** flank each segment and direct the process. Each RSS: a conserved **heptamer (7 nucleotides)** and **nonamer (9 nucleotides)**, separated by a spacer of **12 or 23** nucleotides (bp) [Slides p15].
- **12/23 rule** [Slides p16]: a **12 RSS always pairs with a 23 RSS**, never 12 with 12 or 23 with 23.
  - This decides **which segment joins which**: why D never joins D, J never joins J, and in the heavy chain V cannot join J directly, skipping D. "There's no other possibility."
  - (Which segment carries 12 or 23: **not to be learned**, exam flags. His heavy-chain example: V segments 23, D segments 12 on both sides, J segments 23; light chain: "23 and 12"; heavy chain: "23, 12, 12" **[verify]**, partly garbled.)
  - [Lucía] The sequences are the same but inverted, so when the chromosome bends they can pair; two 12 RSSs lie in opposite orientations and would not match.
- **Only in lymphocytes**: only lymphocytes have the enzymes that recognize these sequences, and **only while they are being generated** (preparing their receptors). [Slides p16] **Recombinase complex V(D)J: RAG-1/RAG-2**; only in lymphocytes during their development; other enzymes involved; **irreversible**.
- **Steps** (his description of the figure, [Slides p17]: Nona-12-Hepta ... Hepta-23-Nona):
  1. The DNA **bends** so that **nonamer pairs with nonamer and heptamer with heptamer**.
  2. The DNA is **cut** there; **what lies between is excised**.
  3. The remaining chromosome is **ligated**: a new junction (VJ, DJ or VDJ).
- [Lucía] RAG-1/RAG-2 cleave at the RSS; Artemis, TdT, DNA ligase IV process the ends; the coding ends form the **coding joint**; the RSSs form a circular **signal joint**, which plays no further role.

## 6. Junctional diversity [Slides p18]
- The segment combinations alone do **not give billions**: other mechanisms are needed, because **cutting and ligation are not perfect** [Slides p18: "Sometimes the fusion of V, D and J segments is not exact"; **80% of adult human antibodies** show some junctional diversity].
- The three imperfect events, as he explained them (partly garbled):

| Event | Lecturer | [Slides p19] | [Lucía] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exonuclease trimming (deletion)** | By chance an **exonuclease removes a couple of nucleotides** before ligation: a **frameshift**, so a different amino acid sequence from that point. Random | Sometimes occurs at junctions, **losing nucleotides and changing reading frames** | Same; changes the reading frame |
| **P nucleotide addition** | Cleavage is **asymmetrical** instead of blunt; the gap is **filled with nucleotides**, then ligated: a new DNA and protein sequence | **Templated** nucleotide addition between joints, from **asymmetrical cleaving of hairpin structures** | Asymmetrical ends; nucleotides added; **no change in the reading frame** |
| **N nucleotide addition** | "Instead of eliminating a nucleotide, a **new nucleotide is added**": again a new frame | **Non-templated**, mediated by **TdT**, random nucleotides between joints | Random nucleotides, TdT; **changes the reading frame**; she links it to "symmetrical ends" |

- **[check]** In his recap he described N addition as "the non-templated, I mean the **asymmetrical cleavage** and then the addition of new nucleotide"; the slides link asymmetrical cleavage to **P** nucleotides and TdT to **N**. Go with the slides.
- **[check]** Reading frame: Lucía says P addition does **not** change the frame and N does; he did not say, and the slides give a frame change only for exonuclease trimming.
- Numbers: about **6,000 heavy, ~200 kappa, almost 200 lambda** combinations; without these junctional events the theoretical number would be "in the order of **2 million**" (transcript "2 years" **[verify]**; later "the original two, which give rise to two millions"). The real number is much higher because of these additions and deletions.
- **Student questions:**
  - *Do we need them?* "Yes, yes, yes": with only the segment combinations the repertoire would be much narrower and might not cover all potential antigens.
  - *How does the body actively produce an error?* It is an error, "but it is an error which is productive", used only for these genes, not for our other genes.

## 7. Mechanisms of antibody diversity [Slides p19-21]
| Mechanism | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Combinatorial diversity** | Naive B cells | Which **heavy chain pairs with which light chain** |
| **Multiple gene segments** | Naive B cells | Which **V, (D), J** are put together (the ~2 million) |
| **P nucleotide addition** | Naive B cells | Section 6 |
| **N nucleotide addition** | Naive B cells | Section 6 (TdT) |
| **Exonuclease trimming** | Naive B cells | Section 6 |
| **Somatic hypermutation** | After antigen encounter | **New mutations in the variable regions** (in a couple of weeks) |
| **Isotype switching** | After antigen encounter | **Same variable regions, same specificity, different constant region**, e.g. IgM then IgG: also a source of variability |

- [Slides p21] lists isotype switching and somatic hypermutation as "(additional)" mechanisms; [Lucía] puts them under mature B cells.
- All five naive mechanisms together: **~10^7-10^11** [Slides p20]; he said "tens, even thousands of millions of billions" **[verify]** (garbled).
- This is the repertoire of **naive B cells** (never seen the antigen): many naive cells, **each with a different antibody**. Once a cell has seen the antigen (non-naive), it undergoes more mutations, so diversity increases further.
- [Lucía] Somatic hypermutation: same antigen recognized with different affinities.

## 8. Allelic exclusion [Slides p22]
- **Very important:** every cell produces **a single antibody**. Each gene is on **two chromosomes** (one per allele). If both alleles of the heavy chain and both of the light chain rearranged at random at the same time, one cell would make "a mess of antibodies" mixing different heavy and light chains.
- So **only one chromosome is used at a time** = **allelic exclusion** ("every time a single allele, I mean a single chromosome, is being used").
- **Order of attempts:**

| Step | Chain | If productive | If not productive |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Heavy chain, allele 1** (D-J, then V-DJ) | Go to the light chain | Try allele 2 |
| 2 | Heavy chain, allele 2 | Go to the light chain | **Cell dies** (no heavy chain) |
| 3 | **Kappa, allele 1** (always first) | Full antibody ready | Try kappa allele 2 |
| 4 | Kappa, allele 2 | Full antibody ready | Go to lambda |
| 5-6 | **Lambda, allele 1, then allele 2** | Full antibody ready | Cell dies [Lucía; Slides p22: nonproductive rearrangements lead to apoptosis during development] |

- So **2 attempts for the heavy chain and 4 for the light chain** (transcript "four attempts with the lambda" **[verify]**: from the sequence he describes, 2 kappa + 2 lambda).
- **Non-productive** = e.g. a P or N addition causes a frameshift that creates a **stop codon**: the chain is much shorter and cannot form. Recombination on that chromosome stops and the cell gets a second chance on the other chromosome.
- The **heavy chain alone** (before any light chain) is enough for the cell to "know" whether things are going correctly: "as we will see later" (next lecture: [[FMCII B cell ontogeny]]).
- [Slides p22] Ensures that each B cell synthesizes **only one heavy and one light chain**. [Lucía] The light chain is kappa or lambda, never both; a molecule that cannot act as a receptor and signal means the cell dies.

## 9. Summary [Slides p23]
- Multiple genes encoding the different Ig chains support Ig variability.
- **Rearrangement of the DNA segments** (V with D and J, or V with J) gives a diverse repertoire.
- **Junctional diversity** (P and N additions, deletions) clearly increases the number of potential Ig sequences.
- All these mechanisms together explain the vast repertoire.
- **Student question, clonal selection:** *if all this is random, how does the body know which antibody to reproduce against a pathogen?* Recall clonal selection (first day): many lymphocytes, each with a different receptor; **only the one that recognizes its antigen gets an activation signal** and **proliferates**; the rest stay resting ("nobody has touched the button"). At first the antibody is only a **receptor**; once activated and proliferating, the cell starts to **secrete** it.
