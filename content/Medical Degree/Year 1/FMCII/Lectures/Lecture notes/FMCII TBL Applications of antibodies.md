## 2. Modes of action in therapy [S1 p4-5]
The two properties that are exploited:
- **Specificity**, from the **variable regions**: binds a target that is either **soluble** or **expressed on a cell membrane**.
- **Effector function**, from the **constant domain (Fc)**: determines what happens after binding.

[S1 p4] figure labels, around a target cell: **Blocking** (ligand antagonist, receptor antagonist), **Signaling**, **Targeting** (toxin/radiation), **ADCC**, **CDC**, with an effector cell. Caption: "Isotype importance??".

### 2a. Ligand antagonism (blocking)
- The antigen is a **receptor** on the target cell. When it binds its natural ligand it triggers a signalling cascade.
- An antibody can **block the ligand**, inhibiting the signal. Clinical logic: if the signal induces **proliferation** of a tumour cell, blocking it stops the proliferation signal.
- In pure blocking, "we don't care whether the target cell is dying": **no Fc receptor engagement is needed**, so no effector cell is involved and the isotype matters less for that purpose (section 2f).

### 2b. Agonism (signalling)
- The opposite case: we **want** the cell to proliferate or activate, so the antibody **mimics the natural ligand** and acts as an agonist.
- **Why bivalency matters:** a one-to-one receptor-ligand engagement "is not the best option to induce the activation". Activation is usually mediated by **several receptors engaged at the same time** by a multimeric or polymeric ligand.
- An antibody has **two antigen-binding sites**, so it binds two receptor monomers at once: **cross-linking** of the receptor. "The signal coming through that is much stronger". The higher the cross-linking (bivalent antibodies, polyvalent ligands), the stronger the signal.
- For this, what is needed is a **clustering receptor**, not an ADCC-capable one.

### 2c. ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity)
- Target cell expresses the antigen, the antibody binds it, and an **effector cell** recognises the antibody's **constant region through its Fc receptors**. This activates the effector cell, which kills the target. Goal: **lysis of the target cell**.
- Used to "increase the capability of the effector cells to kill the desired targets", for example in cancer.

### 2d. CDC (complement-dependent cytotoxicity)
- Antibodies bound to a target cell that are able to **activate the complement cascade** induce cytotoxicity. The mechanisms of complement activation come after Christmas.

### 2e. Opsonization and phagocytosis
- He added it after the slide: cells or molecules **coated by antibodies** are then phagocytosed. Also needs an Fc receptor.
- [S2 p2] lists it as **ADCP, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis**.

### 2f. Targeting (artificial conjugates)
- "Artificial molecules" that combine antibody specificity with the therapeutic properties of a drug: the antibody is linked to a **radioactive isotope**, a **toxin** or similar, to induce **selective killing** of the target cell. Expanded in section 10 (ADCs).

## 3. Isotype and FcγR interactions [S1 p5]
- All the Fc-dependent mechanisms above (depletion, opsonization, ADCC) need a **constant region that an Fc receptor can recognise**. These receptors are named **Fc** plus a letter: **FcγR** is the receptor for the **constant region of IgG** ("because these are some of the most common antibodies used").
- **Two kinds of FcγR:**

| Type | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Activating** | The signal **induces cytotoxicity** by the effector cell, e.g. an NK cell killing by ADCC | FcγRIIA, FcγRIIIA [S1 p5] |
| **Inhibitory** | The signal **blocks** the cytotoxicity: "they do just the opposite" | FcγRIIB [S1 p5] |

- "So this has also to be engineered": the antibody must be chosen or engineered to engage the **activating** receptor, not the inhibitory one.
- **The slide's three-case figure** [S1 p5, from Beers SA et al. *Blood* 2016;127:1097-1101], which is the organising scheme of the whole session:

| Case | What is needed | Optimal isotype on the figure | Example targets on the figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Depleting** | **Activating vs inhibitory receptor for ADCC** | hIgG1, Fc engineered, glyco-optimised | CD20, HER2, EGFR, CTLA4, GITR, OX40, PDL1 |
| **B. Agonistic** | **Clustering receptor, no ADCC** | hIgG1 SE/LF, hIgG2(B) | CD40, CD27, OX40, CD137, GITR [verify]: the last target label on the figure is not legible |
| **C. Blocking** | **No receptor, just blockade** | hIgG4, FcγR null | HER2, EGFR, CTLA4, PD1, PDL1 |

- Target choice logic, in his words: to **deplete** cells expressing tumour antigens, or to deplete **immunosuppressive** cells, use a depleting antibody with an **IgG1** constant region. For agonism, the result differs because of **FcγRIIB vs FcγRIIA**, but "what is important here is the type of target which is recognized".

## 4. Monoclonal antibodies: hybridoma technology [S1 p6]
### 4a. Why they were needed
- **The original source of antibodies was animals.** Students answered "mice"; he added **horses, rabbits, goats**: big animals immunized to obtain enough serum, from which antibodies were purified.
- This is "quite homemade". For a **clinical trial**, testing a medicine in a homogeneous way across cohorts, you cannot rely on that kind of preparation: you need something **homogeneous, repeatable and reliable** for future applications.
- [Lucía] Monoclonal antibody technology was developed **in the 70s** to solve two problems: **control specificity** and **obtain as many antibodies as required**.

### 4b. Polyclonal serum
- Immunize a mouse with an antigen, for example a protein. A folded protein has **several exposed regions**, each recognised by a different antibody: each region is an **epitope**.
- With four epitopes in his example, after immunization and a good memory response the serum contains antibodies against epitopes 1, 2, 3 and 4, each made by a different B cell: a **polyclonal serum**.
- **Student question:** *how do you make a mouse recognise something like pollen as foreign?* Anything exogenous, from another species, is **seen as strange and is generally immunogenic**. **Follow-up:** *but some people do not react to pollen.* Pollen is an **allergen**, not a normal antigen; think instead of antigens from microorganisms, where in most cases contact does induce a response.

### 4c. Making the hybridoma
- Take the **spleen** of the immunized mouse: it contains the different B cells, each producing a different antibody.
- Fuse them in vitro with **myeloma cells**: tumour cells of the **B cell lineage**. Two relevant features: a tumour cell **proliferates spontaneously** ("you do not need to do anything"), and because of the malignant transformation myeloma cells **do not produce antibodies**.
- The cell fused is the **plasma cell**, the cell that results from the differentiation of a B cell and is able to secrete antibody.
- The fusion product is a **hybridoma**: **proliferative capacity** from the myeloma, **antibody-producing capacity and specificity** from the plasma cell. In his example, four different hybridomas, so the supernatant still contains all four antibodies: still polyclonal.
- [Lucía] Same division of labour; "clones coming from a single cell = monoclonal".

### 4d. Cloning by limiting dilution
- A bulk culture of mixed hybridomas is **diluted and diluted** across hundreds or thousands of tubes until there is **a single cell per tube**.
- That cell proliferates into a **clone of identical cells**, all secreting antibody against a single epitope. Each tube's medium then contains antibody of **one specificity and one type of constant region**.
- Result: "an unlimited source of antibodies". Both the **specificity** and the **biological properties** of the antibody are determined by the **original plasma cell** that was fused (used again in the TBL question in section 12c).
- **Attribution:** the transcript names the developer as "this guy, Nils-Ten" **[verify]**: garbled, and the slides do not name anyone.

## 5. CD nomenclature [S1 p7]
### 5a. How it arose
- After the technology spread, everyone was immunizing and making monoclonals, but **nobody knew which epitope each monoclonal was directed against**.
- Laboratories studying lymphocytes, macrophages, monocytes and neutrophils immunized mice with **human blood cells**; since human cells are foreign to the mouse, it raised antibodies against antigens on them, and each lab ended up with different antibodies.
- Characterisation was by **electrophoresis**: lyse the lymphocytes, separate the whole protein repertoire by size, and see which band the antibody recognises, for example "a 40 kDa protein expressed by leukocytes".
- The same molecule was being called different names in different labs: "a complete mess". So the labs **met in workshops and shared their antibodies**, found that many were recognising the **same molecules**, and grouped them.
- A **CD = cluster designation** is therefore a **cluster of monoclonal antibodies that all recognise the same cell surface molecule** [S1 p7].

### 5b. What CDs are used for
- [S1 p7] **Phenotypic characterization of cells**: subset identification, functional status, isolation. Commonly used in **flow cytometry** and **IHC**. **Over 300 CD markers.**
- **Lineage and stage markers** as he gave them:

| Marker | Cell |
|---|---|
| **CD34** | Stem cell, undifferentiated precursor; lost as the cell differentiates |
| **CD45** | All leukocytes: "it can be a lymphocyte, it can be a neutrophil, it can be a macrophage, but all of them express CD45". A CD45-positive cell in a tissue tells a pathologist it is a leukocyte |
| **CD3** | T cells |
| **CD19** | B cells ("that we saw yesterday") |
| **CD14** | Monocytes |
| **CD15** | Granulocytes |
| **CD4** | CD4 T cells (in addition to CD45 and CD3) |
| **CD8** | Cytotoxic T cells |

- [check] The transcript at one point reads "if it expresses CD19 that we saw yesterday, it is a T cell": CD19 is the B cell marker he gives everywhere else and in [[FMCII B cell ontogeny]]; a transcription slip.
- **Second use: dynamic expression.** Markers also distinguish whether a cell is **resting or activated**, and whether it is at the beginning, middle or end of differentiation or activation. Some markers are **constitutive** and some **inducible**, so expression can be followed over time.
- [Lucía] CD34 = precursor stem cell; CD3 = all T cells; CD20 = B cells; used for follow-up and diagnostic purposes.

## 6. Why humanization is needed
- Monoclonal technology came from **mice**. Administering a **murine antibody** to a person means administering something exogenous from another species: **the antibody itself becomes an antigen / immunogen**, and the patient makes antibodies against it.
- Consequence: the therapeutic antibody is **neutralized and eliminated**, so it cannot do its job.
- **The more often you administer it, the stronger the response**, so fully murine antibodies are useful mainly where you need a **single dose or very few doses** (see the acute vs chronic axis in section 7d).
- Two separate reasons human sequences are better, and he pushed the students on this:
  1. **Half-life:** murine antibodies are immunogenic, so they are neutralized and eliminated; human ones last longer. ("In a mouse the half-life will be equivalent... but mouse in human is not the same.")
  2. **Effector function:** effector functions depend on the **Fc region interacting with the corresponding Fc receptors**. Human cells have receptors adapted to human antibodies, so a **human Fc interacts better with the patient's Fc receptors** (for example on an NK cell). A student who answered only "there's less rejection" got "yes and no" for this reason.
- [Lucía] Same two points: human antibodies will not elicit an immune response on repeated dosage, and "human Fc region will fit in human Fc receptor better than a non-human one".

## 7. The humanization series [S1 p8-9]
### 7a. The four types
| Type | What is murine | What is human |
|---|---|---|
| **Murine** | The whole molecule | Nothing |
| **Chimeric** | The **whole variable region** | The **constant region**, by swapping in the human sequences by molecular biology |
| **Humanized** | **Only the hypervariable regions (CDRs)**, grafted in | Constant region **and the framework regions** that connect the CDRs |
| **Fully human** | Nothing | The whole molecule |

- The logic of the humanized step: the **minimal sequence needed to recognise the antigen is the hypervariable regions**, "those six fingers that we have" (from [[FMCII Immunoglobulins structure and isotypes]]). The **framework regions do not dictate specificity**, so they too can be replaced by human sequences, and "the possibility of having a rejection is even lower" (transcript "slower").
- [S1 p9] names the four production routes in the figure: **(A) mouse hybridoma** (chimerization, or CDR graft to a humanized mAb), **(B) phage display** (phage-displayed antibody libraries, biopanning with targets over 3-5 cycles, screening, construction of human IgG), **(C) transgenic mouse** (immunization with targets, harvest splenocytes, generate hybridomas), **(D) single B cell** (sort B cells with labelled antigens, PCR, construct VH and VL).

### 7b. Making fully human antibodies
Two routes he described (the continuation file):
1. **Transgenic mice:** mice genetically modified so that instead of the murine antibody genes their genome **contains the genes for human antibodies**. Immunize them and their antibodies carry human sequence; monoclonals can then be made from them as usual.
2. **Human B cells:** take cells from a person who has been **in contact with that antigen**, **sort and purify** the cells specific for it, then **clone the variable regions** and put them into whatever structure the desired isotype requires.
- [Lucía] adds a third: **immortalize a human antibody-producing cell**.

### 7c. Engineering the constant region
- Beyond the four categories, "some sequences in the constant region can be slightly modified to improve the interaction with the receptor" (matches "Fc engineered, glyco-optimised" on [S1 p5]).

### 7d. The acute/chronic trade-off [S1 p8]
The slide's figure runs **mouse to chimeric to humanized to human** against four scales, with **acute intervention** at the murine end and **chronic therapy** at the human end:

| Scale | Mouse | Chimeric | Humanized | Human |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Percentage of human sequence** | 0% | 80-90% | 90-95% | 100% |
| **Frequency of administration** | High | | | Low |
| **Repeated administration** (due to high antigenicity) | Difficult | | | Possible |
| **Effector function** (CDC, ADCC activity) | Low | | | High |

- His rule: "if you have a disease where you have to administer the antibody many times, because you are afraid of rejection, use this one", the fully human.

## 8. From hybridomas to antibody therapeutics: the development pipeline [S1 p10]
Order as he gave it:
1. **Choose the target**, so that acting on it modulates the disease.
2. **Decide the effector function:** eliminate, reduce, trigger a pathway, block. "Everything has to be thought."
3. **Prepare the antibody** in the right format: chimeric, humanized or human monoclonal; optionally **engineer the Fc**.
4. **Preclinical development**, in vitro and in vivo (an animal with tumours, test whether the tumour is inhibited).
5. **Biomanufacturing**, then **clinical trials** in humans.
6. If the **safety and efficacy endpoints** are met, **regulatory approval**; if not, go back to the start or to an intermediate step and rethink.
- [S1 p10] figure labels for the same loop: basic research into disease biology, therapeutic hypothesis, hybridoma technology or human monoclonal antibodies, chimeric or humanized antibodies, antibody engineering, preclinical development, biomanufacturing, clinical trials, safety and efficacy endpoints met?, regulatory approval of antibody therapeutic, with "insights from the clinic" and "biotechnology innovation" feeding back in.

## 9. Other antibody formats: bispecifics and immune engagers [S1 p11-12]
- Everything so far assumed the **classical structure**: two identical light chains and two identical heavy chains. In the lab you can "make things more complicated to improve their properties".
- **Bispecific antibodies** [S1 p11]: take **half of one antibody and half of another**, so **one arm recognises one target and the other arm another**: a **dual specificity**. Simultaneous targeting of two different epitopes or antigens; they operate through different mechanisms of action depending on the targets; they can evolve into more complex **bi- or multispecific** structures. He added that instead of two epitopes you can use **three or even five**.
- **Immune engagers** [S1 p12]: antibody-based molecules designed to **redirect effector cells to the tumour**; **approved for cancer therapy**. His example: a T cell can kill a tumour cell, so an antibody binds **something on the T cell** and **something on the tumour cell** and **brings the two together**, instead of leaving the T cell to find the target by screening around.

## 10. Antibody-drug conjugates [S1 p13]
- **ADC = monoclonal antibody bound to a cytotoxic drug through a chemical linker** [S1 p13]. Approved for cancer therapy.
- Component roles [S1 p13]:

| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| **Antibody** | Provides **specificity** and **increases half-life** |
| **Drug** | Cytotoxic capacity of the chemotherapeutic drug |
| **Linker** | Bridges both components, maintaining their properties and allowing **timely release** |

- His rationale: ordinary **chemotherapy is not specific** and hits many cells, which is why cancer patients have severe adverse events. Linking the drug to an antibody that recognises a target on the tumour cell means the drug **goes specifically to the tumour cell** and is **released there** (for example because the linker is cleaved), giving higher efficacy.

## 12. TBL questions, part 1 (monoclonals and humanization)
The session's questions with the reasoning he drew out. Options are given as transcribed.

### 12a. Modes of action of therapeutic antibodies
Only the tail of the stem and the options survive in the recording (see Sources). Options, as transcribed: (1) they can block the interaction between other cells and their ligands, preventing those signals; (2) they always actively activate complement independently of the isotype; (3) they exclusively act by direct killing through the antigen-binding regions; (4) they stimulate antibody production by B cells without interacting with the target. **Correct: 1.**
- Why not 2: **some isotypes activate complement and others do not**, so "always, independently of the isotype" is wrong.
- Why not 3: antibodies **do not kill target cells through the antigen-binding region**; killing is through **Fc receptor** binding by an effector cell such as an NK cell.
- Why not 4: monoclonal antibodies are not aimed at inducing antibody production; it could happen if they touched a receptor acting as a ligand, "but it is not a common mechanism".
- A student's first justification was "the other ones don't make sense" and "that just seems wrong"; his reply: **"just because it seems wrong, it should be a reason"**.
- **[verify]** Option 1 as transcribed is garbled ("They can't do interaction between other cells and its ligands"); the sense is receptor-ligand blockade.

### 12b. What does each epitope/clone recognise?
Stem: after immunization of mice with an antigen and generation of hybridomas, which is true? Options: all clones recognize the same epitope; all clones recognize several epitopes of the antigen; **each clone may recognize a different epitope**; each clone produces antibodies with the same functional properties. **Correct: each clone may recognize a different epitope.**
- **Not the same functional properties:** the pool of plasma cells is heterogeneous; one original B cell may be producing **IgG1**, another **IgM**, another **IgG4**. The functional properties follow **the original cell that was fused**.
- **Not "the same epitope"; this is why they are called monoclonal:** a clone is specific for an epitope.
- **Finer point he made, using his hand as a polyepitopic antigen:** even **within the same epitope** different antibodies bind with subtle differences. An epitope is roughly **4-6 amino acids**, and within those there are different ways of binding, so each antibody differs in the precise area and interaction, not in which epitope it names.

### 12c. What does each fusion partner contribute?
Stem: during hybridoma generation, splenocytes from an immunized mouse are fused with myeloma cells; what is the main contribution of each cell type? **Correct: splenocytes provide the antibody-producing cells with the desired specificity, whereas myeloma cells provide the capacity for continuous proliferation.**
- "Remember, the only thing that myeloma does is provide proliferative capacity."

### 12d. How to increase the proportion of antigen-specific hybridomas
Options: (1) fuse with a myeloma of higher proliferative capacity; (2) **improve the potency of the immunization protocol**; (3) immunize more animals; (4) it is random and cannot be increased. **Correct: 2.**
- Why not 1: the hybridomas would proliferate more, but **the proportion of antigen-specific ones would not change**.
- Why 2: a stronger immunization **increases the proportion of cells producing antibody against that antigen** in the spleen. The original repertoire is still there but is **biased**, so the chance that a fusion involves a cell specific for your antigen is higher. Practical corollary: **before killing the animal, check that the serum is rich in antibodies specific for your antigen**.
- Why not 3: with more animals the **proportion is the same**; "you have more work to do", more cells to screen, but you do not improve anything.
- Why not 4: it is not random; it depends on how many antigen-specific cells fuse.

### 12e. What dictates the isotype of the antibody secreted by a hybridoma?
Options: the original B cell partner; the myeloma; it is random after fusion; it switches from IgM to IgG during the process. **Correct: the original B cell partner.**
- The original cell provides **both the specificity and the isotype**: if it was making IgG1 the hybridoma releases IgG1, if IgG4 then IgG4. "Nothing changes", because that cell **has already undergone all the recombination events** ([[FMCII Genetics of immunoglobulins]]).

### 12f. Repeated administration and immunogenicity
Stem: a patient requires repeated administration of a therapeutic monoclonal antibody over a prolonged period; to minimize the risk of an immune response against the therapeutic antibody, what is most appropriate? Options: fully murine, because murine sequences are less immunogenic in humans; chimeric, because it contains more murine sequences than a humanized antibody; **humanized or fully human, because the higher proportion of human sequences generally reduces immunogenicity**. **Correct: humanized or fully human.**
- Framing he gave first: **"acute intervention versus chronic intervention"**, which were suitable for which (section 7d).
- Why not chimeric: it **still contains murine sequences in the variable region**, so it can still be immunogenic.

### 12g. Improving an ADCC-dependent antibody
Stem: a monoclonal antibody eliminates tumour cells expressing a specific surface antigen, and its activity depends on recruiting **NK cells through Fc receptor interaction**; which modification would most likely improve efficacy? Options: increase interaction with **inhibitory** FcγRIIB; **select an isotype or engineer the Fc region to increase binding to activating Fcγ receptors, particularly Fcγ receptor IIIA** (transcript "FC gamma receptor A" **[verify]**: the number is lost, and FcγRIIIA is the activating receptor named on [S1 p5]); eliminate the Fc-receptor interaction; prevent binding to the tumour antigen while keeping Fc receptor interaction. **Correct: the second.**
- Reasoning: on recognition, the NK cell must **activate**. Binding an inhibitory receptor would link the tumour cell to the NK cell but the NK cell "will not act". Eliminating the interaction or preventing tumour binding obviously defeats the purpose.

### 12h. Which reason favours humanization?
Options: (1) human antibodies have higher affinity for the antigen in humans than murine ones, providing stronger neutralization; (2) immunogenicity of murine antibodies in humans enhances their effector functions; (3) **functional properties of humanized antibodies mediated by the Fc region are accomplished in a proper way**; (4) humanized and murine antibodies have a similar half-life in patients. **Correct: 3.**
- Why not 1: **neutralization is not an Fc-dependent property**. Blocking a receptor-ligand interaction depends only on the antibody's capacity to block, that is on the **variable region**, not on the species of the constant region.
- Why not 2: an immunogenic murine antibody is **neutralized and eliminated**, which does not enhance anything.
- Why 3: the functional properties come from the **Fc binding Fc receptors**, and a **human Fc interacts better with the patient's Fc receptors**.
- Why not 4: murine antibodies are immunogenic, so they are eliminated; **human antibodies have the longer half-life in patients**. His caveat: mouse in mouse is equivalent to human in human; it is **mouse in human** that is not.

## 13. Monoclonal antibodies in cancer: three worked examples [S2 p1-8]
- Framing [S2 p1-2]: antibodies in cancer target antigens on **different cells** (the tumour cell, or other cells involved in recognising it), each based on a **different mechanism of action**, and **the isotype is the essential determinant** of that mechanism and so of efficacy.
- [S2 p2] "What do we want the antibody to do after binding its target?" In therapy: activation of **ADCC**, of **CDC**, of **ADCP**; interaction with **activating or inhibitory Fcγ receptors**; **recruitment (or avoidance) of immune cells**. Plus use in **diagnostic** and in **research**.
- His added case: if the tumour contains **suppressive cells** working against the immune response, the goal is instead to **deplete those cells**, which antibodies can also do.

### 13a. CD20 and rituximab [S2 p3-4]
**The target, CD20** [S2 p3]:
- Surface molecule expressed by **developing and mature B lymphocytes**, but **not on early B cell progenitors** and **not on later mature plasma cells**; used as a **B cell marker**.
- Associated with the **amplification of calcium signals transduced through the BCR** during antigen recognition.
- Expressed by the **majority of B cell lymphomas**, which is what makes it an immunotherapy target.
- Diagnostic use as he gave it: stain blood cells with an anti-CD20 antibody carrying a **chromogen or fluorochrome** to distinguish B cells from other cells.
- **[verify] and [check]:** in the transcript he says "it starts its expression in the pro-B cell and goes up to the plasma cell" while pointing at the B cell ontogeny figure, and the CD19/CD20 labels are interchanged several times in this passage. The slide is explicit that CD20 is **absent** on early progenitors and on plasma cells; go with the slide. Compare the staging in [[FMCII B cell ontogeny]].

**The antibody, rituximab** [S2 p4]:

| Feature | [S2 p4] | Lecturer |
|---|---|---|
| Target | CD20 | Same |
| Status | **First monoclonal antibody approved for therapeutic use** | "The first drug used in cancer in the form of monoclonal antibodies"; there were earlier therapeutic monoclonals, but in **cancer** rituximab was first **[check]** |
| Format | Genetically engineered: **human IgG1 heavy and human kappa light constant regions**, **anti-CD20 murine variable regions** | **Chimeric** |

| Indications | **B cell malignancies** such as **non-Hodgkin B cell lymphoma**, and some **autoimmune diseases** (e.g. **rheumatoid arthritis**), through its capacity to **deplete CD20+ cells** | Same, with the autoimmune rationale: B cells producing autoantibodies are causing the problem, so eliminating B cells helps |

**TBL question on rituximab.** Why is the IgG isotype important for efficacy against CD20-positive malignant cells? Options: **strong interaction with activating Fcγ receptors promotes ADCC and phagocytosis**; its inability to bind complement prevents inflammatory damage; its reduced interaction with Fcγ receptors prevents NK cell activation; its ability to block PD-1 signalling. **Correct: the first.** The antibody not only recognises CD20 but **recruits ADCC and phagocytosis**.

### 13b. EGFR: cetuximab vs panitumumab [S2 p5-6]
**The target, EGFR** [S2 p5]:
- **Surface tyrosine kinase receptor** controlling **cell growth, proliferation, survival and tissue repair**: homeostatic processes in many normal cells.
- Four ways it goes wrong: **activating mutations** (a constitutive signal even without ligand), **gene amplification**, **overexpression**, **excess ligand production**. Anything that increases signalling through the receptor leads to overactivation and transformation.
- Cancers characterised by altered EGFR-dependent pathways [S2 p5]: **lung, glioblastoma, colorectal, head and neck**. The lecturer's list: lung, bladder, stomach, prostate "and many others" **[check]**: the slide list is the one to learn.
- **Blockade of EGFR signalling provides a therapeutic opportunity.**

**The two antibodies** [S2 p6], his central comparison: same target, different isotype, different mechanism.

| | **Cetuximab** | **Panitumumab** |
|---|---|---|
| Isotype | **IgG1** | **IgG2** |
| Format | **Chimeric**: murine anti-EGFR variable regions joined to human IgG1 constant regions | **Human**: developed by **immunization of human Ig-expressing mice** (transgenic mice, section 7b) |
| Mechanism | **Binds EGFR, inhibits signalling and triggers ADCC** | **Binds and neutralizes EGFR-dependent activity** (neutralization only) |
| Why | IgG1 binds Fcγ receptors, so beyond blocking it recruits NK cells | **IgG2 is less able to bind the receptors on NK cells**, so there is no significant immunomodulatory mechanism |

**TBL question on the pair.** Which statement describes the difference resulting from the different IgG subclasses? Options: cetuximab blocks the receptor whereas panitumumab recognizes a different form; panitumumab activates complement more efficiently; **cetuximab has stronger Fc-mediated effector activity than panitumumab**; panitumumab activates NK cells more efficiently because IgG2 binds Fcγ receptors more strongly. **Correct: the third.**
- The reasoning he walked through: **what is common to both** is **binding and therefore blocking** the receptor-ligand interaction. **The difference** is that **IgG2 is not as efficient as IgG1 at binding the receptors on NK cells**, so cetuximab adds **recruitment of NK cells** to the shared blocking mechanism.

### 13c. PD-1 and PD-L1 [S2 p7-8]
**The axis** [S2 p7]:
- **PD-1 is an inhibitory receptor expressed on the surface of T cells.** It is **upregulated upon antigen recognition** and **over-expressed in T cells obtained from tumour tissue**.
- On binding its main ligand **PD-L1** (expressed by **tumour cells and myeloid cells**), PD-1 **downregulates T cell activation and functional properties**, leading to **exhausted, dysfunctional cells**.
- His version of the physiology: the TCR sends an activating signal; PD-1 does the opposite, controlling the activation so the response is not exacerbated. The problem in cancer is that the **tumour overexpresses PD-L1**, so a T cell recognising the tumour gets a **positive signal through the TCR and a simultaneous negative signal through PD-1**. This is why patients can have tumour-specific lymphocytes that infiltrate the tumour and still fail to reject it: "the tumour somehow is defending itself".
- **Therapeutic logic:** **blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction** neutralizes the negative signal and restores the response. [S2 p7] blockade gives beneficial responses in patients with **high PD-L1 levels**, hence a **diagnostic test for patient eligibility**.
- [Lucía] Same account: PD-1 is an internal control mechanism; continuous stimulation makes the cell exhausted and non-functional; tumours synthesize PD-L1 as a defence mechanism; it matters whether the inhibiting signal comes from the tumour itself, so PD-L1 expression is evaluated in tumour tissue for a personalized therapy.

**The three antibodies** [S2 p8]:

| | **Nivolumab** | **Atezolizumab** | **22C3** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target | **Human PD-1** | **Human PD-L1** | **Human PD-L1** |
| Isotype | **IgG4** | **IgG1** | (not given; irrelevant, see below) |
| Format | **Obtained by immunization of Ig-humanized mice with PD-1** | **Humanized**; obtained using a **human phage display library** against recombinant human PD-L1 | **Murine**; obtained by **immunization with PD-L1** |
| Use | Binds PD-1 with high affinity, **blocking the interaction with PD-L1**; this enhances **tumour antigen-specific T cell proliferation and cytokine secretion** | **Cancer immunotherapy** against a variety of tumours | **Reagent in immunohistochemistry-based tests** to determine **PD-L1 expression levels in tumour tissue**; part of a **companion diagnostics test** used in combination with PD-1 blocking antibodies |

- **[check] Nivolumab, humanized or fully human?** The slide says obtained by immunizing **Ig-humanized mice**; the lecturer said "it was obtained by immunizing humanization, so it is fully human". Transgenic-mouse-derived antibodies are the **fully human** route in section 7b, but the transcript sentence is garbled; the slide wording is the one to quote.
- **[check] Atezolizumab:** the lecturer never names it ("we have another antibody that instead of recognizing PD-1, recognizes the opposite"); the name and its details come from the slide. He described the same phage display library route.
- [Lucía] names nivolumab and 22C3 with the same roles; she additionally covers **HER-2 and trastuzumab**, which this year's session did not use.

**TBL question: why IgG4 for nivolumab?** Options: IgG4 maximizes complement-mediated destruction of PD-1-positive T cells; it promotes strong cell-mediated killing of activated lymphocytes; it increases the ability to neutralize PD-1; **it minimizes Fc-mediated elimination of PD-1-expressing T cells while blocking the inhibitory receptor**. **Correct: the last.**
- The reasoning: the aim is **only to block**. If ADCC were triggered, the antibody bound to PD-1 on the **T cell** would cause the **T cell** to be killed: "if you kill the T cell, your response is over". The T cells "are the good guys" here and must be preserved.
- **His rule, stated as such:** "if the focus of your antibody is only blocking an interaction without killing any of the partners, please choose an IgG4", because **IgG4 is the isotype that does not activate killing**.
- [Lucía] Same conclusion with her two reasons: IgG antibodies have a **longer half-life**, suitable for therapy; **IgG4 will not elicit complement activation**, so the lymphocyte is not destroyed while PD-1 is blocked.

**TBL question: nivolumab vs 22C3.** Options: they share the capacity to block the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction and inhibit negative signals; they share the capacity to eliminate PD-1-expressing cells; **they may have different applications, not sharing the mechanism of action**; they should both be humanized since they are needed for therapeutic purposes. **Correct: the third.**
- They recognise **molecules that interact with each other** but are **different antibodies with different jobs**: nivolumab targets **PD-1 on T cells** and blocks; 22C3 targets **PD-L1** and is **just a marker**, used in pathology laboratories, so **it does not have to block anything**.
- They do not share an elimination capacity, and they do not even share a target.
- **22C3 does not need humanization:** the only requirement for a diagnostic antibody is **specificity**; it can then be labelled with any kind of molecule.

**TBL question: isotype choice for diagnostic antibodies.** Stem: a pathology laboratory uses monoclonal antibodies against PD-1 and PD-L1 to check expression by immunohistochemistry; what explains the relevance of choosing the isotype? Options: always IgG4, because it has the highest affinity; diagnostic antibodies need to activate Fc receptors to eliminate positive cells during tissue staining; **in immunohistochemistry the purpose is to detect antigen expression through specific antibody binding, so effector functions such as ADCC or CDC are not required**; diagnostic antibodies do not need to bind PD-1 or PD-L1 specifically because the detection system provides the specificity. **Correct: the third.** "The isotype is not relevant. The only thing which is needed is specificity."

### 13d. Closing
- The point of the session, in his closing words: think about **the target and the specificity**, but also about **the Fc**, because the Fc determines what the antibody does. "And not always the purpose is the same": we may want to **eliminate**, to **block**, or just to **mark**.
