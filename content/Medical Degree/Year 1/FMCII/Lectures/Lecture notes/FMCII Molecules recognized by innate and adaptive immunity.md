## 1. Receptors in innate vs adaptive immunity
- To activate or control an immune response there must be **recognition**: interaction between immune cells and the environment, through receptors.
- [Slides]:

| | Receptor | Recognises | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Innate immunity | **Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)** | **PAMPs** and **DAMPs** | Generic |
| Adaptive immunity | **T and B cell receptors** | **Antigens** | Highly specific |

## 2. PAMPs and DAMPs: strangers and dangers
| | PAMPs ("strangers") | DAMPs ("dangers") |
|---|---|---|
| Full name | **Pathogen-associated molecular patterns** | **Danger- or damage-associated molecular patterns** ("depending on the books that you read, you may see danger or you may see damage") |
| Origin | From outside: typical constituents of microbes (viruses, bacteria, fungi) that are **never expressed by eukaryotic cells**, so they distinguish microbes from our own cells | From inside: molecules from a cell that is attacked by a virus or suffers physical or chemical **stress**, dies or is injured; "in many cases, as a consequence of strangers... this secondary signal" |
| Where detected | Released, displayed on the surface, or detected inside after phagocytosis | Released or displayed by stressed or dying cells |

- Cells that detect PAMPs: **macrophages** (activation of phagocytosis and other functions), **polymorphonuclear cells** (e.g. neutrophils), and **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)**. Activated APCs signal to adaptive immunity by displaying the pathogen's antigens.
- Outcome [Slides, both cases]: cytokines/chemokines, recruitment of immune cells (including lymphocytes), **inflammation**, triggering of **adaptive immunity**, **tissue repair**.
- **Student question:** why don't pathogens get rid of these molecules to escape? "In many cases, they need it... if they lose them, they cannot survive"; otherwise this would be an escape mechanism.
- **Student question on apoptosis** (garbled): not all apoptosis is the same; "four, five, six kinds," with different consequences. To be covered later **[verify]** the exact question.

## 3. PRR families and their ligands
- About **1,000** PRRs in total ("in the order of 1,000"). Main groups ([Slides] table, with the lecture):

| PRR family [Slides] | Ligands [Slides] | Lecture comments |
|---|---|---|
| **Toll-like receptors (TLRs)** | Proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, glycans; bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi | Example: a "polisaccharide" that is a constituent of a huge family of bacteria ("a negative bacteria") **[verify]** garbled; probably LPS (listed as a PAMP on the slides of the first lecture) and Gram-negative bacteria. One receptor recognises many bacteria of the same family |
| **NOD-like receptors (NLRs)** | Peptidoglycan, MDP; bacteria | Other bacterial constituents ("glycans, like peptides") |
| **C-type lectins** | Glycans; bacteria and fungi | Proteins that recognise sugars |
| **RIG-I-like receptors** | Viral RNA; viruses | Receptors for nucleic acids |

**How self and microbial nucleic acids are distinguished** (lecture):
- DNA: **CpG sequences** (cytosine-guanine repeats) are typical of bacteria and some viruses and less common in our DNA; whether they are **methylated** distinguishes eukaryotic from bacterial or viral DNA.
- RNA: we have RNA but not **double-stranded RNA**. dsRNA appears during replication of single-stranded RNA viruses (copying a negative strand and then the positive strand), so it is a feature of viral replication.

**Location of PRRs** ([Slides] figure; see the slide):
- **Membrane**: for released PAMPs or DAMPs.
- **Endosomes**: e.g. a phagocytosed bacterium detected inside the cell.
- **Cytoplasm**: e.g. a virus that has lost its capsid or envelope in the cytoplasm, leaving free nucleic acid (such as dsRNA). It can only be detected there, "because outside the cell, the RNA is always encapsulated."

## 4. DAMPs: constitutive and inducible
- The idea: something uncommon appears, "OK, something is wrong."
- [Slides] three types of endogenous DAMPs:

| Type [Slides] | How it becomes a signal | Lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutive**, passively released from **necrotic** cells | A molecule normally inside the cell is an alarm only when released by cell death | Example **ATP**: works inside the cell (energy, phosphorylation reactions), "not much" use outside, so a high concentration outside means a cell is dying. Immune cells have **ATP receptors** |
| **Constitutive**, exposed on the surface of **stressed** cells | Without cell death, normally internal molecules are exposed on the surface | "There are some lipids" that appear at the surface **[verify]** garbled |
| **Inducible**, actively secreted by activated cells | Never present before; synthesised and secreted as a secondary signal after a receptor is triggered | Proteins or small molecules |

- [Slides] DAMP list released by dead, dying or injured cells and the receptors on macrophages, neutrophils and endothelial cells (NLRs, TLRs, AGER, CD147, P2X7, LRG, ST2, CD91, ApoB, GP2B). DAMPs listed include cyclophilin A, uric acid, S100A, HSPs, ferritin, ATP, mtDNA, cytochrome c, HMGB1, histones, IL33, profilin-1, LL-37, calreticulin, enolase-1, matrilin-2, fibrinogen, hyaluronan, fibronectin. ## 5. Antigen, immunogen, hapten, epitope, paratope
| Term | Definition [Slides] | Lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Antigen** | Any molecule specifically recognised by an **antibody** or a **TCR** | Antigenicity = "the capacity of being recognized... nothing else," whether as foreign or as self |
| **Immunogen** | The type of antigen that **evokes an adaptive immune response** | Recognition plus activation |
| **Hapten** | **Antigenic but non-immunogenic** (often low molecular weight); can be made immunogenic by conjugation to a suitable **carrier** | Recognised but "never triggers any response"; conjugating it chemically to an immunogenic molecule transfers properties to it |
| **Epitope** | The portion of an antigen recognised and bound by an antibody or TCR (**antigenic determinant**) | Receptors do not recognise a whole protein (e.g. a viral envelope protein of ~1,000 amino acids), only small regions |
| **Paratope** | The site of an antibody or TCR that binds to an epitope | Only the contact surface of the receptor |

- Antigens include immunogens and haptens; they differ in their capacity to activate the immune system.
- A large protein with many epitopes is **polyepitopic (multiepitopic)**; a tiny antigen with space for one epitope is **monoepitopic**.
- [Slides] figure: myoglobin with numbered epitope regions; see the slide.

## 6. The carrier effect
[Slides] experiment (immunising a laboratory animal, e.g. rabbit or mouse, and testing for antibodies a couple of weeks later):

| Immunisation | Anti-DNP antibodies | Anti-protein antibodies |
|---|---|---|
| DNP (dinitrophenyl) hapten alone | No | No |
| Large protein alone | No | Yes |
| Protein-conjugated DNP | **Yes** | Yes |

- The transcript names the hapten "TNT"/"DNA"; the slide gives **DNP**.
- Lecture explanation: lymphocytes that recognise the large protein (not the hapten) are now involved, and they **help** the lymphocytes needed to produce antibodies against the hapten. Details later.

**Application: conjugate vaccines** ([Slides], meningococcus A):
- The most antigenic part of the bacterium is its outer **polysaccharide**; antibodies against it would bind the bacterium and prevent infection.
- **Pure polysaccharide vaccine**: recognised by B cells, but few antibodies; a weak response with short memory. [Slides]: "weak and short-lived response."
- **Polysaccharide-protein conjugate vaccine**: the sugar is linked to a large, very immunogenic protein, the **tetanus toxoid** ("almost 900, 1,000 amino acids"). Now T cells specific for the tetanus toxin help the B cells. [Slides]: the B cell internalises, processes and presents carrier peptide on MHC II to a CD4+ T cell, which gives T cell help; "strong and long-lasting response."
- "This is on the basis of many vaccines": antigens that are strategically interesting (the microbe's envelope) but behave as haptens or induce very poor responses are conjugated to **carrier proteins**.

## 7. What determines immunogenicity
[Slides]:
1. Properties of the **immunogen**.
2. Properties of the **host**: (a) genetic: antigen presentation (MHC), genes encoding antigen receptors, genes coding for immune regulation; (b) physiological condition of the host.
3. **Mode of antigen exposure**.

## 8. Contribution of the immunogen
**Nature of the molecule** [Slides]:
- **Humoral (B cell) responses**: proteins > polysaccharides >>>> lipids > nucleic acids.
- **Cellular (T cell) responses**: proteins and some lipids.
- "The most immunogenic molecules are proteins," for both B and T cell responses.

**Features of the immunogen:**

| Feature | Lecture and [Slides] |
|---|---|
| **Foreignness** | Bovine serum albumin (BSA) injected into a cow: no antibodies, because it is self. Into a rabbit: response, because cow albumin differs from rabbit albumin |
| **Molecular size** | Larger molecules have more chance of containing regions that differ from self (cow and rabbit albumin probably share many regions). [Slides]: the best immunogens have a molecular mass **approaching 100,000 Da**; table of experimental antigens (e.g. BSA 69,000 Da; tetanus toxoid 150,000 Da; KLH >2,000,000 Da). This is why large proteins such as tetanus toxoid are used as carriers |
| **Ability to be processed and presented by MHC** | T cells do not recognise the epitope on the whole protein. The protein is processed (cleaved into peptides), and peptides bind **MHC** molecules (**HLA** in humans) on **APCs** (dendritic cells and macrophages, from a student question). If no peptide is generated that can be presented, a large protein behaves as a hapten for that individual, "because despite having a T cell receptor, the T cell receptor will never see the peptide." [Slides]: what T cells see is Ag/MHC |

## 9. Contribution of the host and of the mode of exposure
**Host genotype:**
- **MHC**: each person has a different set of MHC molecules, so by chance someone may lack an MHC molecule able to present a given peptide. "You are genetically biased" to respond or not; this depends on the individual, not the immunogen.
- **Antigen receptors**: receptors are generated randomly, so individuals have different receptor sets ([Slides] figure, individuals A and B; see the slide).
- **Genes for immune regulation**.

**Physiological condition** [Slides: immune competence: genetic deficiencies, disease, therapies]: e.g. a cancer patient receiving **chemotherapy**, which kills tumour cells but also immune cells. Even with a good repertoire, the capacity to respond is reduced.

**Mode of exposure** (skipped by the lecturer; [Slides] only):
- Dosage: too little does not induce a response; too much induces **tolerance** (unresponsive state).
- Routes: oral, intradermal, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intraperitoneal, intravenous.

**Adjuvants** (lecture and [Slides]):
- Antigens are not usually given alone but with molecules that improve immunisation.
- [Slides] two types: **immune stimulants** (increase co-stimulatory signals, lymphocyte proliferation) and **vehicle depot** (prolong antigen persistence, targeting to APC).
- Lecture analogy for the **depot effect**: a thick, consistent sauce ("margarine sauce" in the transcript **[verify]** the word) dropped in water stays together longer than a liquid drop. An antigen in an adjuvant **emulsion** is released slowly, like repeated administration without new injections.

## 10. Epitopes: B cell vs T cell
| | B cell epitopes | T cell epitopes |
|---|---|---|
| How recognised | Antibodies recognise the antigen **as it is, in its native conformation** [Slides] | Only after **processing** into peptides presented on **MHC** by APCs [Slides] |
| Location on the protein | **Exposed on the surface**, accessible to antibodies (not the inner regions of the folded protein) | Any peptide that can bind MHC |
| Chemical properties | [Slides] generally **hydrophilic** amino acids (exposed on the protein surface); hydrophobic ones are inside. The transcript says "hydrophobic" for the exposed ones ("halogymic" later) **[check]**: probably a transcription error; the slide says hydrophilic | Depends on the sequence of the MHC groove: different peptides bind different MHC molecules |
| Size | No larger than the paratope / antibody-binding site [Slides]; "usually a few amino acids, between 4 and 6... In some cases it could be 8... Do not think about 25 or more" | Peptides |
| Linear vs conformational | Both: **linear** (a stretch of consecutive amino acids, e.g. 5 in a row) and **conformational** (amino acids distant in the sequence, e.g. residue 132 and 345, brought together by folding) | **Always linear** ("there are no conformational epitopes for T cells") |
| Prediction | Size, hydrophilicity and linear/conformational criteria predict B cell epitopes from the sequence | **Prediction algorithms** based on the sequences of MHC molecules and which peptides bind their groove |

## 11. Practical applications
[Slides] with lecture examples:
- **Vaccine design and optimisation**: select relevant antigens or epitopes; conjugate those that are antigenic but not immunogenic (meningococcus example). Example: **hepatitis B vaccine** uses a single viral **surface protein** expressed in the laboratory, not the whole virus, and induces protective antibodies. Many other viral vaccines use whole virus, inactivated or attenuated.
- **Monitoring adaptive responses**: knowing the antigen lets you measure whether a person was vaccinated properly or needs re-vaccination, the immune status of patients, and whether immunotherapy is working.
- **Generating antibodies** against desired epitopes: therapeutic antibodies (immunotherapy); antibodies for research; antibodies used by **pathologists** on tissue sections.
- **Drug design**: drugs should be **non-immunogenic**. If a drug induces antibodies a few days after administration, they **neutralise** it and it no longer acts on its receptor. A drug that is recognised but never induces a response is tolerated.

## 12. Summary
[Slides]:
- Innate immune cells, through **PRRs**, recognise **PAMPs and DAMPs**.
- Antigenicity and immunogenicity determine a molecule's capacity to be **recognised** and to **elicit** an immune response.
- Immunogenicity depends on the **immunogen**, the **host** and the **mode of exposure**.
- Understanding antigenicity and immunogenicity has practical applications in therapy and technology.
