## 1. Why immunology matters in medicine
- [Slides] Cross-disciplinary field involved in virtually every medical specialty, organised in levels:

| Level | Examples [Slides] |
|---|---|
| Basic knowledge | Immune cells, antibodies, cytokines, complement, HLA |
| Immunological mechanisms | Inflammation, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, tolerance |
| Diseases and clinical conditions | Lupus, asthma, sepsis, type 1 diabetes, transplant rejection, cancer |
| Diagnosis and treatment | Autoantibodies, immunoglobulins, vaccines, immunosuppressants, immunotherapy |

- The lecturer compares it to biochemistry (metabolic diseases, signalling pathways) and genetics (mutations): immunology is "as important or almost as important" as those basic subjects.
- Four reasons [Slides, and in the lecture]: it explains the **mechanisms** of many diseases (inflammation "will appear in many completely unrelated diseases"); it lets you interpret **diagnostic tests** across specialties (autoantibodies, immunoglobulins); it is essential for **treatments** (when and which vaccine, who should receive immunosuppressants, immunotherapy "now the standard of care for many diseases"); it helps you understand **the patient as a whole**, because the immune response is dynamic and takes place all around the body.

## 2. What the immune system is
- [Slides] "**Integrated** group of molecules and cells that are taking part in the induction and maintenance of a **coordinated** response against a potential threat to the body."
- "Nothing goes on its own." If any part of the coordinated response does not work properly, disease follows, either by **excess** or by **lack** of defence.
- The early lectures look like independent puzzle pieces (one cell type or molecule per day); they will be integrated later into effector and control mechanisms working together.

## 3. Innate vs adaptive immunity: the two branches
- **Innate**: "something that we already have"; always present and ready.
- **Adaptive**: developed over time; "maybe in its origins is there, but not fully developed."
- All organisms have innate immunity. Adaptive immunity appeared later in evolution; "small animals and plants... only have innate immunity." [Slides]: adaptive immunity is present only in **jawed vertebrates** (the transcript is garbled here).
- What distinguishes adaptive immunity: **lymphocytes** (B and T) and the molecules they produce.

[Slides] overview:

| | Innate | Adaptive (jawed vertebrates) |
|---|---|---|
| First layer | External barriers | |
| Cellular | Macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, innate lymphoid cells / NK | B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes |
| Humoral ("humors: body fluids") | Complement, cytokines, acute phase proteins, antimicrobial peptides | Antibodies, cytokines |

- **Terminology warning from the lecturer:** "humoral" literally means anything in a body fluid, but **humoral immunity** usually refers to **antibody (B cell) responses**; **cellular immunity**, although all immunity is mediated by cells, usually means immunity mediated by **T cells**. He will say which meaning he uses.

## 4. Innate immunity: external barriers
| Type [Slides] | Examples (lecture and [Slides] figure) |
|---|---|
| Physical | Intact **skin** (protects unless breached by a wound or injury); **mucus and intact mucous membranes** of the respiratory and digestive tracts |
| Mechanical | **Cilia** of the upper respiratory tract (trap foreign material); **coughing and sneezing** expel microorganisms |
| Chemical | **Acidic pH** of the stomach (destroys pathogens that come with food); acid in sweat |
| Biological | **Enzymes** in saliva, mucus and tears that digest microorganisms; antibacterial proteins in semen ([Slides]: "anti-bacterial proteins and zinc in semen") |

- [Slides] only: competition from commensal bacteria in the gut and genital tract.

## 5. Innate immunity: cells
The lecturer's logic: pathogens either live **outside** cells or **inside** cells.

| Group [Slides] | Cells | Role in the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Phagocytic cells** | Monocytes (circulation) become **macrophages** in tissues; **neutrophils**; [Slides] also dendritic cells | Capture and engulf ("just eat it") pathogens that live outside cells: small bacteria, viruses, some fungi. Lower the pathogenic load in blood or tissue |
| **Cytotoxic cells** | **Eosinophils**; **NK cells** | Kill the cell itself when the pathogen lives inside it ("the cell somehow is behaving as a factory"). Eosinophils release their granules to kill an infected cell, "or even... a tumor cell" (a tumour cell is itself the pathogenic entity) |
| **Other granulocytes** | **Mast cells**, **basophils** | Release granules; help against other kinds of pathogens; involved in **allergies** ("a deviation of the normal behaviour") |

- [Slides] figure: haematopoietic tree (stem cell to myeloid and lymphoid progenitors, thymus for T cells, B cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, platelets, erythrocytes); see the slide.
- **NK cells** are lymphocytes but belong to innate immunity. They are part of a small group, the **innate lymphoid cells**. The name "natural killer" comes from their ability to kill a cell **without previous recognition**. All other lymphocytes belong to adaptive immunity.

## 6. Innate immunity: humoral components
[Slides] list, with the lecture's comments:
- **Cytokines**: IL-1, IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α, interferons. Released by the cells.
- **Complement**: not produced by immune cells; produced in the **liver**, but acts together with effector molecules of the immune system.
- **Acute phase proteins**: C-reactive protein, fibrinogen. Produced when an immune response is going on, often associated with **inflammation**; "in many tests, you analyze the levels of these proteins because they tell you whether an inflammation is involved."
- **Antimicrobial peptides and proteins**; **antimicrobial enzymes** (some already shown with the barriers).
- These molecules help the cells: some mark the pathogen so it is phagocytosed, others trigger cytotoxic reactions.
- Separate lectures will cover cytokines, complement and innate immunity.

## 7. Innate immunity: pathogen recognition
- [Slides] Recognises **molecular patterns** through **germline-encoded receptors**, limited in number (**~10³ receptors**). "This may look to you as a lot of receptors... this is nothing" compared with adaptive immunity.
- These are the **pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)**. They recognise general features shared by many microorganisms, so a few receptors cover a whole variety of pathogens.
  - **PAMPs** (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) [Slides]: LPS, CpG, oligosaccharides, viral and bacterial DNA.
  - **DAMPs** (damage-associated molecular patterns): molecules associated with damage or stress, "something extraordinary... taking place." [Slides]: HSP, HMGB1, ATP.
- [Slides] "**PRRs do not recognize specific pathogens.**"
- Detail in the next lecture: [[FMCII Molecules recognized by innate and adaptive immunity]].

## 8. Innate immunity: functions
1. **Clearance** of the pathogenic agent: together with the barriers, the first cellular mechanism that lowers the pathogen load.
2. **Priming of adaptive immunity**: innate immunity "is going to be responsible for waking up" adaptive immunity ("hey, something is taking place here"). [Slides]: it "drives the type of adaptive immunity."
3. **Tissue repair and remodelling**: killing infected cells also damages healthy cells ("some kind of auto-injury"); once the pathogen is gone, innate immunity repairs the tissue. "We do not have to forget about this because they are as important as the two other functions."

**Macrophages** as the best example of the three functions:
- Abundant; most derive from **monocytes** that enter the tissues. Some are located in tissues from the **embryonic stage** and proliferate there.
- Old simplified classification ([Slides] "Discrete polarization model"):

| Subset | Function | [Slides] induced by |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | Pathogen killing (phagocytose and destroy bacteria in endosomes) | M-CSF, IFN-γ |
| M2 | Tissue remodelling after pathogen clearance | M-CSF, IL-4 |
| In between: antigen-presenting macrophages ([Slides]: monocyte-derived DCs) | Antigen presentation, priming of lymphocytes | GM-CSF |

- Now, with sequencing, imaging, proteomics and transcriptomics, it is clear there are **many macrophage populations in a spectrum** with mixed properties ([Slides] "Modular spectrum model": pathogen killing, pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine release, migration to lymph nodes, phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, antigen presentation, tissue remodelling, tissue homeostasis). We no longer speak so much of prototypical M1/M2, but of "not only the name, but the surname."

## 9. Timing and cooperation of innate and adaptive responses
- Innate response is **generic and fast** (minutes or hours): **first line of defence**. It controls some infections on its own and keeps the pathogen under control until adaptive immunity is mounted. Adaptive takes "a week or so"; [Slides] **4-7 days**.
- [Slides] scheme: (1) first line of defence, (2) innate immunity drives the type of adaptive immunity, then **cooperation in the effector phase** (examples promised for later: T lymphocytes with macrophages, B cells with neutrophils), (3) tissue repair and remodelling at the end.

## 10. Adaptive immunity: lymphocytes
| | T lymphocytes | B lymphocytes |
|---|---|---|
| Maturation [Slides] | **Thymus** | **Bone marrow** and secondary lymphoid organs |
| Origin of the name | Identified as typical cells of the **thymus** | **Bursa of Fabricius**, the organ where B cells are produced in chickens (birds); "It is not because of bone marrow" |
| Subsets [Slides] | **Effector CD4 and CD8**; **regulatory CD4** (does the opposite of effector cells: controls the response, "kind of an internal brain") | |
| Antigen receptor | **T cell receptor (TCR)**: "actually... two molecules" | **B cell receptor = antibody**; it is also **secreted** and has effector function |

- [Slides] electron micrographs: small lymphocyte (T or B) 6 µm; blast cell (T or B) 15 µm; plasma cell (B) 15 µm. See the slide.
- TCRs and BCRs are highly specific: "this is virus A, or this is bacteria B."

## 11. Lymphoid organs
[Slides] Anatomical structures where lymphocytes develop, mature, encounter antigens and initiate immune responses.

| | Primary (central) | Secondary (peripheral) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Lymphocytes **develop and mature** from precursors ("not a lymphocyte... a precursor of an immature cell") into real T or B cells. [Slides]: no need to encounter their specific foreign antigen | Mature lymphocytes migrate here and "wait for the antigen to come"; they encounter cognate antigen, **activate** and **initiate** the response |
| Organs | **Thymus** (T cells), **bone marrow** (B cells) | **Lymph nodes**, **spleen**, **mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue** (e.g. digestive tract), **tonsils** |

- Lymphocytes are not always where the infection is: most of the repertoire is in lymphoid organs, and "the signals of an infection come there." Secondary organs are distributed so they drain the different organs and tissues.

## 12. Antigen specificity and clonal selection
- The main difference from innate cells: **exquisite specificity**. Each lymphocyte clone has a **different receptor** recognising a different antigen.
- Repertoire: the lecturer says "thousands of millions, I mean billions of clones"; [Slides] **10¹¹ clones (a different receptor per clone)**.
- One microorganism has many regions or proteins; for each one there are many lymphocytes, each specific for one feature.
- **Clonal selection theory** [Slides]:
  - each B or T cell has an individual specificity for a single antigen (or a single region of an antigen);
  - when it meets its specific antigen it is selected and **activated**;
  - activation leads to **proliferation**, expanding the clonal progeny;
  - all daughter cells are **identical** and share the original receptor, so each clone reacts against the antigen that stimulated the original lymphocyte. The other clones stay without proliferation.
- **Student question**: is it unlikely for a given cell to meet its specific pathogen? Yes: "this is why adaptive immunity takes... four days, a week or so," whereas innate immunity acts immediately through its general receptors. There is continuous sampling until a positive interaction activates the clone.
- [Slides] figure (Kuby, fig 1-7): generation of diversity, **deletion**, clonal selection and expansion:
  - Stem cell (not yet a lymphocyte) gives rise in primary lymphoid organs to lymphocytes with different receptors.
  - **Deletion**: lymphocytes with useless receptors (not properly developed, or that do not recognise anything during development and so receive **no survival signals**) are eliminated. Only "let's say 5%, 10%" remain. A selection-process lecture will follow.
  - Student question: are self-reactive cells also removed? "Yes, some of them... we want to get rid of those to avoid autoimmunity."
  - Surviving cells go to secondary organs; the one that meets its antigen (cell 2 in the figure) is activated and proliferates; the other (cell 3) keeps waiting. Activated cells spread through the body, "hopefully" to where the pathogen is.

## 13. Phases of the adaptive response
[Slides]:
1. **Recognition**: specificity.
2. **Activation**: expansion and differentiation. A cell that has never seen the antigen "has the whole machinery" but has to be activated to become a real effector.
3. **Effector**: expanded effector cells collaborate with the innate immune system to get rid of the pathogen.

## 14. Memory, vaccines and trained innate immunity
- [Slides] "**Memory: a classical hallmark of adaptive immunity.**" The primary response follows the first exposure; **memory lymphocytes** remain after the antigen is cleared. A second exposure to the same antigen restimulates them: faster, stronger, better response.
- Lecture: primary response peaks in "one or two weeks"; the second time "in a couple of days or in three days." [Slides] graph (Kuby fig 1-8): primary adaptive response peaks at about day 14 (see the slide); the secondary response is much larger.
- Student question (why the second response is better): after clearance the population contracts but a few cells remain as memory. The best clones "are already there... because you have already selected them," so there is no need to pick them up "from here and from there."
- **Vaccines** use this property: you vaccinate usually healthy people to promote the first encounter without disease, "usually an inactive pathogen or something like that"; when the real pathogen comes, the response is stronger and protects. Memory is **specific**: flu vaccine protects against flu, not hepatitis.
- **Trained innate immunity** [Slides]: some innate responses increase upon repeated exposure; functional, metabolic and epigenetic changes give "trained" cells new properties. Lecture: innate cells (macrophages, NK cells) stay in a higher background activation and respond better, but **without specific memory**. Example: vaccination against some pathogens may improve responses against other, unrelated pathogens (not pathogen-specific).

## 15. Tolerance, summary and disease
- **Tolerance**: killing infected cells causes damage, so there must be a **balance** between limiting the pathogen and avoiding damage. Lymphocytes that recognise our own cells must be avoided. Two failures: responses against our own constituents (destruction of self), or **full tolerance**, in which nothing is destroyed. [Slides]: many random rearrangements that create B and T cell receptors could be anti-self; tolerance keeps them from circulating.

[Slides] summary table, which the lecturer read through:

| Property | Adaptive | Innate |
|---|---|---|
| Recognition | **Specific** | **Generic** (common patterns) |
| Repertoire | Large (10¹¹ receptors) | Limited ("thousands") |
| Memory | **Specific memory** | Trained immunity: non-specific |
| Self-tolerance | Discriminates self from foreign | Not observed |
| Development | Develops during lifetime, slower, only in jawed vertebrates | Already present, faster, in all organisms |

- [Slides] only ("Immune response and disease", not explained in the transcript):
  - Dysfunctions: overly active or misdirected responses (allergies, autoimmunity); immunodeficiency, primary (genetic) or secondary (acquired).
  - Transplanted tissues: the natural response is to attack foreign tissue; here we want to **avoid** a response (rejection).
  - Cancer: the dangerous cells are our own cells, generally tolerated and hard to generate immunity against.
