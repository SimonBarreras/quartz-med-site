## 1. Objectives [Slides p2]
- Imaging techniques used daily (plain radiographs, CT, MRI): advantages, disadvantages, indications.
- Normal musculoskeletal anatomy on each modality (bones, joints, muscles, tendons, ligaments).
- How to describe images: radiographic densities, echogenicities, signal intensities.
- Spatial orientation: projections and imaging planes.

## 2. Plain radiographs
- **Concept:** X-ray generator, the patient, the X-ray beam and a receptor. The image depends on **how much each tissue attenuates the X-rays**: bone attenuates more and appears **white**; X-rays pass through **air**, which appears **black**. [Slides p4] Ionizing radiation; uses: initial assessment of **osteoarticular** pathology (fractures, arthritis, bone tumours), **pulmonary** pathology, **obstructive pathology / intestinal perforation**. "1st learn the normal anatomy".
- **First-line technique:** start with the X-ray; if it solves the problem, stop; if not, go to another technique.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Fast (an image in seconds) | **Overlap of structures** (3D structure in a 2D image): the most important |
| Cheap, accessible, widely available | **Ionizing radiation**: justify it, do not abuse it |
| Portable (bedside) | Limited range of radiographic densities |

- **Overlap examples (clinical):**
  - Finger with a linear hyperdense foreign body: on the AP view you cannot tell if it is dorsal, palmar or inside the phalanx; the **lateral** view (finger turned 90°) shows it is **palmar**. Needed before removing it in the emergency room. [Slides p6]
  - Knee AP: the **patella** overlaps the **femur**; see it better on a lateral view. [Slides p7]
  - Cervical spine AP: **C1 and C2 overlap the facial bones**; special **open-mouth** projection shows C1, C2 and the **odontoid process** (dens). [Slides p7]

### The five X-ray densities [Slides p8-9]

| Density | Appearance |
|---|---|
| **Air** | Most hypodense: **black** (attenuates the least) |
| **Fat** | Darker grey than soft tissue |
| **Soft tissue / fluid** (muscle, water) | Grey, fluid and soft tissue look similar on radiographs |
| **Calcium** (bone) | Most hyperdense **natural** material: white |
| **Metal** (implants, pacemaker, arthroplasty) | **Very hyperdense**: absorbs all X-rays |

- Fat and soft tissue look very similar on X-rays; they are better differentiated on CT.
- Example: patient with scoliosis treated with surgery (metal), all five densities on one film. [Slides p9]

### Projections [Slides p10-13]
- **AP:** beam enters the **anterior** aspect, exits **posterior**. **PA:** enters **posterior**, exits **anterior**. First word = where the beam enters; second = where it exits.
- The difference matters for some regions (transcript "cell 15" **[verify]**, probably the chest); in MSK both are called **AP**.
- **Orientation (AP or PA):** as if looking at the patient **from the front** (face to face): **left of the image = patient's right; right of the image = patient's left**.
- **Left or right knee?** The **fibula is lateral**. Seen from the front, the side of the image where the fibula lies is lateral. Example on the slide: **a right knee**. [Slides p12]
- **Lateral view:** 90° to the AP. When it is not possible, **oblique** views as complements. [Slides p13]

## 3. Ultrasound
- Few ultrasound images in anatomy. Different technique: **no X-rays**. A **transducer** emits ultrasound and receives the echoes; the image depends on **how deep** the structure is and **how much the ultrasound is attenuated** through the tissues. [Slides p15]
- **Uses:** superficial soft tissues (skin, muscle, tendons, fat lesions); solid organs (liver, spleen, kidneys, bladder, thyroid); joints; **guidance** of percutaneous procedures (drainage, injections, biopsies): the needle is seen in real time. [Slides p15]

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **No ionizing radiation** (children, pregnant patients) | **Operator-dependent** (the image depends on who performs it; reason for few US images in class) |
| Accessible, fast, cheap, mobile (bedside) | **"Enemies": air and bone** (no US of the brain or of the lungs) |
| **Real time**; procedure guidance | **Deep structures**: the deeper, the worse the image |

- **Echogenicity** (always compared with the background): **anechoic** (completely black), **hypoechoic**, **isoechoic**, **hyperechoic** (bright). [Slides p17]
- **Acoustic enhancement:** below a **fluid** cavity (ultrasound crosses fluid without attenuation, so the area behind is brighter). Indicates fluid. **Acoustic shadowing:** behind **calcium** and **air** (all ultrasound reflected at the surface; nothing seen behind). Examples: subcutaneous fluid cavity; fluid with a calcification; calcifications in a tendon. [Slides p17-18]

| | Fluid | Soft tissue | Bone | Air |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ultrasound | Anechoic with acoustic enhancement | Hyper/iso/hypoechoic | Hyperechoic border with acoustic shadowing | Anechoic with reverberation at the interface |
| Anatomy | Gallbladder, bladder | Liver, kidneys, spleen, thyroid | Bones | Lungs, bowel |
| Pathology | Cyst | Tumours, benign solid lesions | Granuloma, calcified lesions | Pneumoperitoneum |

[Slides p19]
- **Anisotropy** (tendons, nerves): if the transducer is not **perpendicular**, a normally hyperechoic tendon looks dark; move the probe to be perpendicular. [Slides p20]
- **Doppler:** assesses vascular flow. [Slides p21-22] (not developed in the transcript)

## 4. Computed tomography (CT)
- Also uses **X-rays**, but the tube and detectors **rotate** around the patient (in the **gantry**) while the table moves: **consecutive slices** instead of one projection. [Slides p24]
- **Post-processing from one single acquisition:** windows/filters (e.g. **lung window** for the lung parenchyma, **bone window**) and **multiplanar and 3D** reconstructions. Not possible in MRI, where each plane and sequence is a separate acquisition. [Slides p25]

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Wider grey scale: more anatomical detail than radiographs | **Ionizing radiation** (more than X-ray) |
| **No overlap** (successive slices) | More expensive than radiographs and ultrasound |
| Multiplanar and 3D reconstructions from one acquisition | |
| **Fast** (whole body in seconds; e.g. **polytrauma**), accessible | |

[Slides p26]
- **Hounsfield units (HU):** each pixel has a value from **-1000 to +1000**, depending on X-ray attenuation at that point; it sets the grey level. [Slides p27]

| Material | HU [Slides p28] |
|---|---|
| Air | -1000 |
| Fat | -40 to -120 |
| Water | 0 |
| Soft tissue | +20 to +100 |
| Bone | +400 to +600 |
| Metal | > +1000 |

- **Positive** HU = more X-ray absorption = **brighter**; **negative** = less absorption = **darker**. **[check]** The slide p28 labels "Negative units: darker, denser" and "Positive units: brighter, less dense"; the lecturer said the slide is the wrong way round ("here is the opposite, sorry, I will change it").
- **Windowing:** the eye cannot distinguish all those grey values, so the grey scale is centred on what you want to study, after the exam: **lung window** (negative values, air: lung parenchyma, bronchi, vessels), **soft-tissue window** (around 0: water, fat, soft tissue), **bone window** (high values: cortical and trabecular bone, metal). [Slides p29]

| Plane | Viewed as if | Top of image | Bottom | Image left | Image right |

| **Sagittal** | From the **side** (≈ lateral radiograph) | Cranial | Caudal | Anterior (usually) | Posterior (usually) |

## 5. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
- Physics, very summarized: protons spin in **random** directions; in the **magnetic field** they **align**; a **radiofrequency pulse** changes their direction; when it stops, they **return** to the initial position **emitting energy**, which varies with the time they take; a computer converts it into the image. [Slides p35]

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **High spatial resolution, especially for soft tissue** (muscles, tendons, ligaments) | **Slow** (a knee MRI about 20 minutes, complex studies up to an hour) |
| No overlap (successive slices) | **The most expensive** technique |
| **No ionizing radiation** (radiofrequency and a magnet): useful in **children and pregnant women** | **Claustrophobic** patients |

[Slides p36]
- Planes as in CT, but each plane is acquired separately (more time). [Slides p37-38]

### Signal intensities [Slides p39-44]
- Sequences: **T1**, **T2** and **T2 with fat suppression (T2-FS)**. T1 is more "anatomical"; T2, especially fat-suppressed, shows **pathology** better.

| Tissue | T1 | T2 | T2-FS | Anatomy | Pathology |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cortical bone** | Hypo | Hypo | Hypo | Cortical bone | Calcifications |
| **Fluid** | **Hypo** | **Hyper** | **Hyper** | Urine, CSF, joint fluid | Cysts, joint effusion |
| **Fat** | Hyper | Hyper | **Hypo** | Subcutaneous tissue, intraperitoneal fat, bone marrow | Lipoma |
| **Solid organs** | Variable (water content) | Variable | Variable | Liver, spleen, kidneys | Tumours |

- **CT vs MRI by the cortical bone:** cortical **always hyperdense on CT**, **always hypointense on MRI**. The **medullary cavity** (fatty marrow) is always **hypodense on CT** and **variable on MRI**, depending on the sequence. [Slides p41]
- **Water tells T1 from T2:** dark on T1, bright on T2 (CSF example). [Slides p42]
- **Fat does not tell T1 from T2**: bright on both; it can be suppressed in any sequence. [Slides p43] The lecturer also mentions fat suppression on T1 when contrast is used (garbled **[verify]**).
- **Ligaments, menisci, fibrocartilage and tendons** (little water): **hypointense on all sequences**. Cartilage and muscle (more water): intermediate on T2. [Slides p40, p44]
