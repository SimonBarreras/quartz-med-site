## 1. Opening exercise: raising the arm to the ear
- Joints moved when raising the arm sideways until it touches the ear: **four**: **glenohumeral** (shoulder), **acromioclavicular**, **sternoclavicular**, and the **scapula with the chest wall** (scapulothoracic). The most frequent class answer was one.
- [Notes] Scapulothoracic joint: elevation-depression, extension-retraction, lateral-medial rotation (p. 9).

## 2. Joints: definitions and types
- **Arthrology:** the study of joints, the structures that allow movement between bones. A **joint** = the union of two or more bones. Joints allow muscles to generate movement through the bones.
- Two main types: **solid** and **synovial**.
- **"Solid" does not mean without movement** (solid joints do not necessarily move); synovial joints are joints with movement, like the hinges of a door.
- Three types of solid joint, by the tissue between the bones: **bone**, **fibrous**, **cartilaginous**.
- [Notes] Solid (synarthrosis): no cavity, components held by connective tissue. Synovial (diarthrosis): elements separated by a cavity.

## 4. Synovial joints: components
- The most common joints; they allow movement. **Defining feature: a cavity with fluid inside.** Every joint with a cavity, fluid and movement is synovial (transcript "also known as VR process" **[verify]**; [Notes] diarthrosis).
- **Seven components:**

| Component | Lecturer's points |
|---|---|
| **Fibrous capsule** | Connective tissue **continuous with the periosteum**; the **envelope** of the joint, keeps the fluid inside; a **stabilizer**, prevents abnormal movement (ankle: tibia to talus; elbow) |
| **Synovial membrane** | Inner part of the capsule; **highly vascularized**, produces **synovial fluid** |
| **Ligaments** | **Stabilizers**; join **bone to bone** |
| **Articular cartilage** | Hyaline; **avascular** |
| **Articular disc or meniscus** | Fibrocartilage (see below) |
| **Bursae** | Soft tissue covering bony prominences so skin or tendon move without irritation |
| **Fat pads** | **Shock absorbers**; important one in the knee behind the patellar tendon |

- [Notes] Components listed: articular cartilage (hyaline), synovial membrane, fibrous capsule, ligaments, synovial bursa, fat pads, **tendons**, articular discs (menisci: pubis, knee, acromioclavicular, sternoclavicular). The notes list 8 items including tendons; the lecturer gives seven.
- **Synovial fluid: three functions:** **lubrication**, **shock absorption**, **nutrition of the cartilage** (cartilage is avascular, so it is nourished through the synovial fluid). There is always fluid in the knee. Appearance: **yellowish**, **high viscosity**.
- Clinical: when something happens inside the knee, the synovial membrane produces more fluid; sometimes it must be drained: **arthrocentesis** (transcript "arthrope thetesis").
- Knee arthroscopy video: patella and its hyaline cartilage, synovial membrane, femur and tibia cartilage; on flexion the normal **anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)**.
- **Meniscus:** made of **fibrocartilage**; three functions: **shock absorber**, **stabilizer**, and **congruency** (helps form the joint between femur and tibia, making movement smooth).
- **Bursitis example:** swollen elbow bursa from leaning on the elbows while studying; goes away with time, ice and no pressure.

### Ligaments and tendons; ACL reconstruction (clinical)
- **Ligament: bone to bone. Tendon: muscle to bone.** Ligaments are part of joints.
- Broken ACL on arthroscopy: to stabilize the knee, **ligamentoplasty**: tunnels in bone and usually a **tendon** converted into a new ligament.
- Student questions: taking a tendon loses some strength, so tendons whose function is shared are used, e.g. the **ischiotibial** tendons (there is more than one, so function is kept); the tendon is **folded** to increase strength and diameter. In growing patients the bone tunnels can cross the **epiphyseal plate**; other techniques spare it (fifth-year topic).
- Elbow video: stable joint; when the ligaments are cut, the joint opens.

## 5. Synovial joint types
- **Seven types:** plane, hinge, pivot, condylar, ellipsoid, saddle, ball and socket (spheroid). **Condylar and ellipsoid are the same**: books call them one or the other.
- Classroom exercise: the **elbow** moves in **one** plane (flexion-extension); turning the forearm is **not** the elbow joint (see pivot). The **hip** (in anatomy: the joint between femur and pelvis, in the groin, not the waist) moves in every plane: flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and rotation.

| Type | Surfaces | Axes / movement | Lecturer's examples | [Notes] examples (p. 4) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Plane** | Plane articular surfaces | One; **gliding** only | **Hand and foot** | Carpometacarpal |
| **Hinge** | Resemble a **pulley** | One plane: **flexion-extension** | **Elbow** (distal humerus with ulna), **ankle**, **interphalangeal**; the most common joints | Interphalangeal |
| **Pivot (trochoid)** | One axis + **one surrounding ligament** | **Rotation** | **Proximal radioulnar** (ulna = axis, radius moves around it, ligament keeps it in place; in the elbow but not the elbow joint); **C1-C2** (C1 rotates around the **odontoid process** of C2, a ligament keeps it in place) | Radioulnar, atlanto-odontoid |
| **Condylar = ellipsoid** | One **convex**, one **concave** surface | Two planes: **flexion-extension**, **abduction-adduction** | **Knee** ("the classic example"; think of a hemisphere) | Condylar: metacarpophalangeal; bicondylar: knee, occipito-atlantal |
| **Saddle** | Saddle-shaped; he says "convex in both surfaces" **[verify]** | Wider range: flexion-extension plus **circumduction** of the thumb | **Trapezium-1st metacarpal** (transcript "trapezius") | Trapezio-metacarpal |
| **Ball and socket (spheroid)** | | **Highest range**; every plane (3D) | **Humeral head + glenoid** (shoulder); **femoral head + acetabulum** (hip) | Shoulder, hip |

- [Atlas] Plate 9: hinge (elbow), pivot (median atlantoaxial), saddle (carpometacarpal joint of thumb, trapezium), condyloid (**knee**), ball and socket (hip), plane (**acromioclavicular**).
- **Summary he wants remembered:** uniaxial = hinge, pivot; biaxial = condylar, ellipsoid, saddle (saddle wider); multiaxial = ball and socket.
- **Movements:** plane joints = gliding; flexion-extension and abduction-adduction = **angular** movements; **rotation** (pronation-supination) happens in pivot joints; **circumduction** in ball and socket and "kind of" in saddle joints.
- **Abduction** = separating from the midline; **adduction** = approaching it.

## 6. Planes
| Plane | View | Movements in that plane (his slide) |
|---|---|---|
| **Coronal** | Looking at the person from the front (anterior to posterior) | Abduction-adduction, lateral flexion, eversion-inversion (foot) |
| **Sagittal** | From the side | Flexion-extension |
| **Transverse** | Cut in half, viewed from above | Rotation, pronation-supination |

- Used in anatomy, radiology and physics.
- [Notes] p. 5 table: sagittal plane = flexion-extension (neck, back, shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers, hips, knee; ankle plantar flexion-dorsiflexion); axial plane = rotations (neck; pronation-supination of forearm; external-internal rotation of shoulder, hip, knee); frontal (coronal) = abduction-adduction (shoulder, hip, metacarpophalangeal), inclination (neck, back), elevation-depression (shoulder), radial-ulnar deviation (wrist), inversion-eversion (subtalar). Circumduction = combination of movements in several axes.

## 7. Myology: fascia, tendons, aponeuroses
- Clinical video: hand surgery on an **awake** patient under **local anaesthetic**, so he could move and show which tendon needed repair; muscle and the repaired tendon visible.
- **Fascia:** **superficial fascia** just below the skin (and the fat below the skin); **deep fascia** wraps muscles, vessels, nerves and organs.
- The connective tissue of the muscle groups at its ends forming **tendons** or **aponeuroses**: both attach muscle to bone. **Tendons are cord-like; aponeuroses are wider, sheet-like and thinner.**
- [Notes] The deep fascia forms closed compartments; aponeurosis example: bicipital aponeurosis.

## 9. Origin, insertion, attachments, naming
- **Origin** = the fixed attachment; **insertion** = the attachment that moves. They **change with the movement**, so he prefers "the muscle goes from bone X to bone Y" and knowing the movement it does.
- **Pull-up example:** the arm is fixed to the bar and the trunk moves up: here the origin is at the bar and the insertion at the trunk; if the arm moves and the trunk stays still, they swap.
- **Direct attachment:** muscle fibres attach to bone without a tendon. **Indirect:** through a tendon or aponeurosis. [Notes] Direct insertion is into the periosteum.
- **Some muscles attach to other tendons**, not bone, to maximize other muscles' function: **lumbricals** of the hand.
- **Naming:** location, function, shape and others; names follow no single rule ("don't ask yourselves why is this called gluteus and not abductor maximus of the hip"). [Notes] Criteria: location, function, shape, direction of fibres, heads, fixation points, size/length.
- **Muscles changing plane** ("fundamental to understand their function"): **latissimus dorsi** and **teres major** posterior to anterior; **sartorius** lateral to medial; **interossei** of the hand palmar to dorsal. [Notes] Same list, p. 6: "This concept is very important to understand their function."
