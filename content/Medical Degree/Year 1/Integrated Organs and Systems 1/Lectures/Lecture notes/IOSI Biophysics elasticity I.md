## 1. Every material deforms
- Whatever the force, there is always a deformation, sometimes too small to see. Example: the chair you sit on is about 1-2 µm shorter than it was at 7 am, because it supports your weight; measurable with instruments.
- Human body materials are less rigid and deform more: this session starts the study of **elasticity**.

## 2. Origin of elasticity: the chemical bond as a spring
- Matter: atoms, molecules, cells, tissues, organs; deformation happens at the level of **molecules**.
- Water molecule: textbooks draw a fixed bond length and angle, but both **change continuously**: **temperature = vibration**.
- A chemical bond = **equilibrium between two forces**:

| Force | Origin | Dominates when |
|---|---|---|
| **Attractive** | Shared electrons (orbital) between the atoms | Atoms too far apart |
| **Repulsive** | The two positive **nuclei** repel | Atoms too close |

- The equilibrium distance is where they balance. Any molecular structure (water, a protein's helices) is the result of an equilibrium of forces.
- **Perturbation** (push or pull, then release): the atom returns to the original position: **stable equilibrium**; the bond **behaves like a spring**.
- **Sustained external force:** the atom does not return; a **new equilibrium** distance is reached (longer if pulled, shorter if pushed). This is the building block of elastic behaviour.
- Summed over all bonds of a material, the microscopic change becomes a **macroscopic** change of size.
- [Lucía] Same idea: bonds can be stretched or compressed; reversible up to a point (elastic behaviour).

## 3. Stress
- Two people identical except for the **cross-sectional area** of one ligament; same force applied: the **thinner** ligament deforms more, because the force is shared by fewer molecules/fibres (example: 5 million vs 1 million molecules in the section).
- **Stress = force / area**, units **N/m²** (books: pascals). It is the relative magnitude needed: "it's not enough to say we have this force".
- Aside: "stress" in psychology and the "plasticity" of the brain are borrowed from elasticity (loosely).
- [Lucía] Same example: A1 > A2, same force: the smaller one breaks first.

## 4. Materials and simple stresses
- Living tissue is usually **heterogeneous** (composition differs between locations: e.g. different along a bone, in the shaft, wall or processes) and **anisotropic** (different properties in different **directions**, because of **fibres**: resists more along the axis than across it).
- The analysis starts with **homogeneous and isotropic** materials (same composition and properties everywhere): a simple cube.
- In elasticity the system is at rest: a force on one face is always paired with an **equal and opposite** force on the opposite face.

| Stress | Forces | Name |
|---|---|---|
| **Compressive** | Perpendicular to the faces, pushing in | Normal stress |
| **Tensile** (tension; Spanish "tracción") | Perpendicular, pulling out | Normal stress |
| **Tangential (shear)** (Spanish term garbled in the transcript) | **Parallel** to the faces, opposite directions on opposite faces | 4 possibilities |

- **"Normal" = perpendicular** (geometry), not "usual" and not the Gaussian of statistics.
- Total: **2 normal + 4 shear = 6 simple stresses**; each can give a different response. **Compound** stresses (several at once) come later.
- [Lucía] Same: 6 stresses, 2 normal and 4 shear.

## 5. The stress-strain curve (homogeneous, isotropic material under tension)
- **Strain = (final length - original length) / original length = ΔL / L0.** [Lucía] Dimensionless; compares materials regardless of length.
- Curve (stress on the vertical axis, strain on the horizontal, as he plots it), starting at zero:

| Region | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Linear** | Stress and strain **proportional** |
| **Non-linear** | No longer proportional, but still **reversible** |
| (both above) = **elastic region** | Remove the stress: the strain **disappears** |
| **Yield point (limit)** | Beyond it, part of the deformation becomes **permanent** |
| **Plastic region** | Permanent deformation: on unloading the material returns along a **parallel line**, not to zero strain |
| **Crack (fracture) point** | Bonds break; one broken bond increases the load on the others |

- He suggests this curve fits a **bone** fairly well; ligaments and muscles behave very differently (next sessions). [Lucía] Ligament "toe region" (unfolding of collagen fibres) and muscle curves: not covered yet.
- Every material has its own curve (aluminium, copper, steel, wood, bone); some have no elastic region, some no plastic region (break first: **brittle**, e.g. the pen discussed in class), some no linear region.
- **"Plastic" warning:** everyday plastics may have **no plastic region** in this sense (they spring back or break), while **metals** (a spoon you can bend permanently) do. [Lucía] Copper rod example.

## 6. Clinical application: ligaments
- Exercise that loads a ligament **within the elastic region**: no consequence, the deformation disappears.
- **Beyond the yield point**: the ligament stays **longer** than before, so it holds the joint less well and **injury becomes easier**.
- **Fatigue of materials** (next session): repeating the process can break a ligament **without reaching the crack point**.
