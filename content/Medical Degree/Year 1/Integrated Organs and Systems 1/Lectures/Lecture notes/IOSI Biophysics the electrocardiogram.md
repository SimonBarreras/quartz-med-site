## Exam flags from the lecturer
- **The take-home, stated twice at the end: the ECG can only measure depolarization and repolarization fronts.** Not a fully contracted heart, not a fully relaxed one (sections 3 and 8).
- **The dipole is a model, nothing more.** "The behaviour of the heart is much more complex than what is captured by this dipole... this is just a prediction of the dipole model. There is nothing more behind this idea" (section 8).
- **What the dipole is good for: the orientation of the total dipole**, which is what lets you localise a region of the heart that is not working (section 6).
- **What he expects the students to be able to do with a trace:** obtain the **frequency**, whether the behaviour of the heart is normal, and the **orientation of the dipole**. The clinical reading itself will be taught elsewhere: "this department is going to explain" it, and "you should play a lot with electrocardiograms".
- Practical framing: "it is quite sure that when you finish here you will start as a resident, and then you will be in the emergency room", where this device is used constantly.

## 1. Why the device exists
- Goal: a way to know the **behaviour of the heart of a patient**, ideally **non-invasively**, without disturbing the person.
- **Historical framing:** in the 19th century there was no way to get this. No ultrasound, no X-ray; **only the sounds** the heart produced. Diagnosis was therefore very hard. The electrocardiogram arrives **at the end of the 19th century**, "crossing between the 19th and the 20th".
- **[verify]** He named the inventor, transcribed as "William McClane", and mentioned a prize "in 1924" for it; neither is recoverable from the recording. [Lucía p48] names the **Einthoven triangle** for the three-lead arrangement he describes in section 7.

## 2. The physics: charges, forces and potential difference
- Setup he drew: a line representing the **skin**, charges **inside** the patient (represented as one positive and one negative accumulation), and a **test charge outside**.
- **Electric forces act at long distances**, so the outside charge **perceives** the inside charges: there is an attractive force and a repulsive force, and therefore a **net force in some direction**.
- A net force means that **moving the test charge from point 1 to point 2 changes its energy**.
- In electricity we do not work with that energy but with the **potential**. A change of energy between two points means there is a **potential difference** between them.
- **Therefore: measure the potential at two points and compare.** If the values differ, something about the internal charge distribution can be deduced; and the **sign** of the difference tells you the **orientation** of the configuration, because reversing the charges reverses which point has the higher potential.
- **"The only ingredient I have used is that there is a force acting at a distance."** No formula is needed to see that the measurement is possible.
- **The instrument** is a **voltmeter**: a device with a screen and two wires that measures the voltage across two points. His aside: one costs about 10 euros in a supermarket, and if you put the two electrodes on your chest the reading is not zero, although a proper trace needs a more specific device.
- [Lucía p46] adds the link he left implicit: the **isopotential lines** run out from the source through the other organs **as far as the skin**, which is why electrodes on the skin recover a voltage difference at all.

## 3. Applying it to the myocyte: the three configurations
From the previous session, a myocyte can be **at rest**, **fully contracted**, or **partially contracted** with a **front** separating the two parts.

| Electrode placement | Result |
|---|---|
| **One electrode inside the cell, one outside** | **Measures the membrane voltage directly.** But this means pushing a metallic wire into a myocyte of a living heart: "the patient is only very happy" is his ironic comment. **Possible in the lab** on an isolated cardiomyocyte, as in the video of the previous session; **impossible in the emergency room** |
| **Both electrodes outside, cell at rest** | **Zero.** Outside the cell the voltage is constant, so the difference between two outside points is zero: **nothing can be measured** |
| **Both electrodes outside, cell fully contracted** | **Also zero**, for the same reason, even though the charges are now reversed (negative outside, positive inside). **So a resting cell and a fully contracted cell cannot be told apart from outside** |
| **Both electrodes outside, cell partially contracted** | **Here there is a signal.** On one side of the front the outside voltage is constant at one value, on the other side constant at a different value. Place one electrode on each side of the front and the difference is **not zero** |

- **Conclusion, the central result of the session:** information can only be extracted **where there is a front propagating** along the fibres. Not from a fully contracted heart, not from a fully relaxed one. **Both depolarization and repolarization fronts work.**
- [Lucía p44-45] gives the same argument and the same conclusion.

## 4. Electrode placement in practice
- The approach, unchanged since its invention: take the voltage on **both arms** and on the **left leg** (it can be at the hip or at the ankle).
- **Minimum set: three electrodes**, or **four counting the ground**.
- **The usual approach adds several more in the intercostal spaces**, "so you can get the deterioration in any direction".
- [Lucía p48] **normally six electrodes are employed**, and a normal ECG records **12 voltage differences simultaneously**.

## 5. What the trace should look like, built from first principles
He worked forward from the anatomy rather than from a textbook trace:
- Starting point: a heart with **atria and ventricles**, everything started by the **pacemaker, the sinoatrial node**, a **self-excited system**. The atria contract.
- Then there is an **isolating barrier** between atria and ventricles, which the electric signal can cross **only at one place** (the conduction pathway; see [[IOSI Heart anatomy]]).
- Ventricular contraction **starts in the middle and propagates**, as established in the previous session: it cannot begin everywhere at once.
- With one electrode near the atria and one near the apex, following the front in time:

| Stage | Expected voltage |
|---|---|
| Before contraction | **Zero**: no front |
| Front appears and grows | Voltage **rises**: the more fibres contracted, the larger the signal |
| Front **crosses the apex** and moves away | The same charge distribution is now seen **from the other side**, so the deflection **reverses** |

- Hence the trace shows **positive and negative deflections depending on where the front is relative to the electrodes**. "That's very complex. It can be very difficult to understand what's happening here" , which is the motivation for the simplification that follows.
- [Lucía p47] works the same cycle through **nine numbered situations** (SA node firing, atria fully contracted, septal depolarization, apical depolarization, maximum ventricular contraction, perpendicular dipoles, relaxed, repolarization, fully relaxed), with the voltage expected at each. Useful alongside this section.

## 6. The dipole simplification
- **Definition as he gave it:** replace the whole charge distribution of the front with **a single positive and a single negative charge**, **total charge zero**. "The minimum set of charges that reproduces anything similar."
- A dipole has an **axis**, drawn as an **arrow pointing from the negative to the positive charge**.
- **Dipoles add as vectors:** each myocyte at the front contributes a small dipole pointing in the direction of propagation; combining all of them gives **one total dipole**, which is **perpendicular to the contraction front**.
- [Lucía p46] gives the dipole's field: the isopotential surfaces are spheres around the charges, with a **flat 0 mV surface midway** between them; two opposed dipoles cancel, two aligned dipoles add to a bigger one.
- **Why this is the useful model, his ischemia example:**

| Heart | Fronts | Total dipole |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | As the front passes the apex it continues up **both sides** of the ventricle, left and right | The two combine into a total dipole pointing in the **normal direction** |
| **With an infarcted or ischemic region** | That region **no longer contracts**, so **one of the two fronts is missing** | Only one arrow remains, so the total dipole is **rotated** |

- **Therefore:** the **orientation of the total dipole tells you which region of the heart is not working**. "You can detect if this patient has an ischemia in one region or another just by looking at where this vector is pointing." [Lucía p48] makes the same point.

## 7. Leads and the projection idea
- **Three simultaneous measurements of the potential are enough to get the orientation of the dipole in the frontal plane.**
- **What each lead measures: the projection of the total dipole arrow along that lead's axis.** For a dipole in a given direction, one lead gives a large deflection, another a smaller one, and a lead **perpendicular to the dipole gives almost nothing**.
- **This is what makes the angle recoverable:** find the lead whose projection is **zero** and you know the dipole is **perpendicular to that direction**.
- **"There are six possible ways"** of combining the limb electrodes: the **first, second and third** leads plus three more that the transcription garbles as "amplifying" to the right, to the left and to the foot **[verify]**.
- The remaining **six** traces come from the electrodes **along the intercostal spaces**.
- He displayed a **12-trace ECG** (apologising that the image quality was poor) laid out in columns, and said the students will later be given **the normal values of the angle and the pathological ones**.

## 8. Closing: what the model is and is not
- **The ECG measures only the depolarization and repolarization fronts.**
- **The dipole is a model of the heart.** The real behaviour is **much more complex** than a single dipole.
- What the dipole does give you: a representation of **which volumes of heart muscle are active** at each moment.
- **Beyond the model:** real systems exist that recover a **true 3D representation** of the heart's electrical activity, but they are **used only in very specific, specialised places**.
