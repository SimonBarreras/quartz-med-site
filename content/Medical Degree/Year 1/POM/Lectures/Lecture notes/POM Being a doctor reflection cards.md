## 1. Introduction: medicine enters people's lives
- As doctors you enter your patients' lives, families and problems; medical and personal problems may shock you. We don't just treat a blood pressure; if that patient is dying of cancer, we enter their whole life.
- **The first death you see will affect you.** She remembers the first, not the second, third or fourth, "because it hurt".
- As a student you are "the watcher", on the sidelines, "watching somebody's movie, but the movie was real".
- Few students have seen someone die; a hundred years ago, everyone in the room would have, because death happened at home. Now it is "tucked away in the hospital", which is where you will be.
- You need to process those feelings to keep going: **journaling, talking, writing**, reflecting on what bothered you and why.
- We all carry our own "baggage" (upbringing, imperfect parents, our past). A patient whose problem "hits home" may make you a worse doctor for them: reflect, and sometimes let someone else take over.

## 2. Her story: the first death
- First rotation as a student: **emergency medicine**, after a major storm left much of the US east coast without electricity for about a day (many chainsaw injuries).
- A large man came in with a **heart attack**; CPR; they "called it": stopped resuscitation and stated the **time of death** (e.g. 11:44). The family was screaming in the hallway amid the chaos. Her first death.
- "You're going to see a lot of stuff... take care of yourself and talk to somebody about it, and don't hold it inside."
- She dislikes the ER (surprises, trauma).

## 3. Seeing patients disrespected
- A third-year student told her about a woman whose migraine medication change caused **depression**; her calls to the clinic went unanswered until her husband brought her in. The student felt the patient was not respected but was part of the team.
- You will feel this often: burned-out doctors can become rough and cold. You cannot tell the attending what to do, but **acknowledge it to yourself** and talk to someone; it is not normal.
- **Dr. Rocío's story:** as a student, an attending told a dying patient's family to leave the room, then had the students stand and **watch the man die**. "My heart almost broke in half." Such things would not happen today.

## 4. The cards: group discussion (students' interpretations)
Each group picked cards, read them aloud and discussed. Example card read by the lecturer: "**Habrá momentos muy bajos. No los pasas solo.**" (There will be very low moments. You don't go through them alone.)

| Card (as read) | Students' discussion |
|---|---|
| Don't become an advertising board (drawing of pens and highlighters with drug brand names) | Don't be used as publicity by the pharmaceutical industry; stay **impartial** and independent of companies; gifts or paid events create influence. Example: the old **Lucky Strike** adverts claiming doctors said they were "less irritating", which people read as a doctor recommending smoking; the US opioid crisis. One student: having a preference for a good drug is fine, being an advert is not. Also: don't become a machine for profit or patient volume; you are there to heal |
| "Your professional development, free of industry" **[verify]** wording | Don't let brands manipulate what you say or recommend |
| "Cambia de bata con frecuencia" (change your white coat often) | Literally keep it clean; deeper: **adapt**, stay open to new ideas, other doctors' opinions, other specialties, teamwork, even wellness practices such as meditation |
| Be careful on social media (picture of a doctor) | **Patient confidentiality** first (by law and by the Hippocratic oath); never post patients; educational content about yourself is acceptable |
| "Libra las guardias" | Interpreted as "get through / survive your on-call shifts": **take care of yourself** (water, food, sleep, comfortable clothes), don't rely on stimulants, protect yourself because a bad state means worse care; take breaks |
