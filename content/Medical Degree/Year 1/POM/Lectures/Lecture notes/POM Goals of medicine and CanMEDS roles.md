## 1. Why did you choose medicine? / What does a firefighter do?
- Opening private reflection: why did you choose medicine (not shared).
- Group brainstorm: **what does a firefighter (bombero) do?** Beyond putting out fires: rescuing people and animals, help in natural disasters (floods, storms, earthquakes, fallen trees), clearing chemical spills, first aid, **prevention** (presence at events, clearing land in summer), **education** in schools and villages, risking their lives (commitment to society), and their mere presence gives a **sense of security**; fire stations are a place of refuge (e.g. for pilgrims on the Camino de Santiago).
- Point: a firefighter does much more than the obvious role; so does a doctor.

## 2. The CanMEDS roles
- **CanMEDS** ("Canadian Medical"): used internationally by many medical schools to define the **roles** of a doctor and the **competencies** required. **Seven roles**.
- Built from the class brainstorm ("what do doctors do?"):

| Role | What he described |
|---|---|
| **Medical expert** (at the centre) | The whole clinical process: prepare for the patient, **observe** (limp, grimace, jaundice, pallor), **history**, **physical examination**, investigations if needed, **differential diagnosis**, diagnosis, **care plan** (pharmacological or not), treatment. Includes **prevention** and **health promotion** |
| **Communicator** | With patients, families and colleagues, with respect and dignity; **tone and non-verbal** communication matter; breaking bad news |
| **Collaborator** | With other **specialties** (e.g. referring for radiotherapy for bone pain) and other **professions** (nurses, social workers, therapists), all with equal roles; increasingly with engineers, AI and data experts |
| **Advocate** | From writing to authorities for a family's home care, to policy and leadership |
| **Scholar** ("scholarship") | **Research**, **teaching**, **quality improvement** |
| **Professional** | Caring for persons; commitment to the patient, to society, to the profession (to be developed in the professionalism class) |
| Seventh role | Not named clearly in the transcript. He mentions **managers and leaders** of teams, services and hospitals within advocacy. **[verify]** against the slide |

- At the centre of all the roles: being **human**: **empathy, compassion, respect, dignity**. Empathy = putting yourself in the other person's shoes (e.g. a very active person after a stroke; a 29-year-old with advanced cancer). Compassion vs empathy will be covered in later workshops.

### Examples he gave for the roles
- **Medical expert:** a 78-year-old with diabetes and long-standing kidney disease, short of breath, swollen feet: think of **heart failure**, look for signs (e.g. jugular venous distension), order an **echocardiogram**.
- **Humanizing:** not "a 63-year-old diabetic" but "José, 63, with diabetes, widowed two years ago, lives in a flat without a lift, a retired artist".
- **Cure, control, comfort:** sometimes we can cure; sometimes not, but we can control (e.g. immunotherapies for cancers and autoimmune diseases); when we cannot control, we can always **comfort**. He gets upset when doctors say "there's nothing more we can do". About 5 to 10% of people die suddenly; 85 to 90% after an illness of weeks, months or years. Chronic illness and multimorbidity are increasing.
- **Research (scholar):** not only diseases and treatments, also **communication** and the **health system**:
  - Palliative care unit in Ottawa: patients felt doctors spent little time with them. A paper showed that doctors who **sat down** were perceived as spending more time than those who stood, for the **same** measured time. They bought folding chairs; within months patients felt doctors spent much more time.
  - Nova Scotia: a protocol sent end-of-life patients by ambulance to the emergency room; most wanted to be at home. After training paramedics in basic palliative care (pain crisis, breathlessness, conversations), transfers fell by **more than 60% in 18 months**; paramedics found more meaning in their work.
- **Primary care** is "the foundation, the bastion of any healthcare system"; countries that underinvest have overcrowded emergency rooms. Do not think of it as second class.
- **Prevention** in every specialty (e.g. obstetrics detecting a placenta in the wrong position; endocrinology controlling diabetes to prevent complications); **public health** (first called in the COVID pandemic, with infectious disease, respiratory and immunology specialists).
- **Advocacy (his story):** as a fifth-year student in South Africa, a Portuguese patient with advanced prostate cancer died in uncontrolled pain, treated only with **pethidine** (a weak opioid with toxic metabolites causing **opioid neurotoxicity**: hallucinations, confusion, agitation, twitching), because morphine was feared as addictive. In Canada (1993-94) he refused to increase a patient's very low morphine dose for fear of addiction; the patient left in tears ("I hope one day doctors like you can look after people like me"). He did a short course in Toronto, called the patient back, apologized, and cared for him comfortably in his last three months. He then specialized (Edmonton) and founded **Pallium Canada** (2000), training health professionals across Canada in basic palliative care (number garbled, "mighty thousand" **[verify]**).
  - Opioids: "like a car": safe if used properly, dangerous if not; appropriate doses depend on the patient (lower in severe kidney or lung disease, or neuromuscular disease affecting breathing).
  - Portugal has had a law since **2012** giving every citizen the right to palliative care, but it is not fully implemented: leaders are needed to make laws real.
  - "No matter what specialty you go into, you will be caring for patients with serious illnesses; don't run away from them."
- **Communicator:** "I'm really busy, I'll come back later" vs "Are you okay? I've got a very big priority and then I'll come back to you": same content, different tone; the patient feels it. Breaking bad news (e.g. worse lung function in **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis**): "I wish I were giving you good news... I'm worried about what I'm seeing" gives a **warning shot**, then the news, keeping **realistic hope**. Avoid **false hope**: it takes away time to prepare.
- **Collaborator:** his son, a biomedical engineer, designed a cheap **laparoscopy simulator** (a blue box) now used here, working with gastroenterologists.

## 3. The Hastings Institute goals of medicine
- Founded in **1969** in the USA by a **psychiatrist and a philosopher**, at a time of change (civil rights movement, Vietnam War, advances in mental health care and technology), to deal with the **ethical challenges** of a medicine that could now prolong life. Still exists today.
- **The goals of medicine:**
  1. **Prevention of disease and injury, and promotion and maintenance of health.**
  2. **Relief of pain and suffering.**
  3. **Care and cure of those with a malady, and care of those who cannot be cured.**
  4. **Avoidance of premature death and pursuit of a peaceful death.**
- Group examples:
  - Prevention: sport and strength training for bone density (osteoporosis), hygiene and hand washing, awareness campaigns (smoking), **screening**, **universal health care / access** (in the USA, undiagnosed diabetes in people who cannot afford care).
  - Relief of suffering: treating pain in incurable cancer; even just talking with and comforting the patient.
  - Peaceful death: a friend of Dr. Jennifer's, in her 30s with breast cancer, kept being sent to the emergency room and died in hospital; doctors should advocate for a peaceful death.

## 4. Euthanasia and assisted suicide (the lecturer's position)
- A student raised euthanasia under "peaceful death". He stated it is **controversial** and gave **his own view**, saying there are other viewpoints and pros and cons.
- Terms: **assisted suicide** = the doctor prescribes a lethal substance and the person takes it themselves (e.g. Oregon, Netherlands, Belgium); **euthanasia** = the doctor administers it. In Canada both fall under **MAID** (medical assistance in dying), a **euphemism** he finds troubling (compared with "apartheid" as a euphemism for racism).
- His position: it **should not be part of a doctor's role**; our role is to maintain life, but also to **let go** when it is time, not to end life. "**This medical school believes it's not part of being a doctor.**"
- His arguments (he testified for the Canadian government in 2013-2014, received threats and needed a police escort):
  - **No real choice without palliative care**: if the only way to relieve suffering is to end your life, society has failed.
  - **Social slippery slope**: first only for the very end of life, then dementia, children... In Canada the end-of-life requirement was later removed. Cases: **about 13,500 in 2023**, **16,000 to 18,000** per year by 2024-2025 (as given). In 2023, **loneliness** was listed as a reason in about **1,300** cases.
  - **Autonomy** has become **imbalanced**: autonomy has limits in society (you cannot drive at 200 km/h). Even an individual request has **ripple effects**: thresholds drop; people with disabilities and elderly people feel less valued (disability associations sued).
  - Depression is not allowed as the main reason in Canada, but he has seen cases recorded under other diagnoses.
  - The **UK** recently voted against legalization because of gaps in palliative care; Spain has legalized it; Portugal has not.
- **The doctor's role regardless:** know how to respond to "I cannot live like this", "my life is not worth living": often a **call for help**, not a request for euthanasia. Sit down, explore the **source of the suffering** with compassion and without judgment.
- **Palliative sedation:** for patients in the **last days or weeks** with a refractory symptom (e.g. pain, severe breathlessness in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis) despite all treatments: **intentionally lowering consciousness** with the **lowest effective dose** of a sedative, usually **midazolam** (short-acting benzodiazepine, adjustable). Needed in about **5%** of dying patients.
- **Four pillars of ethics:** **autonomy**, **beneficence** (doing good), **non-maleficence** (not doing harm), **justice** (and equity). They must stay **balanced**.

## 5. Videos and lessons on the doctor-patient relationship
- **Burnout video** (ChenMed): doctors' goals are prevention, cure and easing suffering; documentation and lack of control over schedules lead to burnout; remember why you went into medicine.
- **Patch Adams** clip: a professor presenting a diabetic patient's ulcers to students without addressing her; a student asks her name ("Marjorie") and greets her. The palliative care lecturer said this happened when he was training, and to his own family (next point).
- **His sister's story:** as a final-year student he saw a tumour in her acetabulum on an X-ray. The orthopaedic surgeon (his admired professor) never greeted them, held the X-ray to the window and said "I've got to send you to a cancer doctor". Surgery showed a **benign** tumour, but she was traumatized for years by how it was told.
- **Dr. Jennifer's stories:** an infectious disease doctor who listened and did a one-minute **magic trick** for her son; a doctor who put a hand on hers and said "we're going to find out what's wrong with you". "Take the things you like and put them in your pocket; throw out the things you don't."

## 6. Film clip: code status and informed consent
- Clip: a nurse sits with the patient, shares a popsicle, admits the cancer is not being killed, and explains **code status**: **full code** (resuscitation, ICU) vs **DNR** (do not resuscitate). The patient chooses DNR.
- Lesson on **autonomy vs informed consent**: in very advanced disease (last weeks or months, advanced heart disease or cancer), CPR succeeds in **less than about 4%**; on TV and in films it succeeds in about **75%**, which is what people believe. CPR is very effective in sudden events (heart attack, trauma, lightning).
- How he discusses it: we are here to look after you, there is still a lot of living to do; at the very end, when treatments have not worked (not because you gave up), compressions and shocks work in about 4%, survivors are usually on machines, ribs are broken. With this information most choose not to be resuscitated; some still choose everything, and that must be respected.
- Asking "do you want us to resuscitate you?" without this context gives **autonomy without informed consent**.

## 7. History of the profession
- **Hippocrates**: the **Hippocratic oath** (help patients and society, **do no harm**); scientific thinking about causes and treatments.
- **Asclepius**: mythological figure whose **staff with a snake** became a symbol of medicine (he calls it the caduceus **[verify]**). His school: you can only be a healer when you have **healed yourself**, are aware of yourself and your biases; seeing the patient as a **whole person**.
- **Life expectancy around 1900**: about **50 to 60**; pneumonia a common cause of death at all ages.
- **Penicillin** changed this, but also contributed to a **death-denying society**; large hospitals (early to mid 1900s) moved **death from the community to hospitals**. Few students have seen someone die; a century ago everyone had.
- **"To cure sometimes, to relieve often, to comfort always."**
- ICU picture: a great advance, but balance cure and control with quality of life; sometimes say "these treatments won't help you; let's talk about other possibilities".

## 8. Medicine in your generation
- **Personalized medicine**: targeting cancers by their receptors instead of the same chemotherapy for all.
- **Artificial intelligence**, digital health.
- **Antimicrobial resistance** ("superbugs"): e.g. tuberculosis now resistant to treatments he used in South Africa. **Stewardship**: antibiotics for sore throats (mostly viral) breed resistance; examine, test, and if viral, no antibiotic ("come back in two or three days if worse").
- **Self-awareness**: notice your nerves as a student; it is fine to tell your supervisor or friends.
- **Lifelong learning**, teamwork, **quality improvement**.

## 9. Quadruple aim (not in the exam)
- Improve the **patient experience**, **health outcomes**, **lower costs**, and improve the **care providers' experience**.
