## 1. What nuclear medicine is
- Completely different from other specialties: it studies the **function** of organs; radiology studies **anatomy**.
- Uses **small amounts of radioactive material**, **radiopharmaceuticals**, for diagnosis and treatment of different diseases.
- Usually combined with an anatomical image: molecular imaging fused with CT (PET-CT, SPECT-CT): **hybrid imaging**.

## 2. The radiopharmaceutical
- A molecule with two parts:

| Part | Determines | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Radioisotope** | **Physical characteristics** (the radiation emitted, detection) | The radioactive part |
| **Pharmaceutical** | **Biological behaviour** | Non-radioactive part; the **vehicle** that takes the isotope to the target organ (e.g. a kidney-seeking pharmaceutical to study the kidneys) |

- Administration: intravenous, inhalation, oral.
- Many combinations of isotope and pharmaceutical, chosen for what you want to study or treat.
- [Lucía] The drug targets the site; the isotope "acts as a flashlight".

### Three ways the pharmaceutical reaches its target
1. **Binding a cellular receptor**: very common in oncology. Tumour cells are more active and **overexpress receptors/transporters** (e.g. **glucose transporters**: radioactive glucose, **FDG**, enters through them, so tumour cells take up more than normal cells).
2. **Normal cell channels**: e.g. **iodine** for the thyroid, entering through the **sodium-iodide transporter**.
3. **A support/carrier** created for the purpose: e.g. **microparticles** of calcium or resin containing the isotope, placed directly in the organ. Example: treatment of **intraperitoneal carcinomatosis** in **ovarian cancer**: calcium microparticles distributed through the peritoneal cavity.
- [Lucía] For thyroid scintigraphy no drug is needed: iodine is incorporated naturally (hormonogenesis). Iodine emits gamma and beta, so it serves for diagnosis and for thyroid cancer therapy.

### Production of radioisotopes
- **Nuclear reactors**, **generators** and **cyclotrons**. Her centre has a cyclotron in the nuclear medicine facility, so they produce their own radiopharmaceuticals.

## 3. Radioisotopes and types of radiation
- Isotopes can be stable or unstable; radioisotopes are **unstable** and **emit energy to reach stability**. That energy is what the cameras detect. (She refers to the biophysics lectures by "Javier Burgette" **[verify]** name.)

| Radiation | Energy / damage | Penetration | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha** | Very high energy, a lot of damage | Very, very short | **Therapy**, e.g. treating surfaces without damaging deeper tissue |
| **Beta** (beta+ and beta-) | Less harmful than alpha | Farther than alpha | **Beta-: therapy**; **beta+ (positrons): diagnosis (PET)** |
| **Gamma** | Less ionizing | More penetrating than beta | **Only diagnostic imaging**, not treatment |

- Neutron radiation exists but belongs to radiotherapy, not nuclear medicine.
- Radiopharmaceuticals often emit **a combination** of radiations: a therapeutic (alpha or beta-) agent may also emit gamma, allowing **post-treatment imaging**. The activity given is calculated to produce the intended damage and no more.
- [Lucía] Alpha stopped by paper, beta by aluminium foil, gamma by lead. For a deep (e.g. spinal) lesion a gamma emitter is chosen for its penetration.

## 4. Therapy vs diagnosis
- **Therapy:** **alpha** and **beta-minus** emitters (tumour treatments).
- **Diagnosis:** **beta-plus** (positrons) for **PET**; **gamma** for the **gamma camera** and **SPECT** (the gamma camera image in 3D).
- [Lucía] Same division.

## 5. Examples of radiopharmaceuticals
| Radiopharmaceutical | Isotope / emission | Target | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **18F-FDG** | Fluorine-18 (PET) | Glucose transporters | Most tumour cells; "you have to know" |
| **68Ga-DOTATOC** | Gallium-68 (PET) | **Somatostatin receptors** | Neuroendocrine tumours (overexpress them) |
| **99mTc-bisphosphonates** | **Technetium-99m** (gamma) | Bone | Bone scan; Tc-99m is the most common gamma-camera isotope |
| **99mTc-MAG3** | Technetium-99m | Kidneys | Renal studies |
| **Yttrium-90** microspheres (transcript "Y9-3S"; yttrium **[verify]**) | Beta- | Deposited in the tumour via **intra-arterial** injection (not receptors or channels) | **Liver tumours** (transcript "batheic" **[verify]**) |
| **Lutetium-DOTATATE** (transcript "nutrition"; lutetium **[verify]**) | Beta- | Somatostatin receptors | Treatment of neuroendocrine tumours |

- Patients become radioactive: in X-rays the machine emits radiation; in nuclear medicine **the patient is the source**. Hence shielded rooms for treatment administration and measures to protect doctors and nurses.

## 6. Theragnosis
- Before treating, confirm that the tumour **still expresses the receptor**: a more aggressive tumour may have lost it; the therapeutic agent would then not bind and would irradiate the body without benefit.
- So: **same (or similar) pharmaceutical, change only the radioisotope**: first image with a positron emitter, then treat with a beta-minus emitter.
  - **Somatostatin receptors:** 68Ga-DOTATOC to see the receptors; then the lutetium (**[verify]**) DOTATATE to treat.
  - **PSMA** (antigen on the membrane of prostate cancer cells): **68Ga-PSMA** PET to locate the tumour, then the same PSMA ligand with the therapeutic isotope (transcript "nutrition", lutetium **[verify]**) to destroy it (beta-minus).
- **Theragnosis** = combination of diagnostic imaging and therapy. "That's very important." (More in L2.)

## 7. Emission vs transmission imaging; the cameras
- **Transmission imaging** (X-ray, CT): the machine emits radiation that crosses the patient; images from attenuation.
- **Emission imaging** (nuclear medicine): the **patient emits**; devices detect it.
- A PET image alone is only molecular imaging (no anatomy); fused with CT it gives both.

| Camera | Isotopes | Configuration |
|---|---|---|
| **Gamma camera / SPECT-CT** | Gamma emitters; gamma rays detected **directly** | **Two detectors** (anterior and posterior) with **collimators** that accept only radiation perpendicular to them; often a CT behind |
| **PET-CT** | Positron emitters (detection explained in L2) | A whole **ring** of detectors, CT inside |

## 8. Bone scintigraphy, SPECT and SPECT-CT
- **Planar whole-body bone scan** (old camera without CT): Tc-99m bisphosphonates; anterior and posterior views; only bone is seen (functional imaging). Example: normal distribution with **increased uptake in one joint** (inflammation).
- Planar images may not localize an uptake (e.g. which part of a vertebra, or a rib vs the vertebra): **SPECT** moves the detectors **around the patient** for a **volumetric (3D)** image with axial, coronal and sagittal planes; can be whole-body.
- **SPECT-CT fusion:** the SPECT is coloured and fused over the CT, localizing the activity precisely on the anatomy.
- **Bisphosphonates** are absorbed by the surface of **hydroxyapatite crystals**. Studies infection, trauma, tumours and other bone conditions.
- Practice: compare a lesion (e.g. skull) with the whole body, then a dedicated planar image or SPECT of the region.
- **Normal distribution:** kidneys and **bladder** visible (urinary elimination); the bladder is **masked** because it takes all the counts.
- **Dynamic study** for inflammation or infection: from the vascular injection, to the tissue phase, to early bone images; then late images (about 2 to 3 hours later) once the tracer has incorporated.
- **Case: temporomandibular joint** (SPECT): a young patient (transcript "70/75 year old" **[verify]**; context implies a teenager) with asymmetric mandible. Surgery only if the joint is **no longer active** (joints usually active until about 18 to 21); otherwise bone keeps growing and the deformity worsens. Both joints were very active: **not operable** yet.
- [Lucía] Bisphosphonates (MDP) taken up in proportion to vascularization and osteoblastic activity; physiological uptake in joints, sternum, spine, sacroiliac joints, kidneys and bladder. Usually planar first, then SPECT/CT if an abnormal uptake needs localizing.

## 9. Questions answered
- **How long is the patient radioactive?** Depends on the isotope's **half-life**: **Tc-99m about 6 hours**, so about 24 hours. **F-18**: transcript "a 100 and I think that is nine minutes"; in L2 she gives **109 minutes**: by the end of the day the patient no longer emits and could repeat a study the next day.
- Another nuclear medicine study must wait until the first radiopharmaceutical has been eliminated, or images would mix.
- Masking of organs (bladder, injection site...) with software (recording ends here).
- [Lucía] Other conventional studies (not recorded this year): **renal scintigraphy** (Tc-MAG3, Tc-DTPA; dynamic studies with activity-time curves); **myocardial perfusion** (Tc-MIBI; stress vs rest: defect only in stress = ischaemia; in both = infarction); **sentinel node** (Tc-nanocolloidal albumin; first node draining a tumour, e.g. melanoma); **thyroid** (iodine; cold nodule = reduced uptake).
