## 1. Reference values
| Ion | Extracellular (mmol/L) | Intracellular (mmol/L) |
|---|---|---|
| Na+ | 145 | 15 |
| K+ | 5 | 150 (transcript "550"; 150 matches the lecture table **[verify]**) |

- Potassium is mainly inside, sodium mainly outside, but there is always a small concentration on the other side.
- Nernst potentials in a normal neuron: **Na about +60 mV, K about -90 mV**. Resting membrane potential about **-70 mV**, an equilibrium between K pulling towards -90 and Na pulling towards +60. **Threshold** to trigger an action potential about **-50 mV**, a property of the cell that does not change in these scenarios.
- Boltzmann constant given in class: **1.38 x 10^-23 J/K**.

## 2. Scenario 1: severe hyponatremia
- Question: extracellular Na falls to **120 mmol/L** (hyponatremia: extracellular sodium below normal; "natrium", the Latin name, gives the symbol Na). How does this affect the Nernst potentials of Na and K?
- "You're going too far": first answer about the Nernst potentials, before jumping to action potentials.
- Do not assume the missing sodium went into the cell (it may be the kidneys extracting too much). **Only extracellular sodium changes**; intracellular Na and both K values stay the same.
- **K Nernst potential: unchanged** (-90 mV), because it depends only on K concentrations outside and inside.
- **Na Nernst potential: decreases** (below +60 mV): the top of the fraction is smaller, so the logarithm is smaller. The limits within which the membrane potential can move become narrower.
- Follow-up (not on the sheet): with Na's Nernst potential lowered (e.g. to about 40 mV), the **resting membrane potential decreases** (below -70 mV). The threshold stays at -50 mV, so it is **harder to trigger an action potential**. A small change in sodium can have large consequences, in neurons and, as will be seen, in muscle, especially the heart.
- [Lucía] Same answers; less Na entry, RMP more negative.

## 3. Scenario 2: hypokalemia
- Extracellular K falls to **3 mmol/L** (normal 5).
- **Na Nernst potential: unchanged.**
- **K Nernst potential: decreases**: the top of the fraction is smaller, so it becomes **more negative**, e.g. about **-95 mV** instead of -90 ("you have to do the numbers to get the correct value").
- "Don't be confused about what reduction means: **reduction means more negative**."
- Membrane potential **decreases** again, so it is **more difficult to trigger an action potential**. Different mechanism from scenario 1 (K's Nernst potential vs Na's), **same consequence**.
- [Lucía] Same; RMP decreases towards -90 mV.

## 4. Scenario 3: sodium permeability halved (at rest)
- Resting situation, all concentrations normal; only p(Na) is halved. Effect on the membrane potential?
- (Some students thought it would only change timing, not the value; he corrected this.)
- Explanation with the Goldman equation: ignore chloride (its potential, about -70 mV, is close to rest). It is a **competition** between a sodium term and a potassium term: if the K term were zero, Na wins (+60 mV); if the Na term were zero, K wins (-90 mV). -70 mV results because p(Na) is smaller than p(K).
- Halving p(Na) reduces sodium's influence further: the **membrane potential falls below -70 mV**.
- **Take-home message:** the membrane potential changes with **concentrations** and with **permeabilities**.
- If instead p(Na) **doubles**: the membrane potential **increases**.
- [Lucía] Same: the decrease comes from permeability, not from a change in the Nernst potential. **[check]** She gives p(Na) = 0.02 p(K) here but 0.002 p(K) in her lecture notes.

## 5. Concentrations do not change during an action potential
- During depolarization many sodium channels open and many sodium ions cross. Do the intra- and extracellular concentrations change? **No.**
- The number crossing is tiny compared with the pools: e.g. 2,000 ions crossing against perhaps 10^10 ions outside. "This is something that you need to remember."
- The charge distribution changes only right next to the membrane.

- Hint: with **more** sodium permeability, sodium crosses **faster**, so the time is **reduced**. "I leave that for you"; he would send an answer.
