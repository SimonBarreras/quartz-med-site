## 2. Anatomy reminder
"The anatomy is just a very good reminder."

### Sympathetic
- Mainly based on the **sympathetic chain**: a chain of ganglia just in front of the vertebral bodies, **one on the left and one on the right**, except the last one: a **single** ganglion in the middle, the **ganglion impar** ("impar as in not the pair"). All the others are bilateral.
- Groups of ganglia: celiac, aorticorenal, hypogastric, mesenteric. [Slides] Prevertebral ganglia: **celiac**, **superior and inferior mesenteric**, **aortico-renal**, **hypogastric**. Slides also list the **splanchnic nerve** and the **spinal nerve (piloerector...)**.
- **Two neurons, always**, in the ANS: the first leaves the spinal cord, connects with the second, and the second goes to the effector.
- **Short preganglionic** (spinal cord to the chain or ganglia), **long postganglionic** (ganglia to the effector).

### Parasympathetic
- "A completely different system when it comes to anatomy": **cranial nerves** and **sacral nerves**, and "in the middle there's nothing". The sympathetic is mainly a chain along the spine.
- Above C1 the nerves that come out are the cranial nerves. [Slides] **Cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X** (transcript garbled here).
- **Cranial nerve X, the vagus nerve**, the most important: about **75%** of the parasympathetic capacity on its own (also [Slides]).
- **Long preganglionic**, **very short postganglionic**: the opposite of the sympathetic.
- [Lucía] Brainstem nuclei: Edinger-Westphal (III), superior and inferior salivatory (VII), dorsal vagal (X), ambiguus (IX and X); sacral S2 to S4. The sympathetic preganglionic neurons are in the intermediolateral columns T1 to L2.

## 3. Neurotransmitters: acetylcholine and noradrenaline
- Two completely different molecules: **acetylcholine (ACh)** and **noradrenaline**. **Noradrenaline = norepinephrine**, **adrenaline = epinephrine**: the same thing; he uses either.
- **Cholinergic** = mediated by acetylcholine. **Adrenergic** = fibres whose transmitter is noradrenaline.

| Fibre | Transmitter |
|---|---|
| **All preganglionic** (sympathetic and parasympathetic) | **ACh**, always |
| **Parasympathetic postganglionic** | **ACh**, always |
| **Sympathetic postganglionic** | **Mainly noradrenaline** |
| Exception: sympathetic postganglionic to **sweat glands** (and some blood vessels) | **ACh** |
| Exception: **adrenal gland** | the second "neuron" is not a neuron (section 7) |

- "Remember only sweat glands. Sweat glands are the exception." [Slides] "S-ANS: mainly adrenergic (except sweat glands and some blood vessels)."
- **[check]** [Lucía] lists **sweat glands and piloerector muscles** as the cholinergic exceptions (muscarinic M3 for sweat glands); elsewhere in the same notes she calls arrector pili muscles adrenergic. Lecturer and slides: sweat glands (and some blood vessels). The slides side with the lecturer.
- [Lucía] Preganglionic co-transmitters (enkephalin, substance P, neurotensin) are modulatory.

## 4. Cholinergic receptors
- Every transmitter, hormone or drug needs a **mechanism of action**: a chemical and a **receptor**, "like putting a key into a keyhole". Each molecule has its specific receptor(s) (one or two in this case).

| | Muscarinic | Nicotinic |
|---|---|---|
| Named after | **Muscarine**, from some mushrooms ([Slides] "poison from toadstools"; image: *Clitocybe dealbata*) | **Nicotine** |
| Mechanism | **G protein** (he did not go into it) | **Ligand-gated ion channel** |
| Subtypes | **M1 to M5** | **N1 and N2** |
| Location [Slides] | Postganglionic cholinergic, P and S | Autonomic ganglia (pre-post junction), P and S; **N1: skeletal muscle**, **N2: ganglia** |

- [Lucía] Muscarinic subtypes: M1 CNS and stomach secretion; M2 heart; M3 smooth muscle; M4 pancreas, lungs; M5 salivary glands, pupil sphincter.
- **Atropine**: "one drug you have to remember". A **vagolytic**: it inactivates the parasympathetic nervous system. Pharmacology "will come step by step".

## 5. Adrenergic receptors
- Sympathetic postganglionic fibres are the only adrenergic ones; they work through adrenergic receptors.
- **Alpha** (alpha-1, alpha-2) and **beta** (beta-1, beta-2, beta-3). All **G protein coupled**. Activated by **noradrenaline** (the sympathetic postganglionic transmitter) and by **adrenaline**.
- **Agonist** = activates; **antagonist** = inactivates.
- **Noradrenaline: mainly alpha agonist.** **Adrenaline: alpha and beta**, **beta-2 > beta-1 > alpha** (also [Slides]).
- Used clinically, e.g. in **hypertension** and possibly asthma (passage garbled; transcript "for my ass mom" **[verify]**).

### Locations
| Receptor | Lecturer | [Slides] |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha-1 | **Vascular smooth muscle** | Vascular smooth muscle; GI tract and bladder |
| Alpha-2 | not mentioned | **Presynaptic**; **negative feedback** (inhibition) |
| Beta-1 | **Heart** | Heart; salivary glands, fat, kidney |
| Beta-2 | **Respiratory system, mainly the bronchi** | Bronchial, bladder and GI smooth muscle |
| Beta-3 | not mentioned | Fat |

- [Lucía] Alpha-1: postsynaptic, IP3, Ca2+ release, smooth muscle contraction. Alpha-2: pre- and postsynaptic (autoreceptor), adenylate cyclase inhibition, inhibits noradrenaline release. Beta-1, -2, -3: postsynaptic, adenylate cyclase stimulation, increased cAMP. (Compare [[FMF Physiology Smooth muscle]]: IP3 contracts, cAMP relaxes.)
- Mechanism of action of adrenergic receptors: see the slide.

### Clinical example: pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine
- "You all use alpha-1 agonists without even knowing it": **pseudoephedrine** and **phenylephrine** are potent **alpha agonists** ("I'm sure once in your lifetime you've taken any of these drugs").
- Runny nose: they cause **vasoconstriction** in the nose, which stops the runny nose; that is why the nose feels dry. "The effect is purely alpha."
- They also act on the rest of the body: hence the warnings, e.g. **be careful if you have heart disease** ("cardiopathy").

## 6. Elimination of the neurotransmitter
- Sequence: the signal reaches the end of the axon, the transmitter is released, it activates the postganglionic (postsynaptic) receptor, which creates a new action potential; then the transmitter must be eliminated. "The elimination is completely different" for the two.

| | Acetylcholine | Noradrenaline |
|---|---|---|
| Enzyme | **Acetylcholinesterase** ("esterase" = it cuts) | **Catechol-O-methyltransferase** (transcript "OBCGNT", "catechol-o-mintyl transparase") |
| Products | **Acetate + choline** | An **inactive metabolite**, excreted |
| Recycling | **Choline is reabsorbed** to make a new ACh molecule | None: completely inactivated; each new molecule must be made from scratch |

- **[verify]** "Noradrenaline is simply completely inactivated" with no part reused: no vault source covers noradrenaline removal (the elimination slide is a figure, "xxx" in the text). Check the slide and a physiology textbook for reuptake.
- **Catecholamines:** "the name of the family": adrenaline, noradrenaline and others (transcript "Dovermines, glutamines" **[verify]**). He defined them as all drugs acting on adrenergic receptors **[verify]** against a source.

## 7. Adrenal medulla: the exception
- Normal sympathetic route: short preganglionic (spinal cord to chain or ganglia), long postganglionic (ganglia to effector).
- Adrenal medulla: the **preganglionic fibre goes directly**, via the spinal nerve, to the **suprarenal (adrenal) gland** ("the little hat on top of the kidney") and connects there.
- The connection is **not a proper synapse** (a synapse is nerve to nerve): the nerve connects to a **cell of the adrenal medulla**, a **secretory** cell directly connected to the **bloodstream**.
- Still sympathetic: cholinergic preganglionic fibre, ACh released between the axon end and the gland "just like with the synapse". But the second element is a **gland**, which releases **noradrenaline and adrenaline, actually more adrenaline than noradrenaline**, "keep it simple", **directly into the blood**.
- [Slides] Preganglionic fibres enter the adrenal medulla; direct excretion of catecholamines into the bloodstream.
- [Lucía] These are **chromaffin cells** (modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons), releasing adrenaline (80%) and noradrenaline (20%); preganglionic fibres from T5 to T9.

### Why it matters: vascular tone and redistribution of blood
- Link to the nasal decongestant example: activating the sympathetic system causes **vasoconstriction** (diameter less); vasodilation = diameter more.
- We always need a **vascular tone**: vessels are always contracting and relaxing.
- The vascular system is **closed**, roughly **5 litres of blood**: the total is fixed, so blood must be taken from one side and sent to another (now, to the brain because you are thinking; not to the muscles, since only the lecturer is moving).
- Dilating one bed and constricting another moves the **cardiac output** (litres leaving the heart every minute) around the body. The adrenal medulla is interesting here because it releases catecholamines **directly into the blood**.

## 8. ANS actions
- Physiology is always about balance and compensation; the two systems are **usually opposite**: one activates, the other inhibits.
- Not that simple: they are **not distributed everywhere in the same proportion**, and even with the same distribution (e.g. the **heart**), the effect of one can dominate.
- [Slides] Distribution may be unequal: **smooth muscle and kidney: sympathetic**; **genitalia: both, but differently located**; **heart: even distribution**.
- **Sympathetic: "fight or flight"** (strength; [Slides] strength, energy, movement). Example: something of "700 kilos" in front of you (garbled).
- **Parasympathetic: "rest and digest"**, repair, restore, relax. Example: after lunch reading a book (anecdote: "don't watch Netflix... you can read a book much better").
- Typical opposite pairs: saliva reduced vs stimulated; heart rate increased vs decreased; bronchoconstriction vs bronchodilation.
- **Sympathetic effects mentioned:** increased heart rate (tachycardia), increased blood pressure (hypertension), decreased digestion (when trying to fight "there's no need to have blood go to your problems", last word garbled). Part of this list is garbled **[verify]**: see the slide.
- Parasympathetic: the opposite. Full actions table: see the slide ("just memory").

### Working together: micturition
(Transcript "nutrition"; [Slides] "Neural control of micturition".)
- **Bladder (detrusor):**
  - **Sympathetic: inhibits bladder contraction**, so the bladder fills. High sympathetic tone: "you don't go to the loo" (you don't stop when running).
  - **Parasympathetic: bladder contraction**: "perfect moment to go to the toilet".
- **Urethral sphincter:** a **sphincter** is a mainly circular muscle that contracts a tube and decreases its diameter. The sphincter is **contracted by the sympathetic**.
- **To urinate:** **activate the parasympathetic** (contract the bladder) **and inhibit the sympathetic** (relax the sphincter).
- **Filling:** he then said "activation of the parasympathetic in order to contract the sphincter and activation of the sympathetic in order to fill up the bladder". **[check]** This contradicts his own statement a moment earlier (sphincter contracted by the sympathetic) and [[FMF ANS 2]] (the sympathetic closes the sphincters). See the micturition slide.
- Point: opposite effects do not mean they are always opposites; often they must work together. Also in sexual function ("you cannot imagine the amount of input from one and the other") and **pupil** contraction and dilation (both always present; details later from another lecturer).

## 9. ANS tone
- Distribution even or uneven; the **effect** can also be even or uneven. This is **ANS tone**: some organs have more sympathetic, some more parasympathetic tone, always compensating ([Slides] "Some organs have uneven autonomic effect").

### Heart: parasympathetic tone
- Both inputs: sympathetic gives **tachycardia** and hypertension; parasympathetic mainly **bradycardia**, via the **vagus nerve**.
- The parasympathetic effect is greater: **"parasympathetic tone in the heart"**.
- **Clinical example, heart transplantation:** the vessels are sutured and the heart works as a pump with its **own automatism** (explained later), but the nerves are cut and cannot be sutured. A transplanted heart beats at **about 100 bpm instead of about 60 to 70**, always. Reason: the **parasympathetic tone is lost** (vagal fibres cut); the sympathetic is cut too, but since the parasympathetic dominated, the denervated heart is "always tachycardic". [Slides] "Automatism: 100 bpm; denervated heart!" (Transcript says "if you cut both, you get to have a heart attack. It's always tachycardic": "heart attack" is probably a transcription error **[verify]**.)

### Blood vessels: sympathetic tone
- He first said vessels receive both, then corrected to "mainly sympathetic tone" to keep it simple. **[check]** [Slides]: **"Only sympathetic"**. The slides decide: only sympathetic. (Consistent with [[FMF ANS 2]]: the sympathetic is the only system innervating blood vessels.)
- Vessels are **normally contracted**, by the sympathetic nerves directly and by **circulating catecholamines** from the adrenal medulla ([Slides] "circulating catecholamines + S-ANS"; "can increase contraction").
- A figure value of blood flow "roughly 200 millilitres" was mentioned **[verify]**: see the slide.

## 10. Cardiac output
- **Cardiac output** = litres of blood leaving the heart per minute = blood ejected per heartbeat x beats per minute.
- Normally about the same amount is ejected each beat; with **bradycardia** there are fewer beats, so **lower cardiac output**, less blood reaching the organs. The first organ to suffer: the **brain**.

## 11. Clinical case: vasovagal syncope
- Typical triggers mentioned: standing too long, heat, thirst or dehydration; seeing blood in the operating theatre ("there's always one or two every year"). Part of the definition is garbled **[verify]**.
- Mechanism: **increased parasympathetic (vagal) activity** -> **bradycardia** -> **lower cardiac output** -> **less cerebral perfusion** (less oxygen supply to the brain) -> **loss of consciousness**.
- **Treatment:** a student suggested acetylcholine: **no**, the parasympathetic tone is already increased, so you do not give something that activates it more. Other options were mentioned but are garbled ("acetylglymesterase", "LB and EFB" **[verify]**). The simple one: **atropine**, the **vagolytic** mentioned before, which stops the effect of the vagus. Other approaches: later, with the heart.
- [Lucía] (ANS TBL, see [[FMF ANS 2]] section 9): reflex bradycardia via the vagus and **M2** receptors is treated with muscarinic antagonists such as atropine.
