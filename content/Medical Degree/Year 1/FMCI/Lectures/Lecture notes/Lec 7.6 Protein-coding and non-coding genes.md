## 1. What is a gene?
- Classical definition (a student's answer, and what he would have given about 20 years ago): **a section of DNA that codes for a protein**.
- Largely thanks to the human genome sequence, many genes have been found that do not meet this definition. Two main classes are now recognized:
  - **Protein-coding genes** (the classical gene).
  - **Non-coding RNA genes**: they make an RNA that is not translated; the functional molecule is the RNA itself. This whole class was unknown when he was a student; "probably many more surprises" remain.
- Current definition (lecturer): **a gene is a stretch of DNA that makes a functional RNA.** Its boundaries are the start and the end of transcription, and it includes exons and introns.
- [Lucía] "A gene is a sequence of DNA that is used to make a messenger." Enhancers do not make RNA, so they are part of the DNA but are not genes.
- [Notes] Even the gene count is uncertain: some genes make both coding and non-coding transcripts, and some "lncRNA" genes turn out to have short coding sequences that make micropeptides.

## 2. Structure of a typical protein-coding gene
- **Transcription.** RNA polymerase II (a protein complex) binds a region of the genome, opens the double strand and moves along, using the **bottom strand as template** to make an RNA complementary to it.
  - The RNA therefore has **the same sequence as the top strand**, with **U instead of T**. Flagged "very important".
- **Start of the gene** = the first nucleotide transcribed = the **TSS (transcription start site)**, drawn as an arrow. By definition it is also the start of **exon 1**.
- **End of the gene** = the last nucleotide of the RNA, where the polymerase complex disassembles and transcription stops.
- **Splicing.** The long primary RNA is cut; some segments are degraded and the rest are joined ("spliced") into a shorter mRNA.
  - **Exons** = the segments spliced together. **Introns** = the segments removed between them.
  - The gene in the genome = all exons + all introns.
  - The mRNA exits the nucleus and is translated in the cytoplasm.
  - [Lucía] The unspliced RNA is the **primary transcript**; spliceosomes cut it exactly at exon-intron boundaries; the mature mRNA has a 5' cap.
- **Translation.** The ribosome scans the mRNA until it finds **AUG** (methionine), the initiation codon; methionine is always the first amino acid. The AUG is usually somewhere in exon 1.
  - Stop codons are in the final exon. [Lucía] **UAA, UAG, UGA** (the transcript is garbled on the third).
- **CDS (coding sequence)** = from start codon to stop codon; the only part that codes for amino acids.
- **UTRs (untranslated regions)** = transcribed into the mRNA but not translated: **5' UTR** (before the start codon, in exon 1) and **3' UTR** (after the stop codon, in the last exon). [Notes] Shown in orange in Figure 1.

## 4. Alternative splicing
- Far more transcripts than genes. The only explanation is that **one gene makes several transcripts**, by **alternative splicing**: using different combinations of exons (e.g. exons 1, 2, 3 in one transcript; exons 1, 3, 4 in another).
- Lecturer: on average **about 10 transcripts (isoforms) per protein-coding gene**, so 20,000 genes give about 200,000 RNA molecules.
- Advantage (lecturer): economy of genome space. One gene per RNA would need a huge genome; this saves space and energy and is more efficient.
- [Lucía] Each transcript codes for a slightly different protein.

## 5. Non-coding RNA genes
- Some examples were known long ago but thought to be exceptions; then more and more were found. "A big discovery"; very fashionable now.
- Divided by size, because the functions differ. The threshold is arbitrary: **200 nucleotides**.

| Class | Size | Lecturer's examples and functions | [Notes] Table 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short ncRNA | Up to 200 nt | Many already known. snRNAs (splicing), tRNAs (translation), microRNAs (later class). Mostly work inside the nucleus | snRNA (splicing); snoRNA (rRNA maturation); scaRNA (snRNA maturation); tRNA (protein synthesis); miRNA (regulation of target genes by base pairing); piRNA and siRNA (genome defense, limiting transposons in germ cells) |
| Long ncRNA (lncRNA) | Over 200 nt | The new class nobody suspected. Transcribed in the nucleus, spliced; can stay in the nucleus or go to the cytoplasm; not translated. Usually regulate other (protein-coding) genes, activating or silencing them. Some help make chromatin loops. Important in cancer and many processes | Nuclear lncRNA: regulate target genes by modifying chromatin or by transcriptional regulation (includes antisense transcripts). Cytoplasmic lncRNA: some regulate translation, protein localization or activity |

- [Notes] The division is "extremely arbitrary": it would split rRNAs (28S, 18S long; 5.8S, 5S short), and many important regulators are small. Many lncRNAs have a 5' cap and poly(A) tail like mRNAs, but others do not.

### The tissue-expression experiment
- Cell lines representing **14 tissues** (brain, liver, lung, spleen, pancreas, stomach...). For each gene, count in how many of the 14 it is expressed; plot how many genes are expressed in 1, 2, ... 14 cell types.
- Red = protein-coding; green and blue (including "intergenic novel") = lncRNA.
- **Result:** most protein-coding genes are expressed in all cell types, very few in only one. lncRNA genes are the opposite: most are expressed in just one cell type.
- Take-home message (lecturer): **lncRNA genes are very tissue-specific**; protein-coding genes are usually expressed in several tissues. lncRNAs are also expressed at much lower levels.
- Background: a neuron and a skin cell differ because, during differentiation, they activated and silenced different sets of genes.

## 7. Pseudogenes
- "False genes": **copies of genes that have been inactivated** (degraded by mutations, mostly inactive). Symbol: Greek letter psi.
- The catalogue lists three types: unitary, processed and non-processed. About 10,000 processed and about 4,000 to 5,000 non-processed (lecturer). [Notes] Processed account for nearly three-quarters, non-processed for nearly one-quarter.

| Type | Origin (lecturer) | [Notes] |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-processed** | **Duplication** of a stretch of DNA during evolution. A copy of the gene, usually close to the original, inactivated by mutations | Direct copies made at the DNA level (e.g. tandem duplication). A full-length one keeps introns and nearby regulatory sequences such as the promoter |
| **Processed** | Copy of the **mRNA** (already spliced) inserted elsewhere in the genome by the proteins encoded by **LINEs** (retrotransposons), which sometimes take the wrong mRNA from the cytoplasm. Then inactivated by mutation or simply not expressed | "Retropseudogenes": copies of processed transcripts converted into cDNA by the LINE-1 machinery. Lack the promoter, regulatory sequences and introns, so usually silent |

- Why the names: "processed" because they come from a processed (matured, spliced) mRNA; the others are therefore "non-processed".
- The retrotransposon mechanism was the answer a student gave from the previous class (7.5, not recorded).
- Pseudogenes matter because some are expressed or have other functions; to be discussed later.
- [Notes] Not all pseudogenes are functionless; some are transcribed. A cDNA copy that acquires regulatory sequences and is useful can be kept by selection as a functional **retrogene** (Box 1 Figure 1).
- [Notes] Olfactory receptor family: close to 1,000 genes, the majority of the sequences inactive.
