## What the lecturer wants you to know
- The **overall process** of synthesis (4 phases, locations), not every reaction or carbon count.
- The **HMG-CoA reductase** reaction in detail: it is the regulated step and the target of statins.
- Regulation of HMG-CoA reductase (long and short term).
- Concepts over numbers: cholesterol synthesis is very costly.

## 1. Introduction
- Cholesterol "is not bad, it is not good, it is essential". Too much or too little is harmful; the amount must be tightly controlled.
- Functions: membrane component; precursor of many molecules (bile acids, steroid hormones, vitamin D).
- At the molecular level there is only one cholesterol molecule; there is no "good" or "bad" cholesterol.
- **[Lucía]** Mostly hydrophobic with a very small hydrophilic area; in membranes it regulates fluidity and stability. Two sources: **diet** (absorbed by enterocytes) and **de novo synthesis**, mainly in the liver.
- **[Notes]** Structure: four fused rings (cyclopentaneperhydrophenanthrene, "steran"), a small polar OH head and a large non-polar region. Synthesis mostly in liver and **intestine**; also adrenal cortex and gonads (for steroid hormones). An adult on a low-cholesterol diet can make close to 1 g/day.
- **[Notes]** Absorption: enterocytes take up dietary cholesterol mainly through **NPC1L1**; **ABCG5/ABCG8** pumps sterols back into the lumen. **Ezetimibe** inhibits NPC1L1. The ring structure cannot be metabolized, so elimination is hard: faecal excretion of bile acids and biliary cholesterol is the main route.
- Importance: many Nobel Prizes of the 20th century relate to cholesterol (synthesis, metabolism, structure, steroid hormones). Statins were the most profitable drug class in history (over 10 billion dollars per year for one early statin).

## 2. Cholesterol synthesis (overview)
All 27 carbons come from **acetyl-CoA** (acetate, 2 C). Mainly in **hepatocytes**. Acetyl-CoA is made in mitochondria and must be **exported to the cytosol** (as citrate, via the citrate transporter: see [[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]], section 3).

| Phase | Reaction | Product | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Condensation of **3 acetyl-CoA** (3 reactions) | **Mevalonate** (6 C) | Cytosol |
| 2 | Phosphorylation (from ATP) and **decarboxylation** (loses 1 C as CO2) | **Activated isoprenes** (5 C, with 2 phosphate groups; two types) | Cytosol |
| 3 | Condensation of **6 isoprene units** (6 x 5 C) | **Squalene** (30 C, linear) | Cytosol, then to ER |
| 4 | Cyclization, oxidations, loss of 3 carbons, many reactions | **Cholesterol** (27 C) | Mostly **smooth ER** |

- Phase 1 resembles ketone body synthesis (also a condensation of acetyl-CoA units).
- Isoprene units: the pyrophosphate groups are released as inorganic phosphate during condensation; their hydrolysis supplies the energy for condensation.
- **[Notes]** The two activated isoprenes are interconvertible isomers: **isopentenyl diphosphate** and **dimethylallyl diphosphate**; making them uses 3 ATP per mevalonate. The last step to squalene is catalyzed by **squalene synthase** (ER). Squalene is converted to cholesterol in the ER by monooxygenases, cyclases, isomerases, reductases and others.
- **[Notes]** HMG-CoA reductase is an **integral membrane protein of the smooth ER** and its reaction is **irreversible**.
- **[Notes]** Other organisms: plants make phytosterols (sitosterol, stigmasterol), fungi make ergosterol.
- Squalene: the lecturer said it is named after the animal from whose liver it was first isolated. **[verify]** The transcript says "stars"; probably a mistranscription (the name suggests sharks, *Squalus*). Not covered in the slides or notes.
- The lecturer was not fully sure where squalene enters the ER; what is certain is "start in the cytosol, finish in the smooth ER".
- Steroid nucleus = the **four fused rings**; any molecule keeping this structure is a steroid.

### Cost
- Very costly: lots of ATP, lots of carbon (acetyl-CoA) and lots of reducing power (NADPH, which itself costs energy to make).
- Real cost is higher than the slide numbers because acetyl-CoA must be exported from the mitochondrion.
- **[check]** The sources disagree on cost. **Slides: 18 acetyl-CoA, 18 ATP, 16 NADPH.** Study notes: 18 ATP directly (activation of six mevalonates), higher estimates if acetyl-CoA supply is counted. Lucía: 18 acetyl-CoA, **36** ATP, 16 NADPH. Learn the slide numbers. Cholesterol is also not easily (or safely) eliminated, another reason for tight regulation.

## 3. The key reaction: HMG-CoA reductase
Last reaction of phase 1.

**HMG-CoA + 2 NADPH + 2 H+ to mevalonate + CoA-SH + 2 NADP+**

- HMG-CoA = **3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA**: glutarate (5 C, two carboxyl groups, as in alpha-ketoglutarate) with a methyl and a hydroxyl on C3, one carboxyl bound to CoA as a thioester.
- How to read it (compare substrate and product):
  1. CoA is removed.
  2. The thioester carboxyl becomes a **primary alcohol**: a **reduction in two steps** (carboxyl to aldehyde to alcohol).
  3. The electrons come from **2 NADPH**, the typical electron carrier for biosynthesis, produced mainly by the **pentose phosphate pathway** (important in lipid-synthesizing tissues).
- Enzyme name = substrate + reaction: HMG-CoA **reductase**.
- It is the **rate-limiting, regulated step** of cholesterol synthesis.

## 4. Statins
- Statins are structurally similar to mevalonate/HMG-CoA, bind the active site of HMG-CoA reductase and act as **competitive inhibitors**.
- Result: less cholesterol synthesis, used in patients with high cholesterol.
- Discovery (Japan, 1970s): a group screened more than 6,000 fungal products for inhibitors of cholesterol synthesis and found one in a soil fungus related to the mould used for blue cheese (*Penicillium*). Modern statins are modified versions with better stability and specificity.
- **[check]** The transcript gives the first statin as "Novastatin". **[Slides]** In the early 1970s **Akira Endo** and colleagues screened 6,000 microbial strains; *Penicillium citrinum* (a relative of the blue-cheese fungus) yielded **mevastatin**, the first cholesterol-lowering compound identified.
- **[Lucía]** / **[Notes]** Statins (natural fungal products and synthetic derivatives) are **competitive** inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase; they also reduce other mevalonate-derived compounds.
- Lecturer's message: there are not "natural" versus "pharmaceutical" medicines, only medicine that works (with evidence) and medicine that does not. The most profitable drug in history is a natural product.

## 5. Regulation of HMG-CoA reductase
Needed because excess cholesterol precipitates in vessels, forming plaques that impair circulation, and because synthesis is energetically expensive.

### Long-term (adaptive, takes time)
- Depends on dietary cholesterol. High dietary cholesterol: less synthesis. Low dietary cholesterol: the body makes more.
- Mechanism: changes in **transcription** of the reductase gene (more mRNA, more protein) and in **degradation** of the enzyme.
- **[check]** The transcript says the hepatocyte "levels of glucose" reflect the diet; from context, and as in Lucía's notes, he means **cholesterol**.
- **[Notes]** **Transcription**: controlled by **SREBPs** (ER integral proteins). When ER cholesterol falls, the SREBP-SCAP complex moves to the Golgi, SREBP is cleaved, and its DNA-binding domain goes to the nucleus and binds **sterol regulatory elements (SRE)** of genes such as HMG-CoA reductase.
- **[Notes]** **Degradation**: the reductase has sterol-sensing regions; high sterols trigger ubiquitination and proteasome degradation.

### Short-term (fast): phosphorylation
- First question to ask with any phosphorylation: which form is active? There is no fixed rule.
- Here: **phosphorylated = inactive**, dephosphorylated = active.
- **AMPK** (AMP-activated protein kinase) phosphorylates and **inactivates** the reductase. **[check]** The transcript says "when the kinase is active, you have an activation of the enzyme"; it is inactivation (Lucía: the kinase phosphorylates = inhibition).
- **Phosphatases** remove the phosphate and reactivate it.

| Signal | When | Effect | Cholesterol synthesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Insulin** | High glucose (after a meal) | Activates phosphatases, inhibits the kinase | **On** (plenty of energy) |
| **Glucagon** | Low glucose | Inhibits phosphatases | **Off** (save energy for glucose production) |
| **High AMP** (low ATP) | Low energy | Activates AMPK | **Off** |

**[check]** The transcript says "level of ALT" and "AFP activates the kinase"; this is **AMP** (Lucía: high AMP signals low energy and inhibits cholesterol synthesis).

## 6. Fates of cholesterol made in the liver
1. **Bile acids**: a large fraction of cholesterol.
2. **Free cholesterol secreted in bile** into the intestine: the **main (and inefficient) route of elimination**. Humans are very poor at eliminating cholesterol, which is why it accumulates.
3. **Esterification**: the 3-OH of cholesterol forms an ester with a fatty acid (**cholesteryl ester**), making it even more hydrophobic. Done for **storage** in the liver (lipid droplets) or **export** in lipoproteins (VLDL); free cholesterol is not released into blood because it is insoluble. **[Slides]** / **[Notes]** Enzyme: **ACAT** (acyl-CoA:cholesterol acyltransferase), transferring a fatty acid from CoA to the C3 hydroxyl.
4. **Delivery to other tissues** for membranes and hormone synthesis. The liver is the main producer and distributor.

### Bile acids and enterohepatic circulation
- Bile acids are **steroids** (keep the four-ring nucleus) with extra hydroxylations; different hydroxylation patterns give different bile acids. Example: **cholic acid**. Be able to recognize one as a cholesterol derivative.
- Circulation: made in the liver, **stored in the gallbladder**, released into the intestine after a fatty meal, help digest (emulsify) fat, then **most are reabsorbed** and return to the liver; only a small fraction is lost in faeces. Recycling matters because they are expensive to make.
- **[check]** The transcript says bile acids are "stored in the whole blood"; Lucía's notes: stored in the **gallbladder**.
- Free cholesterol in bile follows the same circulation; the fraction not reabsorbed is eliminated.
- Some cholesterol-lowering drugs target this step, reducing reabsorption and increasing elimination.
- **[Notes]** Most abundant human bile acids: **cholic** and **chenodeoxycholic acid** (recognized by the carboxylic acid on the side chain). Most are conjugated with **glycine or taurine** to form bile salts (glycocholic, taurocholic acid).

## 7. Other cholesterol derivatives
- **Steroid hormones**: sex hormones, glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids (covered later in physiology). **[Notes]** All derive from cholesterol via **pregnenolone**. Corticosteroids (adrenal cortex): mineralocorticoids (ion balance, e.g. aldosterone) and glucocorticoids (carbohydrate metabolism, immunity, inflammation, e.g. cortisol). Sex hormones (gonads, placenta): androgens (testosterone), estrogens (estradiol, which have an aromatic ring), progestogens (progesterone). Androgens and estrogens lack the C17 side chain. Only a small fraction of cholesterol is used, since hormones act at low concentrations.
- **Vitamin D**: from **7-dehydrocholesterol** (a cholesterol precursor). **UV light** in the skin breaks one ring (B ring), so it is technically no longer a steroid. It is then modified further to the active form, which is important for bone formation and remodelling.
  - It is not strictly a vitamin because we can make it, but supplementation is sometimes needed.
  - **[check]** The transcript says "B-hydrocholesterol"; Lucía and the study notes: **7-dehydrocholesterol**, whose UV-broken bond gives **cholecalciferol (vitamin D3)**.
  - **[Notes]** The ring opens between **C9 and C10**; strictly a prehormone, not a vitamin or a steroid. Activation by two cytochrome P450 mixed-function oxidases: **C25 in the liver**, then **C1 in the kidney**, giving **calcitriol** (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol), which promotes intestinal Ca2+ and phosphate absorption. Deficiency: **rickets** in children, **osteomalacia** in adults.
- **[Notes]** **Isoprenoids** from isopentenyl diphosphate: dolichol (N-glycosylation), ubiquinone (CoQ10, [[Lec 5.7 Oxidative phosphorylation]]), and **prenylation** of proteins, e.g. **Ras** anchored by a farnesyl group ([[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]]); drugs blocking Ras prenylation have had little success.

## 8. Lipid transport: lipoproteins (recording cut mid-section)
Lipids are insoluble in water, so cholesterol and triacylglycerols cannot circulate free; they travel in **lipoproteins**.

### Structure
- **Surface** (polar parts facing the blood): **apolipoproteins**, **free cholesterol** (OH facing out), **phospholipids** (phosphate heads facing out).
- **Core** (non-polar): **triacylglycerols** and **cholesteryl esters**.

### Classes (by density)
| Lipoprotein | Size | Protein content | Density |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chylomicrons | Largest | Lowest | Lowest |
| VLDL | | | Very low |
| LDL | | | Low |
| HDL | Smallest | Highest | Highest |

Lipid is less dense than protein, so more protein means higher density.

### Chylomicrons
- Made in **enterocytes** from digested dietary fat, packaged with protein, released into circulation.
- After a high-fat meal, plasma is **turbid** (milky) from circulating chylomicrons; a fasting sample is clear.
- **[check]** The transcript says diacylglycerols are packed into chylomicrons. The 5.9 study notes ([[Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies]]) say fatty acids and glycerol are **reconverted into TAG** in the enterocyte, and TAG with dietary cholesterol and proteins form chylomicrons. **[Notes, 5.9]** At target tissues apoC-II activates **lipoprotein lipase**, which hydrolyzes the TAG again.

The recording ends here. **[Lucía]** for the rest:
- **Chylomicrons** reach the liver or other tissues; what the body does not use arrives at the liver.
- **VLDL**: made in the **liver**; releases fatty acids to other tissues and, once depleted, becomes the cholesterol-rich **LDL**.
- **LDL**: the liver's way of delivering cholesterol to other cells. **ApoB-100** recognizes the LDL receptor; LDL is internalized in clathrin-coated vesicles (review endocytosis, Lec 3.4). Defects in the receptor cause **familial hypercholesterolemia**.
- **HDL**: removes cholesterol from tissues and returns it to the liver. Lucía says it is produced in the intestine. **[verify]** origin in the lipoprotein chapter (not yet read).
- **[Slides]** LDL uptake by clathrin-mediated endocytosis (Anderson, Brown and Goldstein, *Cell* 1977; Nobel Prize).
