## 1. Why oxidative phosphorylation matters
- We use roughly our **body weight in ATP per day** but store very little (about 250 g).
- This is only possible because ADP is **continuously rephosphorylated** to ATP using the energy of electron transport.
- **[Notes]** Humans use about **83 kg of ATP per day** against stores of about **250 g**; each ATP is recycled about **300 times a day**. Oxidative phosphorylation is the culmination of cellular respiration: an electron-motive force (NADH, FADH2) is converted into a proton-motive force, which drives ATP synthesis.

## 2. Chemiosmotic theory (Mitchell)
1. Electrons from **NADH** or **FADH2** pass through carriers organized as **complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane**, ending at **oxygen**.
2. At some points the energy of these redox reactions is used to **pump protons** from the matrix to the intermembrane space.
3. This creates a **proton gradient**: an electron flow is converted into a proton gradient.
4. Protons flow back through the "fifth component", **ATP synthase**, which uses the energy of the gradient to make ATP.

**[verify]** The lecturer dated the theory to the 1970s; neither the slides nor the study notes give a date.

**[Slides]** ADP + Pi to ATP is highly unfavourable; ADP is not phosphorylated by a direct reaction with a high-energy phosphate carrier, but by the flow of protons down their electrochemical gradient. The intermembrane space has the higher H+ concentration (lower pH), the matrix the lower; cristae increase the inner membrane surface.

### Requirements
- **Intact, impermeable inner mitochondrial membrane.** If holes or channels let protons back in, the gradient dissipates without passing through ATP synthase and no ATP is made (basis of uncoupling, section 7).
- **Proton pumps**: three complexes of the chain pump protons.
- **ATP synthase**, which uses the gradient.

## 3. Electron carriers
| Carrier | Key features |
|---|---|
| NAD+, FAD | Electron donors to the chain (structures already known) |
| **Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone)** | Carries electrons **one at a time** (quinone to semiquinone to ubiquinol). **Lipid-soluble**: moves within the inner membrane and **connects complexes** (I/II to III). Not a cofactor: it is itself a mobile carrier/connector. |
| **Iron-sulfur clusters** | Proteins with Fe coordinated to S from cysteine side chains; Fe carries electrons |
| **Cytochromes** | Proteins with a **heme** (porphyrin) group. Cytochromes b, c, etc. differ in the protein and in side groups on the porphyrin |
| **Cytochrome c** | Small protein with a heme; second mobile **connector** (III to IV) |
| **Oxygen** | Final acceptor, reduced to water |

- **[check]** The lecturer said cytochrome c is overall hydrophobic and moves in the inner membrane. **[Slides]** Cytochrome c is not part of a complex; it moves between complexes III and IV **as a freely soluble protein** (Lucía and the study notes agree). Ubiquinone is the **lipid-soluble** carrier inside the membrane. Both are connectors.
- **[Lucía]** Complex I contains **FMN** and Fe-S centres as cofactors. Fe-S clusters are one-electron carriers; there are three cytochrome types in mitochondria (a, b, c).
- **[Slides]** Lehninger Table 19-3: I (NADH dehydrogenase; FMN, Fe-S; 43 subunits), II (succinate dehydrogenase; FAD, Fe-S), III (hemes, Fe-S; reduces two cytochrome c per QH2; the **Q cycle** moves 4 H+), cytochrome c (heme), IV (hemes and **Cu centres**). Cytochromes and Fe-S clusters are one-electron carriers; ubiquinone takes two electrons and two protons to become ubiquinol.
- Aside: anti-ageing creams contain coenzyme Q because it can capture free electrons and lower oxidative stress in the skin. Oxidative stress lowers the activity of **prolyl hydroxylase**, which adds OH groups to proline in collagen; these OH groups form hydrogen bonds that give collagen fibres their strength.

## 4. The four complexes
Each complex is named after its enzymatic activity.

| Complex | Name | Electrons from, to | Protons pumped (per electron pair) | Coupling site? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | **NADH dehydrogenase** | NADH to CoQ | **4** | Yes |
| II | **Succinate dehydrogenase** | Succinate (via FADH2) to CoQ | **0** | **No** |
| III | **Ubiquinone:cytochrome c oxidoreductase** | CoQH2 to cytochrome c | **4** | Yes |
| IV | **Cytochrome oxidase** | Cytochrome c to O2 (water) | **2** | Yes |

- NADH comes from the Krebs cycle, PDH, beta-oxidation and other reactions.
- Complex II contains succinate dehydrogenase, the **only Krebs cycle enzyme bound to the inner membrane** rather than free in the matrix. It oxidizes succinate to fumarate, reducing FAD to FADH2.
- **Coupling site** = a point where electron transfer is coupled to proton pumping. Complex II is **not** a coupling site: "really important".
- Student question: complex I needs protons to make CoQH2, so how is that different from pumping? Protons are present everywhere for reduction; pumping is moving them against the gradient, which only happens in complexes I, III and IV.

### Entry points ("doors") for electrons
- Complex I (NADH) or complex II (succinate/FADH2). Both end at CoQ.
- Also directly to CoQ: **acyl-CoA dehydrogenase** of beta-oxidation (FADH2) and the **glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle** (section 10). **[Lucía]** Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase passes its electrons via the **electron transfer flavoprotein (ETF)**.
- **[Lucía]** Oxidative phosphorylation is mainly regulated by the **availability of ADP**.

### Yield
| Entry | Protons pumped | ATP |
|---|---|---|
| NADH (complex I) | 4 + 4 + 2 = **10** | **2.5** |
| FADH2 (complex II) | 4 + 2 = **6** | **1.5** |

More protons pumped means more gradient and more ATP.

## 6. Oxidative stress
- **Main source of reactive oxygen species (ROS): the electron transport chain itself.** Electron leakage forms **superoxide** and other ROS.
- Mechanism of damage: ROS are radicals, highly reactive; they react with nearby molecules, creating more radicals in a chain:
  - **Membrane lipids**: change their properties. **[verify]** He said unsaturated lipids are turned into saturated ones; the study notes only say ROS damage membrane lipids.
  - **Proteins**: oxidize amino acid side chains, changing structure and enzyme activity.
  - **[Notes]** Superoxide (O2 + e- to O2.-) can generate even more reactive hydroxyl radicals (.OH), which damage enzymes, membrane lipids and **nucleic acids**.
- Linked to **ageing**, cardiovascular and arterial disease, and neurodegeneration.

### Defences
- Enzymes that inactivate ROS. Peroxides derived from superoxide are reduced using **glutathione**, which becomes oxidized (a disulfide bridge between two glutathione molecules).
- **Glutathione reductase** uses **NADPH** to break that disulfide bridge and regenerate reduced glutathione.
- NADPH comes from the **pentose phosphate pathway**.
- **[Notes]** Sequence: **superoxide dismutase (SOD)**: 2 O2.- + 2 H+ to H2O2 + O2; then **glutathione peroxidase** neutralizes H2O2 using glutathione (GSH, the tripeptide Glu-Cys-Gly), whose SH groups reduce it to water, forming GS-SG; **glutathione reductase** uses NADPH to regenerate 2 GSH. GSH also keeps protein -SH groups reduced.
- **[Lucía]** Antioxidants also help.
- **[Notes]** / **[Lucía]** In **hypoxia**, electrons keep entering the chain but cannot reach O2, so ROS rise. Cells respond through **HIF1** (hypoxia-inducible factor 1): it inhibits PDH (via PDH kinase, Lucía) and replaces complex IV with a form more effective in hypoxia (study notes); Lucía adds increased glucose transporters, glycolytic enzymes and lactate dehydrogenase.
- **[Notes]** Also in hypoxia (heart attack, stroke) the gradient disappears and ATP synthase could run backwards as an ATPase, draining ATP. The fall in pH (lactate) makes the inhibitor protein **IF1** dimerize, blocking ATP hydrolysis.

### Ageing
- With age, the activity of these enzymes and of the pentose phosphate pathway falls, so less NADPH and less capacity to control oxidative stress. More damage then further impairs mitochondria: a vicious circle ("pez que se muerde la cola").
- Age is a main risk factor for vascular and neurodegenerative diseases; oxidative stress is one reason, not the only one. Healthy habits reduce the risk.

## 7. Coupled vs uncoupled mitochondria (likely exam question)
- **Coupled**: electron transport (respiration, O2 consumption) drives ATP synthesis.
- **Uncoupled**: electrons are transported and **O2 is consumed**, but **little or no ATP is made** because the proton gradient is dissipated.

### Experiment (isolated mitochondria, e.g. from bovine heart; O2 consumption and ATP synthesis measured)
| Condition | O2 consumption | ATP synthesis | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buffer only | No | No | No electron donor |
| + Succinate (+ ADP, Pi) | Yes | Yes | **Coupled** |
| + Cyanide | No | No | Electron transport blocked. **NOT uncoupled.** |
| + 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) | Yes | No | **Uncoupled** |

- Cyanide result supports Mitchell's theory: no electron transport, no proton pumping, no ATP. A common mistake is to call these mitochondria uncoupled; they are simply inhibited.
- **DNP**: a lipid-soluble weak acid that picks up protons in the intermembrane space and carries them across the inner membrane, bypassing ATP synthase. The lecturer called it a poison ("venom"). Lucía's notes and the study notes give the same experiment.
- **[Notes]** The reverse also holds: blocking ATP synthase with **oligomycin** or **venturicidin** stops electron transport (no O2 consumption).

### Physiological uncoupling: brown fat
- Brown adipocytes express **UCP1 (uncoupling protein 1, thermogenin)**, a **proton channel** in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Protons re-enter the matrix through it, so energy is released as **heat** instead of ATP.
- Newborns have brown fat (e.g. at the back of the neck) because their hypothalamic temperature control is still immature.
- Brown fat decreases with age; the lecturer said some muscle groups contain a little brown fat and that it can be trained, with a genetic component related to physical performance.
- He also said cold-climate animals (he gave whales and birds as examples) have lots of brown fat.
- **[Slides]** Brown adipose tissue mitochondria contain large amounts of UCP (thermogenin), which is **activated by fatty acids**.
- **[verify]** The claims about brown fat in adult muscle and in whales and birds; not covered in the slides or study notes.

## 8. ATP synthase (F0F1)
"The most perfect molecular mechanism we have", a molecular machine.

### Structure
- **F0**: in the inner membrane, works as a **proton channel**.
- **F1**: the "mushroom head", facing the **matrix**. Made of **3 alpha + 3 beta** subunits, alternating. The **beta** subunits hold the **active sites** (bind ADP and Pi).
- **Gamma subunit**: central shaft connecting F0 and F1. It is **asymmetric**, so as it turns it contacts each beta subunit differently.

### Binding-change mechanism (rotational catalysis)
Each beta subunit cycles through three conformations:
| Conformation | Affinity | Event |
|---|---|---|
| **Open (O)** | None for ADP or ATP | Releases ATP |
| **Loose (L)** | Binds ADP + Pi | Takes up substrates |
| **Tight (T)** | Very high | Forms the anhydride bond: ATP made |

1. **3 protons** pass through F0.
2. The gamma subunit **rotates**, changing its contacts with the beta subunits.
3. Each beta subunit changes conformation: T to O (ATP released), O to L (substrates bound), L to T (ATP formed).
4. The next 3 protons repeat the cycle.

- Analogy: a **hydroelectric turbine**. Water (protons) flows through a tube (F0) and turns a turbine (gamma) that generates electricity (ATP).
- What drives rotation: the passage of protons through F0. So more gradient means more ATP. In uncoupled mitochondria, protons pass through other structures (thermogenin), not F0.
- **[check]** The names of the middle state differ between sources: the **slides** say **open, loose, tight**; the **study notes** say open, **light**, tight (so the transcript's "light" follows the study notes). Use "loose" as on the slides. "Time" in the transcript is "tight". States: open = empty/low affinity, releases ATP; loose = binds ADP + Pi; tight = makes and binds ATP.
- **[Notes]** Model proposed by **Paul Boyer**: **rotational catalysis**. F1 composition: **alpha3 beta3 gamma delta epsilon**. Each 120 degree turn of gamma meets a different beta subunit.
- **[Notes]** About **4 H+ per ATP** (3 through ATP synthase + 1 for transporting Pi, ADP and ATP across the inner membrane). With 10 H+ per NADH and 6 per FADH2, that gives **2.5 and 1.5 ATP**. The lecturer, Lucía and the slides say 3 protons fuel one ATP synthesis; the 4 includes the transport proton.

## 9. Brain and ATP per glucose (link to section 10)
Brain oxidation of glucose gives about **30 ATP instead of 32**, because neurons use the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle for cytosolic NADH.

**[Slides]** Lehninger Table 19-5, ATP per glucose: glycolysis 2 ATP + 2 cytosolic NADH (3 or 5 ATP depending on the shuttle); pyruvate oxidation 2 NADH (5); citric acid cycle 6 NADH (15), 2 FADH2 (3), 2 GTP/ATP (2). Total **30 or 32**.

## 10. Shuttles for cytosolic NADH (self-study, not the most important part)
- Most NADH is made in the matrix, but glycolysis makes some in the cytosol (at **glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase**, GAPDH in Lucía's glycolysis notes). **[check]** The transcript says "glycerol-3 dehydrogenase" here; for glycolytic NADH it means GAPDH.
- Without oxygen, NAD+ is regenerated by reducing pyruvate to lactate. With oxygen, the electrons are better used to make ATP, but there is **no NADH transporter** in the inner membrane, so a shuttle is needed.

### Malate-aspartate shuttle (yields NADH in the matrix, 2.5 ATP; Lucía: liver, kidney and heart)
1. Cytosol: oxaloacetate + NADH to **malate** (cytosolic malate dehydrogenase).
2. Malate enters the matrix through a malate transporter.
3. Matrix: malate to oxaloacetate, **regenerating NADH** (mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase, as in Krebs). "We have tricked the system."
4. Oxaloacetate must return: transaminated with glutamate to **aspartate**, which leaves through an aspartate transporter.
5. Cytosol: aspartate gives its amino group to alpha-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate and **oxaloacetate**, ready for the next NADH.

### Glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle (neurons; yields FADH2, 1.5 ATP)
1. Cytosol: NADH reduces **dihydroxyacetone phosphate** to **glycerol 3-phosphate** (cytosolic glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase).
2. On the outer face of the inner membrane, a second isoform oxidizes glycerol 3-phosphate back to DHAP using **FAD** (not NAD+), giving FADH2.
3. Electrons go straight to **CoQ**, bypassing complex I: 2 fewer ATP per glucose (2 cytosolic NADH x 1 ATP less each).
- **[check]** The transcript says the electrons enter at "complex 2"; Lucía and the study notes: this shuttle reduces **ubiquinone** directly (via G3PDH in the inner membrane), skipping complex I and one coupling centre (1 ATP less).
- **[Lucía]** / **[Notes]** This shuttle is used in **skeletal muscle and brain**; the malate-aspartate shuttle (the most active) in liver, kidney and heart.

## 11. Regulation **[Notes]**
Oxidative phosphorylation is regulated by **ADP availability**: when ATP use rises, ADP rises, which increases O2 consumption and ATP synthesis. **[Slides]** Mainly by substrate availability (NADH and ADP/Pi). **IF1** prevents ATP hydrolysis at low O2 (active only at low pH). Blocked oxphos makes NADH accumulate, which feeds back up to **PFK-1** in glycolysis.
