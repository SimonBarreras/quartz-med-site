[Slides] Learning goals:
- Formation of gametes requires a special type of cell division.
- Meiosis includes two segregation steps (and two cell divisions).
- In order to increase genetic diversity in the offspring, meiosis includes HOMOLOGOUS RECOMBINATION by which homologous chromosomes exchange segments of DNA.

## 1. Why a special division (recorded)
- Meiosis is a different type of cell division. **Mitosis** is the standard division for all somatic cells; every dividing cell in the body uses it, except the cells that will make the **gametes** (eggs in women, sperm in men).
- Why do gametes need a special division? (Student:) from a **diploid** cell we must generate **haploid** cells.
- He began explaining that a diploid cell has two sets of chromosomes, drawing one chromosome. **The recording ends here.**

## 2. Not recorded: overview (from [Notes], [Slides], [Lucía])
- [Notes] Diploid **primordial germ cells** migrate into the embryonic gonad and divide by mitosis to give **spermatogonia** (males) or **oogonia** (females); growth and differentiation give **primary spermatocytes** and **primary oocytes**, which undergo meiosis. Many more mitotic divisions in males than in females, likely contributing to sex differences in mutation rate.
- [Notes] Meiosis is a **reductive** division: **one round of DNA replication, two cell divisions (meiosis I and II)**, giving **four haploid cells**. Each gamete is designed to be genetically unique.
- [Slides] Ploidy and DNA content:

| Cell | Ploidy / DNA content |
|---|---|
| Gonium | 2n / 2C |
| After replication (S phase): primary oocyte / spermatocyte | 2n / 4C |
| After meiosis I: secondary oocyte / spermatocyte | n / 2C |
| After meiosis II: gamete | n / 1C |

- **[check]** [Notes] Figure 3 caption calls the products of meiosis I "two diploid cells" ("diploid secondary spermatocytes"); the slide scheme and the [Notes] main text (secondary gametocytes have the haploid number, n, with two chromatids per chromosome, 2C) say n/2C.

## 3. Two sources of diversity
- **Independent assortment** [Notes]: each diploid cell has two homologs of each chromosome (one paternal, one maternal; X and Y are the special case in males). For each of the 23 pairs the choice of daughter cell is independent: **2^23, about 8.4 x 10^6** (8,388,608) combinations from one meiosis (Figure 1). [Lucía] Bivalents are randomly oriented at metaphase I: 2^23 options.
- **Recombination** in prophase I superimposes more variation: each transmitted chromosome is a mosaic of paternal and maternal sequences (Figure 2).
- [Notes] The combined effect makes each gamete genetically unique.

## 4. Meiosis I: prophase I
[Notes] Homologs pair (**synapsis**) to form a **bivalent**; because DNA has already replicated, each bivalent has **four chromatids**. X and Y can also form a bivalent in male meiosis. Prophase I has five stages (Figure 4):

| Stage | Meaning | Events ([Notes]) |
|---|---|---|
| Leptotene | leptos = thin | Replicated chromosomes begin to condense, unpaired. **DSBs begin to form**; their repair using the homologous chromatid starts to bring homologs together. Chromatin in loops joined at the base by **cohesins**. |
| Zygotene | zygos = join | The **synaptonemal complex (SC)** forms between homologs: two lateral elements and a central element (proteins of the "SYPC" family, as written in the notes); lateral elements bind the cohesin axis. Homologs pair completely: synapsis into bivalents. |
| Pachytene | pachy = thick | Starts when the SC is complete. **Crossing over** becomes evident: physical breakage of DNA in one paternal and one maternal chromatid. Each connection point is a **chiasma** (plural chiasmata). About **55 chiasmata per cell in male** meiosis and about **90 in female**, mostly at recombination hotspots. |
| Diplotene | diplos = double | SC degraded; homologs separate slightly but stay joined at the chiasmata. |
| Diakinesis | dia = through, kinesis = move | Bivalents contract; nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear; spindle begins to form, leading into metaphase I. |

- [Slides] Figure label: "Homologs linked only by chiasmata".
- [Lucía] Chiasmata form during pachytene but are only seen in diplotene, when the chromosomes separate a little. **[check]** For diakinesis she writes "chiasmata disappear... and histones begin to form"; [Notes] say the bivalents contract and the spindle begins to form.

## 5. Homologous recombination at the molecular level
[Notes] (Figure 7, Figure 8):
1. **SPO11** (the main endonuclease in humans) makes a **double-strand break** at specific sites in one chromatid.
2. The **MNR** complex (**MRE11, NBS1, RAD50**), an exonuclease, digests the ends, leaving **3' overhangs**.
3. One 3' overhang **invades** a chromatid of the homologous chromosome, forming a **Holliday structure**.
4. A polymerase extends the 3' ends using the other chromatid as template (DNA synthesis). The complex includes **replication protein A** ("REPA" in the notes), **RAD54** and others such as **BRCA1 and BRCA2** (mutated in breast cancer).
5. The **double Holliday junction** is **resolved** by cutting two strands and religating to the opposite partner. Depending on how the junctions are cut: **crossover** (recombinant) or **non-crossover** (non-recombinant).
- **Gene conversion:** some new DNA may be copied from one chromatid to the other. Four possible outcomes: crossover with or without gene conversion; non-crossover with or without gene conversion.
- In meiosis the resolution complexes **favour crossovers**; in mitotic repair (HRR) non-crossovers are favoured.
- Recorded in Lec 7.8: "SPO11 creates double-strand breaks to initiate meiosis... the beginning of the process of homologous recombination"; the same process (MNR digestion, overhangs invading the other double strand, synthesis, two Holliday junctions, resolution) is reused in HRR with partly different proteins. Videos in the study material: "7.2 HR_Watson.mp4", "7.2 holliday model.mp4".

## 6. Meiosis I (rest) and meiosis II
- [Notes] **Metaphase I:** homologous pairs align at the equatorial plane; microtubules attach to each pair of centromeres. **Anaphase I:** each homolog of a pair goes to opposite poles (chiasmata separate). **Telophase I** and **cytokinesis:** two secondary gametocytes, haploid (n) with two chromatids per chromosome (2C).
- [Notes] **Meiosis II** resembles mitosis (prophase II to telophase II) with 23 chromosomes instead of 46; sister chromatids separate at anaphase II. Unlike in mitosis, the two chromatids are usually **genetically different** because of recombination in meiosis I. No DNA replication before meiosis II.
- [Lucía] At the end of meiosis I, the two haploid cells have duplicated material and enter the second division directly; telophase I nuclear membrane only partly forms.
- [Slides] Figure label: "Meiosis I anaphase".

## 7. Gametogenesis: male vs female
| | Male | Female |
|---|---|---|
| Divisions | Both symmetrical: **four** functional spermatozoa | Asymmetrical: **one** egg and polar bodies (discarded) |
| Meiosis I product | Two secondary spermatocytes | Large **secondary oocyte** + **first polar body** |
| Meiosis II product | Spermatids, then sperm | Mature egg + **second polar body** |
| Timing | From puberty, continuous to old age ([Notes], Lec 9.1 section) | Starts in fetal life |
| Arrests | None | **Prophase I (dictyotene)** until ovulation; **metaphase II** until fertilization |

- [Notes] Primary oocytes enter meiosis I during fetal development and arrest in a prolonged diplotene called the **dictyotene**. Each menstrual cycle the dominant follicle completes meiosis I just before ovulation, giving a secondary oocyte, which arrests in **metaphase II**; meiosis II is completed when a sperm penetrates the oocyte. Since ovulation continues to the fifth or sixth decade, meiosis I can be arrested for decades. The notes pose the question of the implications for the embryo's health (see maternal age effect in [[Lec 9.1 Cytogenetics and genome analysis]]: about 70% of Down syndrome cases from nondisjunction at maternal meiosis I).
- [Slides] "Arrested in Prophase I (Dictyotene)" and "Arrested in Metaphase II"; testis labels: spermatogonium (diploid), mitotic division, primary spermatocyte (in prophase of meiosis I), first meiotic division, secondary spermatocyte (haploid), second meiotic division, spermatids (haploid), sperm cells; Sertoli cell; seminiferous tubule, epididymis.
- [Lucía] Male meiosis takes **64 days** in the testis. Female meiosis starts around the **5th month** of gestation; oocytes arrest at diplotene of prophase I until puberty/ovulation; after fertilization the secondary oocyte divides, the second polar body is removed and the pronuclei fuse to form the zygote. Neither figure is in [Notes].

## 8. Mitosis vs meiosis
[Notes] Table 1:

| Characteristic | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Location | All tissues | Specialized germ-line cells in testis and ovary |
| Products | Diploid somatic cells | Haploid sperm and egg |
| Replication and division | One round of replication per division | One round of replication per two divisions |
| Duration of prophase | Short (about 30 min in human cells) | Can take decades |
| Pairing of maternal and paternal homologs | No | Yes, during meiosis I |
| Recombination | Rare and abnormal | Every meiosis; normally at least once in each chromosome arm after pairing |
| Daughter cells | Genetically identical | Genetically different (independent assortment and recombination) |

- [Lucía] Example with one pair of homologs (A, a): after replication there are 2 A and 2 a; mitosis gives two cells each with A and a; meiosis, with recombination between non-sister chromatids, gives four haploid cells with mixed maternal and paternal information.
- From Lec 7.7 (recorded): misaligned homologs at repeated sequences (segmental duplications) can undergo **unequal crossing over**, giving duplications and deletions (genomic disorders such as DiGeorge). See [[Lec 7.7 Genetic variation]].
