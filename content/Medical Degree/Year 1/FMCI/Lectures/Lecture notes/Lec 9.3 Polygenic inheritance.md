## 1. Polygenic, multifactorial (complex) diseases
- Very common diseases affecting millions: heart disease, diabetes, neurodegenerative diseases, asthma, many autoimmune diseases, cancer.
- Not usually considered genetic: not single genes, not Mendelian. But genes are involved in the **predisposition**: many genetic variants (SNPs, "remember the variants we talked about"), hundreds or thousands, each conferring increased susceptibility. Hence **polygenic**.
- Plus **environmental risk factors**: cholesterol, dietary fat, exercise, lifestyle.
- Genetic + environmental = **multifactorial**, "in modern days" called **complex** diseases.
- They also appear in families, but less than single-gene diseases; they show different degrees of heritability.
- **[check]** He says "many rare variants... SNPs"; [Notes] Box 2 describes the GWA-study loci for late-onset AD as **common** disease susceptibility factors with modest or weak effects, and says rare variants of strong effect occur in some subsets (heterogeneity). Possibly a transcription error for "many variants".
- [Notes] Even single-gene disorders are influenced by **modifier loci**. Non-genetic factors include environmental (sometimes via epigenetic modification) and chance (stochastic) factors. Affected individuals often appear as **sporadic** cases; some families show quasi-Mendelian transmission (early-onset AD, diabetes, Parkinson, cancers).

## 2. Heritability: the concept
- **Heritability** = the contribution of genetic factors to the **variation** in the risk of developing a disease.
- His example: if we could measure everyone's risk of Alzheimer's in the room, the values would form a **normal (bell-shaped) distribution**: few with very low risk, few with very high, most intermediate. A normal distribution is defined by mean and standard deviation; the curve can be narrower or broader with the same mean: "what is interesting here is the variance".
- **Total phenotypic variance = genetic variance + environmental variance** (VP = VG + VE). Environmental factors for Alzheimer's he names: schooling (for cognitive diseases), diet, lifestyle, exercise ("quite important for the risk of developing Alzheimer's in particular").
- Heritability measures how much of the total variance in risk is due to genetic factors.
- [Notes] h² = VG/VP, from 0 (no genetic factors) to 1 (exclusively genetic). VG = additive + dominance + interaction effects. Heritability is not fixed: it describes a population in a specific environment, not an individual.
- [Notes] Box 1, **liability threshold**: continuous susceptibility in the population, disease only above a threshold; in first-degree relatives the curve shifts to higher susceptibility, so more are affected. Thresholds can differ between sexes (pyloric stenosis about 1 in 200 newborn males vs 1 in 1000 females); environmental factors can move the threshold. (He described the shifted curve for siblings, section 4, but not the threshold.)
- [Lucía] Only when risk goes beyond a certain threshold will a person manifest the disease.

## 3. Example: Alzheimer's disease
### Pathology
- Two accumulations: **amyloid plaques** outside neurons and **neurofibrillary tangles** inside neurons (tangles made of **phosphorylated tau**).
- **Amyloid precursor protein (APP)**: a normal, physiological transmembrane protein, processed by **β-secretase** and then **γ-secretase**. γ-secretase can cut at different positions, releasing fragments of different sizes. Cutting at one position releases **amyloid β-42** (42 amino acids), "particularly dangerous" because it aggregates into amyloid deposits (plaques).
- Accumulation over years triggers an inflammatory reaction, loss of neurons, dementia. "It's the action of γ-secretase that matters."
- [Slides] Plaques and tangles consist of amyloid fibrils with different components but similar tertiary (cross-β sheet) structure.
- [Notes] Box 2: APP is 770 residues; Aβ40 is the predominant isoform, Aβ42 the most aggregation-prone; disease mutations raise the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio; APP duplication and trisomy 21 (three APP copies; APP on chromosome 21) also cause AD pathology.

### Familial vs sporadic
| | Familial (rare) | Sporadic (typical) |
|---|---|---|
| Inheritance | Like autosomal dominant "basically, or almost" | Does not run in families |
| Cause | Mutations in **presenilin** (presenilin-1, -2), the gene for γ-secretase: more Aβ42; everyone who inherits the mutation develops AD | No mutations; **genetic variants (SNPs)** that shift the risk ("not just a black and white sort of situation") |
| Onset | Very aggressive; people of about 50 in these families | |
- "Sporadic" = from time to time.
- [Slides] Early onset < 65 years (β-amyloid pathway); late onset > 65 years, 95% of all patients (downstream pathways). [Notes] Dominant early-onset AD presents between 30 and 60; causative genes APP, PSEN1, PSEN2; presenilin is the catalytic subunit of γ-secretase.

### Why many genes: convergent pathways
- The final phenotype results from several pathways working together: production of Aβ42, deposition, and **clearance** (macrophages or astrocytes in the brain degrade deposits). The balance decides the amount of amyloid β in plaques.
- ApoE turned out to work in several of these pathways, "in subtle ways", but strongly enough to change risk.
- When a disease results from many converging pathways, you need several variants across them to raise or lower risk. The genes in yellow on his slide each raise the risk "by two times or three times" separately; combined (e.g. bad variants of "B1", "Picarbonate" and ApoE in the transcript; probably BIN1 and PICALM, both in [Notes] Box 2 Figure 2) they explain the heritability of AD. Plus environmental factors, "always".
- [Notes] Box 2 Figure 2: early-onset genes (APP, PSEN1, PSEN2, blue) and GWA susceptibility loci (yellow: CR1, BIN1, CLU, SORL1, PICALM, GAB2, CH25H, ACE; APOE with a red border, moderately strong effect), grouped into production, clearance and degradation of Aβ. [Lucía] If clearance works well, even high Aβ production need not lead to disease.

## 4. Study type 1: familial aggregation (epidemiological) studies
- Epidemiology studies how diseases are distributed in populations. Question: does the disease aggregate in families?
- Siblings share half their alleles (50%), much more than two random people.
- Take healthy people who have a sibling with the disease (his example: type 1 diabetes). Their risk curve is **displaced to the right**. Follow them 20 to 30 years and count how many develop it.
- **Relative risk (RR) or λ**, subscript for the relationship (λS for siblings) = prevalence in relatives of affected people / prevalence in the general population.
- [Slides] Worked example: general population 3/100, siblings 15/100, **λS = 15/3 = 5** ("five times greater"). (He attaches this example to type 1 diabetes; it is illustrative: [Notes] Table 1 gives λS = 15 for type 1 diabetes, as he then says: "For type 1 diabetes it's 15 times actually".)
- **Problem:** siblings share the family environment too, so the excess could be environmental.

[Notes] Table 1, approximate λS:
| Disease | λS |
|---|---|
| Alzheimer disease (late-onset) | 4 |
| Autism spectrum disorder | 6.5 |
| Breast cancer, female | 2 |
| Crohn's disease | 25 |
| Multiple sclerosis | 20 |
| Schizophrenia | 9 |
| Type 1 diabetes | 15 |
| Type 2 diabetes | 3 |
- His comments: type 1 diabetes very high ("more genetic factors involved in this because it's an autoimmune disease"); type 2 not so high; Crohn's and multiple sclerosis (autoimmune) very high; autism "quite a few factors"; schizophrenia: having a sibling or parent with it increases risk a lot.
- **Clinical aside, schizophrenia:** the strongest environmental risk factor is **cannabis** use during adolescence, dose-dependent ("the number of joints you smoke during adolescence") because the brain's connections are still forming; the effect disappears in adulthood. Final risk = genetic + environmental: low genetic risk with very high environmental risk can still give disease.

## 5. Study type 2: twin studies
| | Monozygotic (identical) | Dizygotic (fraternal) |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | One zygote that divided into two embryos very early | Two different fertilizations sharing the womb |
| Genetics | Identical (they acquire small differences over life) | Share 50% of variants, like any siblings |
| Sex | Same sex | Can differ |
- **Concordance:** if one twin has the disease, the other has it too (concordant pair); otherwise discordant. Measure concordance in many MZ and DZ pairs where at least one twin is affected.
- Difficult: you need 100 to 200 pairs; usually collaborative between hospitals in several countries. A collection of such families "is a treasure".
- **h² = 2 x (CMZ - CDZ).** DZ concordance is what any pair of siblings would show; what matters is the **difference**, not whether the values are high or low.
- His values from the slide: schizophrenia about 0.7 ("like 35 times 2"), Alzheimer's about 0.48, type 1 diabetes about 0.7, type 2 lower, multiple sclerosis 0.4. Values **above 0.4** "begin to be very, very suggestive of high heritability".
- **Measles example:** very contagious; twins share everything, so concordance is high in both: DZ about 0.9, MZ "very high as well", "very similar". h²: a difference of about 0.05, "0.05 times 2, 0.1", very low. Same for a traffic accident: concordance may be high but genetics has no impact.

|---|---|---|---|

## 6. Study type 3: adoption studies
- Even more difficult: someone adopted at birth (ideally) or very early into a different, distant family. Compare the biological family and the adoptive family.
- If the adoptee develops a disease frequent in the **adoptive** family: probably environmental. If, e.g. at 25, he develops a disease present in the **biological** family: genetic factors.
- "The only type of study that really corrects for environmental factors"; if it shows up, genetic factors must be very strong.
- Logistics: workable for frequent diseases (type 1 diabetes); for rarer ones hard to find adoptees who developed the disease, and you must trace who had it in both families.
- [Notes] Figure 3 problems: adoption at birth? selective placement? intrauterine influences?

## 7. Heritability of quantitative (continuous) traits
- Variables can be **continuous** (height, blood pressure, blood glucose) or **categorical/dichotomous** (healthy vs diseased; hypertension yes/no).
- For a continuous trait (his example: blood pressure), compare the **mid-parent value** (mean of father and mother) with the **offspring value**.
- Plot mid-parent (x) vs child (y) and fit a regression line, y = coefficient x X. The **slope = heritability**.
- No genetic influence: high-pressure parents with low-pressure children and vice versa, a shapeless cloud, flat line, slope near 0. Strong genetic influence: slope tends to 1.
- Hard to do: you must measure the trait in parents and children.
- [Notes] Figure 4: values normalized (mean = 0); slope of the regression line shows low (left) or high (right) heritability. Infectious diseases are mainly environmental, though host genetics contributes; schizophrenia, ASD, AD, type 1 diabetes, MS and Crohn's have a strong genetic contribution; Parkinson and type 2 diabetes less.
