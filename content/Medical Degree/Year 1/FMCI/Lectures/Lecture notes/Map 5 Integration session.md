## Reading the map
- **Keys**: a membrane separating two compartments = the **inner mitochondrial membrane**; **palmitoyl-CoA** in the cytoplasm; a transport system for it = the **carnitine shuttle**.
- Palmitoyl-CoA enters the matrix and is converted into **myristoyl-CoA** (14 C) + **L**: so the process is **beta-oxidation** and **L = acetyl-CoA**.
- L condenses with **J** to give **I**: J = **oxaloacetate**, I = **citrate**. Part of the citrate is oxidized in the Krebs cycle, part is exported to the cytoplasm.
- In the cytoplasm citrate is split (citrate lyase) into acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate ([[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]]).
- Oxaloacetate is **transaminated** with **glutamate**, giving **aspartate** and **N = alpha-ketoglutarate**. The enzyme is **aspartate aminotransferase**.
- Aspartate donates its amino group in **purine synthesis**: **IMP** (inosine monophosphate, a purine nucleotide and the precursor of AMP and GMP) to adenylosuccinate; **adenylosuccinate lyase** releases **AMP** (6-aminopurine) and **fumarate**, which can be oxidized in the Krebs cycle ([[Lec 5.12 Nucleotide metabolism]]).

## The point of the map: parallel reactions
The two oxidations of beta-oxidation are chemically the same as two Krebs reactions:

| Beta-oxidation | Krebs cycle |
|---|---|
| Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (FAD): acyl-CoA to trans-enoyl-CoA | Succinate dehydrogenase (FAD): succinate to fumarate |
| Enoyl-CoA hydratase: hydration to beta-hydroxyacyl-CoA | Fumarase: hydration of fumarate to malate |
| Beta-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (NAD+): to beta-ketoacyl-CoA | Malate dehydrogenase (NAD+): malate to oxaloacetate |

The 5.9 study notes say the same: the first three beta-oxidation reactions are analogous to succinate dehydrogenase, fumarate hydratase and malate dehydrogenase ([[Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies]]).

## Transamination recap
- Purpose: collect the amino groups of all 20 amino acids into a few (mainly **glutamate**, then **alanine** and **glutamine**) with one general reaction instead of 20 enzymes.
- A transaminase moves the amino group of an amino acid to the carbonyl of an **alpha-keto acid**: with pyruvate you make **alanine**; with alpha-ketoglutarate you make **glutamate**.
- Glutamate can be aminated to **glutamine**. Lecturer: this happens mainly in the **brain**, which sends ammonia to the liver as glutamine; glutamate itself is "too acidic" to be the transport form. **[verify]** these two claims; [[Lec 5.8 Amino acid catabolism and the urea cycle]] says glutamine is used by most extrahepatic tissues.
- **[check]** The transcript says "with alpha-ketoglutarate we will produce glutamine"; transamination of alpha-ketoglutarate gives **glutamate** (5.8 notes and Lucía).
- Carbon skeletons: some give glucose (oxaloacetate, pyruvate, succinate, malate); those that give acetyl-CoA cannot make glucose.

### Clinical names of the two main transaminases
| Name | Alternative name (blood tests) | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| **AST** (aspartate aminotransferase) | **GOT** (glutamate-oxaloacetate transaminase) | Aspartate + alpha-ketoglutarate to oxaloacetate + glutamate |
| **ALT** (alanine aminotransferase) | **GPT** (glutamate-pyruvate transaminase) | Alanine + alpha-ketoglutarate to pyruvate + glutamate |

The GOT/GPT names tell you the substrates.

## Quiz answers given in class
1. **Coenzyme Q is directly reduced by** electrons from **both** succinate dehydrogenase (complex II, FADH2) and fatty acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (via ETF) ([[Lec 5.7 Oxidative phosphorylation]]).
2. **CAT I**: inhibited by the product of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (**malonyl-CoA**); catalyzes the rate-limiting step of fatty acid oxidation; catalyzes a transesterification. All three statements true.
3. **Turns to oxidize myristoyl-CoA (14 C)**: **6 turns**, so **6 FADH2** (and 7 acetyl-CoA). **[check]** Someone in class said "14 carbons are 7 cuts"; by the rule in the 5.9 note (turns = n/2 - 1) it is 6.
4. **Cofactor for transaminations**: **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**. "Typical question."
5. **Urea cycle enzyme** in the question: the class debated arginase vs argininosuccinase. The lecturer clarified that **arginase** is the enzyme that splits arginine into urea + ornithine, while **argininosuccinase (argininosuccinate lyase)** releases **fumarate**. **[verify]** which option was marked correct; the recording is unclear.
6. **Main regulator of urea synthesis**: **N-acetylglutamate**, the allosteric activator of **CPS I**; without it CPS I does not work. "Typical question", "very important".
