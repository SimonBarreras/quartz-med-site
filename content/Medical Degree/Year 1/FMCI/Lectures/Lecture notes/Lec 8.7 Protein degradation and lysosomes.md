## 1. Discovery: Christian de Duve and ultracentrifugation
- **Christian de Duve**, a medical doctor, discovered both **lysosomes and peroxisomes** while studying liver enzymes in cell fractions. **Nobel Prize in Medicine 1974** [Slides].
- **[verify]** His country is garbled in the transcript ("Ventium"); not on the slides.
- **[Slides]** Discovery by ultracentrifugation and TEM.
- Goal of the technique: isolate specific organelles to study their **function** (more than their morphology).

| Step | Spin (lecturer) | Pellet (sediment) | Supernatant goes to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Homogenize the tissue (mortar and pestle, buffer) to break the cells | | Centrifuge tube |
| 1 | **800 g, 10 min** | **Nuclei** (densest, sediment fastest) | Step 2 |
| 2 | **15,000 g, 10 min** | **Mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes** ("mitochondrial fraction"; similar density) | Step 3 |
| 3 | **100,000 g, 60 min** | **Membranes** (plasma membrane, RER, SER) | Step 4 |

## 2. Structure of lysosomes
- Function: digest (destroy) molecules: proteins, lipids, nucleotides, etc.
- **[Slides]** Size **0.2-0.5 um**. Name: lysis (decompose) + soma (body).

### Membrane
| Component | Role (lecturer) |
|---|---|
| Lipid bilayer | |
| **Carrier proteins** | Export useful products out of the lysosome. Example: after LDL endocytosis, the protein part is destroyed but **cholesterol** is exported for the cell's membranes. Not everything is fully destroyed |
| **Complex glycans** on the inner face ([Slides]: glycosylated proteins) | **Protect the membrane from self-digestion**: no lysosomal enzyme can digest them |
| **Proton pumps** | Pump H+ in against the gradient to keep **pH about 5**, needed for digestion |
- **[Lucía]** The pump is an ATPase that imports H+.

### Interior
- **Acid hydrolases**: about **40 types** (slides: lipases, nucleases...). Not every lysosome has all 40; they can be **specialized** (30, 15...) for specific substrates.
- All lysosomes contain **acid phosphatase**: the **universal marker** of lysosomes. A round organelle containing acid phosphatase is a lysosome.

## 3. Endocytosis review and primary vs secondary lysosomes
1. **LDL** binds its receptor in the plasma membrane and enters in **clathrin-coated vesicles**.
2. Vesicles fuse with the **early endosome**, which has proton pumps: low pH separates **receptor and ligand**.
3. The receptor is **recycled** to the plasma membrane; LDL moves to the **late endosome**, where digestion will happen.
4. Lysosomal enzymes are made on **RER ribosomes**, N-glycosylated in the RER, then go to the Golgi (N- and O-glycosylation there) for modification and stability.
5. They leave the trans-Golgi in vesicles: **hydrolytic vesicles = primary lysosomes** ("bags full of enzymes").
6. Primary lysosomes fuse with late endosomes: now substrate and enzymes meet = **secondary lysosome = endolysosome**.

| | Primary lysosome (hydrolytic vesicle) | Secondary lysosome (endolysosome) |
|---|---|---|
| Content | Enzymes only, no substrate | Enzymes + substrates |
| pH | About **7** | About **5** (proton pumps) |
| Digestion | No | Yes |
| EM | Small round vesicles near the Golgi; to be sure, immunohistochemistry for **acid phosphatase** | **Very variable** morphology (some with concentric membranes) |
- Student question in class: the early endosome contains **no enzymes**; enzymes reach the late endosome deeper in the cell. A primary lysosome holds many enzymes, bound to the receptor on its membrane.

## 4. Tagging and sorting lysosomal enzymes (M6P)
Lecturer's sequence:
1. Enzyme synthesized on the **RER** and **N-glycosylated**; one of the sugars in the branch is **mannose**.
2. Vesicles carry it to the **cis-Golgi**, where a **phosphotransferase** adds a phosphate to **carbon 6 of the terminal mannose**: **mannose 6-phosphate (M6P)** = the tag ("barcode").
3. The enzyme moves through the Golgi stacks to the **trans-Golgi network**, where **M6P receptors** bind it and drive the formation of vesicles (primary lysosomes) towards the late endosome.
4. In the late endosome, **acid phosphatase removes the phosphate** so the enzyme is free and fully functional; it then digests the substrates (e.g. LDL).

- **[check]** Number of enzymes adding the phosphate: the lecturer named one **phosphotransferase**; the slides and Lucía say mannose is phosphorylated "by sequential activity of **two enzymes**". The 8.6 study material names a phosphotransferase.
- **[check]** How the enzyme is released from the receptor: the lecturer said acid phosphatase removes the phosphate "because otherwise the enzyme gets attached to this receptor". The slides and Lucía say **low pH in the late endosome causes dissociation** of enzyme and receptor; the 8.6 study material says low pH dissociates them and **then** a phosphatase removes the M6P, and the receptor is recycled to the Golgi. The slides list "Receptor is recycled".
- **[check]** Lysosomal pH: lecturer, slides and Lucía say **5**; the 8.6 study material says **3-4**. The 8.6 study material also gives the receptor-binding pH in the Golgi as 6-6.5.

### Default pathway
- Every compartment needs its own tag (Golgi-resident, RER-resident, lysosomal...). A protein made in the RER with **no tag** goes **out of the cell by default** (secretion).

## 5. I-cell disease (inclusion-cell disease, mucolipidosis type II)
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Inheritance | **Autosomal recessive** |
| Cause | Mutations in the **phosphotransferase**: no M6P on lysosomal enzymes |
| Consequence 1 | Untagged enzymes are **secreted** out of the cell. They need pH about 5, so outside they work suboptimally, but may still have some activity. [Lucía] This extracellular activity contributes to defects |
| Consequence 2 | **Lysosomes devoid of enzymes**: undigested substrates accumulate as **inclusions** (hence the name) |
| Why so severe | Lacks essentially **all** lysosomal enzymes (not just one) |
| Patients | Deformities, malformations, mental retardation; **life expectancy about 5 years**. [Slides] abnormal development, **dwarfism** |
| Cells | Example: peripheral blood **lymphocytes** filled with lysosomes of undigested material; cytosol occupied, cells stressed |
- Lecturer's analogy: a person who hoards garbage at home (photo of a car full of rubbish). **[verify]** He named a syndrome for this ("Diabetes Syndrome" in the transcript); garbled, and it is not on the slides.

## 6. Lysosomal storage disorders (LSD): general features
- Storage of undigested substrates, cell stress and malfunction, **developmental and metabolic problems** [Slides].
- Two kinds of cause: lack of **all** enzymes (tagging defect, I-cell) or mutation of **one specific enzyme** (e.g. Gaucher).
- **More than 50** LSDs; incidence **1:5,000 to 1:8,000 newborns**: **rare diseases** [Slides].
- **Inheritance**: **autosomal recessive**, except some **X-linked** (example: **Fabry**) [Slides].
- **High incidence in endogamic populations**, e.g. **Ashkenazi Jews**: carriers marrying carriers within a closed community propagate the disease. Hence their traditional use in genetic studies of rare diseases.

### Lecturer's remarks
- **Rare diseases and the pharmaceutical industry**: industry traditionally did not invest (hard to treat genetic diseases; not profitable for few patients). Developing a drug is very expensive: of about 1,000 candidates, with luck one reaches a clinical trial. This has changed; there are now drugs for some LSDs.
- **Story**: a rabbi in Brooklyn lost two children to such a disease (they died at 5-6) and founded an organization offering genetic counseling to young Jewish couples. **[Slides]** **Dor Yeshorim**, founded by **Rabbi Josef Ekstein, 1983, Brooklyn** (doryeshorim.org); premarital counseling to prevent LSDs in Jewish communities.

## 7. Gaucher disease
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Cause | Mutation in one enzyme, **glucocerebrosidase** (tagging is normal) |
| Accumulates | **Glucosylceramide** |
| Key cell | **Macrophage** (all cells carry the mutation). Affected macrophages = **Gaucher cells**; bone marrow biopsy shows macrophages full of bluish undigested material |
| Clinical | **Skeletal and neurological defects**; **hepatosplenomegaly** (enlarged liver and spleen); **bone loss** is a typical finding |
- **[Lucía]** Macrophages are abundant in bone, liver and spleen, which is why the manifestations affect those organs.

### Treatment
| Option | Lecturer | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Symptomatic | Treat pain and other symptoms | |
| **Bone marrow transplant** | Macrophages come from the bone marrow. High-dose irradiation kills the patient's marrow to make room; donor marrow repopulates it and produces normal macrophages | Approved |
| **Enzyme replacement therapy** | See below | Approved |
| **Gene therapy** | E.g. bone marrow stem cells infected with a virus carrying the normal gene, replacing the defective one | Clinical trials ([Slides]: "in trials") |

### Enzyme replacement therapy (understand the concept only; "you don't need to know this")
1. Recombinant **glucocerebrosidase** is produced in bacteria.
2. It is covered with **glycans typical of bacteria**.
3. The Gaucher macrophage "thinks" it is a bacterium and **phagocytoses** it.
4. Through the endosome pathway it reaches the endosomes/lysosomes holding the undigested substrate, which can now be digested.
- "A very smart way of fooling" the macrophage.
- **[Lucía]** The enzyme is covered with **mannose** to favour its endocytosis.
