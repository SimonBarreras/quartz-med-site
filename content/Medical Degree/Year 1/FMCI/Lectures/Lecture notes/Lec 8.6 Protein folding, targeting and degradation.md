## 1. Why this topic matters: proteinopathies
- Last step of gene expression: the protein must fold, be modified (post-translational modifications), reach its destination and eventually be degraded.
- Many diseases are linked to failures of **folding, maturation, targeting and degradation**.
- **Neurodegenerative diseases** are now grouped as **proteinopathies**: a common feature is **protein aggregates** inside neurons or in the brain tissue.
- Newest therapies try to **block aggregate formation**. For Alzheimer's, treatment used to be only symptomatic; now there are **antibodies** against the aberrantly aggregated proteins (beta-amyloid, tau; alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's).

### Clinical example: cystic fibrosis (not neurological)
- Caused by a mutation in one codon that **eliminates one phenylalanine** in the primary structure of the chloride channel **CFTR**. That is enough to cause **degradation** of the channel, so lung cells lack it. A disease caused by aberrant degradation.
- **[check]** He then said the cells "don't produce enough surfactant, which is a mucus that allows absorption of oxygen". Lucía (Lecture 3.4 Microtransport, PDF p. 47-49) describes it differently: CFTR expels Cl-; when it fails, Cl- is not transported out, water is drawn in by osmosis, the **mucus layer becomes thicker**, cilia cannot move and infections follow.

## 2. Protein folding
- A protein is not just its primary structure: it needs **folding** to reach its three-dimensional **native conformation** and be active. (Lecturer's image: insulin-like growth factor; "this is not the insulin".)
- **Question asked in class**: how long does an average 100-amino-acid protein take to fold? **Milliseconds** (less than a second).
- Folding starts **during translation**. For proteins made on ER-bound ribosomes, translation, folding and post-translational modifications happen **at the same time**.

### The Levinthal paradox (understand, do not memorize)
| Step | Value |
|---|---|
| Conformations per amino acid | about 10 (because rotation around the **phi and psi angles** is almost free; side chains add possibilities) |
| Conformations for 100 aa | 10^100 |
| Time to sample one conformation | 10^-13 s |
| Random search for 100 aa | 10^87 s = **3.17 x 10^79 years** [Slides] |
| Age of the universe | about 1.38 x 10^10 years |
- Conclusion: folding cannot be a random search; other forces drive it. **[Slides]** "If protein folding were a purely random search, no protein in your body would ever fold."
- Main driving force: **hydrophobic interactions** (also hydrogen bonds and others).
- Biological sense: a gene is expressed because its product is needed **as soon as possible**.

### Stepwise and cooperative ("this is important")
- Analogy with **hemoglobin cooperativity**: when one region adopts its secondary structure, the rotation possibilities of nearby amino acids decrease, so folding accelerates as it proceeds.
- In parallel: **glycosylation**, needed by many proteins to reach their final conformation.
- **[Notes]** As some regions acquire their secondary conformation, it is easier for others; certain sequences fold into helices or sheets driven by the backbone rotation restrictions.

## 3. Aggregation risk and chaperones
- Hydrophobic forces and modifications are sometimes not enough: some proteins **cannot fold by themselves**. **Chaperones** help them fold and also drive them to their destination.
- **Why intermediates are dangerous**: in intermediate folding states, regions of the protein are exposed; **hydrophobic** segments (e.g. sheets) tend to stick to hydrophobic parts of other molecules of the same protein, so **aggregation** is likely. Hence folding must finish fast.
- Chaperones **prevent misfolding and aggregation**, and can **fix** aggregation problems: the cell activates signaling that induces chaperones.
- If chaperones cannot fix it, a secondary pathway drives the cell to **apoptosis**.
  - Lecturer's example: neurons of the **hippocampus** (memory acquisition). If a protein aggregates, the neuron tries to fix it (chaperone expression); if that fails, the neuron dies by apoptosis.
- Folding with chaperones consumes **a lot of ATP**: ATP hydrolysis changes the chaperone's conformation and lets it move along the protein from one segment to another.

| Family | Structure (lecturer) | How it works | [Notes] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hsp70** (heat shock proteins) | Usually **monomeric** | Binds mainly to **intermediate states**, prevents aggregation, helps folding | Expression induced by heat stress; creates a favourable microenvironment; can bind poorly folded regions and unfold them so they refold correctly; also keeps proteins unfolded during transport into organelles |
| **Chaperonins** | **Multimeric** | Form a **cavity** (tube) with a chemical environment in which the unfolded protein folds | Multiprotein complexes for proteins that do not fold spontaneously; prokaryotic **GroEL/GroES**, eukaryotic analog **Hsp60**; rings forming two chambers; the protein is trapped by hydrophobic interactions and folds before the ring's ATP is hydrolyzed; ATP hydrolysis changes GroES conformation and releases the folded protein |
- **[check]** The transcript gives the Hsp as "HSP-17". The slides and study material say **Hsp70**.
- **[Lucía]** Hsp were discovered after a heat shock (many proteins had been denatured). Chaperones avoid long exposure of hydrophobic regions, bind them and unfold them to refold correctly; some take part in targeting and transport.
- **[Notes]** When glycosylation in the ER is disrupted, ER stress triggers the **UPR (unfolded protein response)**, aimed at reactivating the process or, if not achieved, at **apoptosis**. (Lucía: incorrect glycosylation causes many proteinopathies.)

## 5. [Slides]/[Notes] Signal sequences
- Proteins move from their site of synthesis to exit the cell, become part of a membrane, or enter a subcellular compartment [Slides]. This compartmentalization is a feature of eukaryotic cells.
- The destination is encoded in the protein itself as a short **signal sequence**, usually at or near the **N-terminus**, **13-36 amino acids** long [Slides, Notes].
- **[Notes]** Proof: chimeric proteins in which the N-terminal region is replaced by that of a lysosomal protein end up in the lysosome.
- **[Notes]** Except those made in mitochondria, most proteins are synthesized on membrane-associated ribosomes (RER) and must be targeted. Degradation selectivity is also encoded in the sequence.

### ER signal sequence (consensus)
| Feature | [Slides] | [Notes] |
|---|---|---|
| Core | Rich in hydrophobic aa | Hydrophobic aa abundant |
| N-terminus | A positively charged aa | A positively charged aa |
| C-terminus | aa with short side chains (Ala, Gly) | Short sequence of polar aa with small side chains (Ala); contains the proteolysis point that removes the signal peptide in the ER |
- **[Lucía]** The C-terminal feature refers to the signal peptide, not the whole protein.

## 6. [Notes] Translation at the ER (SRP pathway)
1. Synthesis starts on **free ribosomes**.
2. The N-terminal signal sequence emerges from the ribosome.
3. It binds the **signal recognition particle (SRP)**, a ribonucleoprotein; **translation stops**.
4. SRP binds **GTP**; SRP-GTP brings the ribosome to the **SRP receptor** on the ER membrane; the signal peptide crosses the membrane through the **peptide translocation complex**.
5. **GTP is hydrolyzed**, SRP dissociates, translation **restarts**.
6. Translation continues to completion.
7. A **peptidase** cleaves the signal peptide.
8. The ribosome dissociates from the ER and is recycled.
- **[Slides]** After the ER (where some modification such as glycosylation occurs), transport vesicles take proteins to the Golgi: the **biosynthetic/secretory pathway**.

## 8. [Notes] Lysosomal targeting: mannose 6-phosphate
1. Lysosomal **hydrolases** reach the Golgi carrying a "tag" recognized by a **phosphotransferase**, which phosphorylates a terminal **mannose** of the oligosaccharide.
2. **Mannose 6-phosphate (M6P)** is the signal for lysosomes.
3. An **M6P receptor** in the Golgi membrane binds it at **pH 6-6.5**.
4. Hydrolase-receptor complexes are coated by **clathrin**, forming vesicles sent to the lysosome.
5. Lysosomal pH is **acidic (3-4)** (continuous proton pumping into the lumen); low pH dissociates the hydrolase from the receptor.
6. A **phosphatase** removes the M6P tag and the receptor is recycled to the Golgi. Both events stop the hydrolase from returning to the Golgi.
- Failure of this transport: waste accumulates in lysosomes, leading to diseases such as **sphingolipidoses, glycogenoses, mucopolysaccharidoses** ([Lucía]: "lysosomal storage diseases").
- See [[Lec 8.7 Protein degradation and lysosomes]] for the lysosome lecture.

## 9. [Slides]/[Notes] Mitochondrial targeting
- Directed by specific **N-terminal** sequences. The protein binds **chaperones in the cytosol** that bring it to **receptors on the outside of the mitochondrion**, which promote its translocation inside.

## 10. [Slides]/[Notes] Nuclear targeting
- Many nuclear proteins (RNA and DNA polymerases, histones, topoisomerases, transcription factors) are made on cytoplasmic ribosomes and imported [Notes].
- **NLS (nuclear localization sequence)**: **4-8 aa, rich in basic residues (Arg, Lys)**; stays in the protein after transport [Slides, Notes].
- **[check]** Lucía describes the NLS as "rich in hydrophobic regions". The slides and study material say **basic** amino acids.
- Mechanism [Notes]:
  1. **Importins alpha and beta** bind the protein in the cytoplasm and take it to the **nuclear pore complex**; it is translocated inside.
  2. In the nucleus, **Ran** (GTPase; Ras-related nuclear protein) binds importin beta and separates it.
  3. The nuclear protein-importin alpha complex binds Ran and **CAS** (cellular apoptosis susceptibility protein), releasing the nuclear protein.
  4. Importins return to the cytoplasm, where Ran hydrolyzes GTP and releases them.
  5. Ran-GDP returns to the nucleus via **NTF2**; in the nucleus **Ran-GEF** exchanges GDP for GTP.
- **[Slides]** Ribosomal proteins are made in the cytosol, imported into the nucleus, assembled into subunits in the **nucleolus**, then exported to the cytosol.

## 11. [Slides]/[Notes] Protein degradation
- Inevitable; maintains protein homeostasis, prevents accumulation of aberrant proteins, recycles amino acids [Notes].
- **Half-lives**: from seconds to days or even months [Slides]; [Notes] about 30 s to several days; **hemoglobin** lives about as long as the erythrocyte (about **110 days**). Short-lived: damaged or erroneous proteins, and critical **regulatory** proteins.
- **Two systems in eukaryotes** [Notes]:
  - **Lysosomal**: membrane proteins, extracellular proteins, long-lived proteins.
  - **ATP-dependent ubiquitin-proteasome** system.

### Ubiquitin-proteasome system
| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Ubiquitin | **76 aa**, highly conserved (identical in most organisms) [Notes, Slides] |
| Attachment | Covalently to **lysine** of the target, ATP-dependent, by **E1 (activating), E2 (conjugating), E3 (ligating)** [Slides, Notes] |
| Polyubiquitin | Repeated ubiquitination forms polyubiquitin tails [Notes] |
| Proteasome | **26S** proteasome cleaves ubiquitinated proteins [Slides]; [Notes] two copies of a complex with 32 different subunits; proteolysis inside |
| Signals | Not exactly known; some sequences make proteins more prone to ubiquitination [Notes] |
- **[Lucía]** The more ubiquitin, the more likely degradation. **Cyclins** carry a sequence that favours ubiquitination, hence a short life.

### [Slides] N-terminal residue and half-life (Bachmair et al. 1986, measured in yeast)
| N-terminal residue | Half-life |
|---|---|
| Ala, Gly, Met, Ser, Thr, Val (stabilizing) | > 20 h |
| Gln, Ile | about 30 min |
| Glu, Tyr | about 10 min |
| Pro | about 7 min |
| Asp, Leu, Lys, Phe | about 3 min |
| Arg | about 2 min |

### [Notes] Diseases of degradation
- Altered degradation of cell-cycle proteins (oncogene products) leads to **tumors**.
- Neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer, Parkinson, Huntington): aberrant aggregates accumulate partly because cells cannot get rid of them (links back to section 1).
