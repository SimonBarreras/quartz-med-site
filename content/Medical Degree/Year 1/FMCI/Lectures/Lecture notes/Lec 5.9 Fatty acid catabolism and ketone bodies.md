## 1. Brown fat
- White adipocytes: normal mitochondria. Brown adipocytes: **uncoupled** mitochondria. They transport electrons and consume O2 but make very little ATP; the energy is released as heat.
- Uncoupling is due to UCP1 (thermogenin), a proton channel in the inner mitochondrial membrane (explained in [[Lec 5.7 Oxidative phosphorylation]], section 7).
- In humans, brown fat is mostly present in childhood, especially just after birth, for heat production. Adults can increase brown fat (lecturer: "train" the body to make it).

## 2. Why fat yields more energy than carbohydrate
- Fatty acids are **more reduced**: more electrons per carbon to feed the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis.
- Lecturer: about 9 kcal/g for fat vs 4.5 kcal/g for carbohydrate. **[check]** The study notes give **9 kcal/g fat vs 5 kcal/g carbohydrate**. The sources disagree on the carbohydrate figure; ask which value the exam uses.
- **[Slides]** Fat is also stored with less water (non-polar). Glucose and glycogen serve short-term needs with quick delivery; fats serve long-term needs (months). About 80% of the energy of heart and liver comes from fatty acid oxidation; about one third of our energy needs come from dietary TAG.

## 3. Sources of fat
1. Internal storage (triacylglycerols, TAG, in adipose tissue)
2. Diet (part oxidized immediately, part stored)
3. Endogenous synthesis

Clinical aside: fat around the liver and the heart is dangerous; reducing fat accumulation is the first advice for weight loss.

## 4. Hormonal control: insulin/glucagon ratio
- More insulin: more storage, less mobilization. More glucagon: more mobilization.
- Glucagon exceeds insulin in **hypoglycemia** (low blood glucose, fasting). High glucose: insulin up, glucagon down, and excess glucose is converted to fat.
- Key rule: carbohydrate carbons can become fatty acids (all excess carbohydrate is stored as TAG), but fatty acid carbons **cannot** be used to make glucose.
- Why: explained in [[Lec 5.8 Amino acid catabolism and the urea cycle]], section 8. The 2 C that enter the Krebs cycle as acetyl-CoA are lost in its two decarboxylations, so there is no net carbon for glucose.
- Tissue preference: the heart (among others) prefers fatty acids; the brain (neurons) depends mainly on glucose. When glucose is low, it is spared for the brain and other tissues are given fatty acids.

## 5. Fat mobilization (lipolysis) in the adipocyte
Links to Prof. Tavira's GPCR lecture (Lec 4.3).

### Cascade
1. Glucagon binds the **glucagon receptor**, a GPCR (G protein-coupled receptor; 7 transmembrane domains).
2. Ligand binding causes a conformational change that reaches the intracellular domain and promotes exchange of **GDP for GTP** on G-alpha.
3. G-alpha-GTP dissociates from the G-beta-gamma dimer; both can signal.
4. **G-alpha-s-GTP** activates membrane **adenylyl cyclase**: ATP to cAMP. (**[check]** The lecturer said "GTP binds adenylate cyclase"; in [[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]] he says it is the alpha subunit that stimulates adenylyl cyclase.)
5. cAMP, a hydrophilic second messenger, binds proteins with cAMP-binding domains such as **PKA** (cAMP-dependent protein kinase). More cAMP, more PKA activity.
6. PKA phosphorylates:
   - **Hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL)**: hydrolyzes the ester bonds between fatty acids and glycerol in TAG of the lipid droplet.
   - **Perilipin**: protein coating the lipid droplet; phosphorylation "opens holes" in the coat so the lipase can reach the TAG.
7. Free fatty acids leave the adipocyte, bind **serum albumin**, and are carried to the liver, muscle, heart and other tissues.

**[Lucía]**
- **Epinephrine** as well as glucagon triggers this cascade in hypoglycemia.
- Phosphorylated perilipin attracts the cytosolic lipase to the TAG, which is hydrolyzed into **glycerol and fatty acids**; both are released into the bloodstream.
- **[Notes]** Glycerol can also provide energy through glycolysis. Albumin carries fatty acids to liver, muscle, heart and adrenal cortex.

### G protein review
- Heterotrimer: alpha, beta, gamma. G-alpha has intrinsic **GTPase** activity.
- Alpha subtypes: alpha-s, alpha-i, alpha-q, alpha-o, alpha-12 (details not required). Alpha-s stimulates adenylyl cyclase (cAMP up); alpha-i inhibits it (cAMP down).

### Switching the signal off
1. Glucose rises, glucagon falls, less receptor activation.
2. G-alpha hydrolyzes its GTP to GDP; G-alpha-GDP changes conformation, rebinds beta-gamma and stays inactive until a new stimulus.
3. **GAPs** (GTPase-activating proteins) increase GTPase activity: more GTPase activity means a shorter, weaker signal, and vice versa.

Practical point: to burn more fat, eat less carbohydrate or raise energy expenditure. Balancing fat and carbohydrate intake plus physical activity is enough; strong carbohydrate restriction is not needed. Genetics and other factors also play a role.

## 6. Burning fatty acids in the target cell
Sequence: mobilization, then **activation**, then **transport into the mitochondrion**, then **beta-oxidation** (in the mitochondrial matrix).

### 6a. The transport problem
- The inner mitochondrial membrane is highly restrictive; fatty acids are hydrophobic.
- Short fatty acids cross freely; long ones (16, 18, 20 C, which are most fatty acids) need the carnitine shuttle.
- **[check]** The lecturer said 14 C or fewer cross freely. Study notes and Lucía's notes: **12 C or fewer** enter the mitochondria without a transport system; **14 C or more** need the carnitine shuttle (slides: fewer than 12 C diffuse freely).

### 6b. Activation (all fatty acids, any length)
- The carboxyl group is activated by forming a high-energy **thioester** with coenzyme A:
  fatty acid + CoA-SH + ATP to fatty acyl-CoA + AMP + PPi
- Enzyme: **fatty acyl-CoA synthetases**, a family of isoenzymes in the **outer mitochondrial membrane** (study notes). **[check]** The transcript says "fatty acid synthetase"; the study notes and Lucía's notes give fatty acyl-CoA synthetases. Do not confuse with fatty acid synthase ([[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]]).
- Occurs in two steps; the lecturer said you do not need to learn them.
- Not a phosphorylation. ATP goes to AMP + PPi, so both phosphoanhydride bonds are used.
- Cost = **2 ATP equivalents**: oxidative phosphorylation regenerates ATP from ADP, not from AMP, so adenylate kinase first does AMP + ATP to 2 ADP, and then both ADP are rephosphorylated.
- Reversibility: asked in class; the lecturer was unsure ("I guess it is reversible"). **[verify]** Not addressed in the slides or study notes.

### 6c. Carnitine shuttle
- Carnitine = 4-trimethylammonio-3-hydroxybutyrate: butyrate (4 C with carboxylate), trimethylated, positively charged N on C4, OH on C3. Known in gym culture as a "fat burner" because it is needed to bring fatty acids into the mitochondrion.
- Components:
  - **CAT I** (carnitine acyltransferase I): outer side. The lecturer learned it as the outer face of the inner membrane and said the location does not matter; the **study notes** also place it on the **outer face of the inner mitochondrial membrane**.
  - **Acylcarnitine/carnitine translocase**: antiporter in the inner membrane.
  - **CAT II** (carnitine acyltransferase II): matrix side of the inner membrane (study notes and Lucía: inner face of the inner membrane).
- Cycle:
  1. CAT I: acyl-CoA + carnitine to **acylcarnitine** + CoA-SH (transesterification onto the OH of carnitine).
  2. Translocase: acylcarnitine in, free carnitine out (keeps free carnitine available outside).
  3. CAT II: acylcarnitine + CoA-SH to acyl-CoA + carnitine, inside the matrix.
- Net result: the same molecules on both sides. The point is only to get the long acyl chain across the inner membrane, where all the beta-oxidation enzymes are.
- **[Lucía]** / **[Notes]** This transport is the **rate-limiting stage** of fatty acid oxidation; once in the matrix, the only fate of the fatty acid is oxidation. The main allosteric regulator of CAT I is **malonyl-CoA**, made by acetyl-CoA carboxylase in fatty acid synthesis, so fatty acids are not made and degraded at the same time. In hypoglycemia, glucagon (cAMP, PKA) inhibits malonyl-CoA synthesis and CAT I works at full capacity ([[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]]).

## 7. Beta-oxidation: introduction
### Three stages of cellular respiration
1. Oxidation of fuels to acetyl-CoA: glycolysis, pyruvate oxidative decarboxylation (PDH), **beta-oxidation** of fatty acids.
2. Krebs cycle.
3. Oxidative phosphorylation (electron transport + ATP synthase).

The lecturer taught beta-oxidation before oxphos so that oxphos can then cover the electrons from glycolysis, Krebs and beta-oxidation together.

### Why "beta"
- Each turn releases one **acetyl-CoA** (2 C) by breaking the bond between **C2 (alpha) and C3 (beta)**. Numbering starts at C1 = the thioester carbon bound to S-CoA.
- A C-C bond between two methylene groups is very stable; breaking it directly is not thermodynamically feasible.
- Strategy: oxidize the beta carbon (C3) to a **carbonyl (keto) group** (beta-ketoacyl-CoA). This weakens the C2-C3 bond so it can be cleaved.

### Acetyl-CoA yield
| Fatty acid | Carbons | Acetyl-CoA | Turns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Palmitic | 16 | 8 | 7 |
| Stearic | 18 | 9 | 8 |

Turns = (n/2) - 1, since the last turn yields two acetyl-CoA (section 8). (The class gave mixed answers for stearic; 9 is correct.)

## 8. End of each turn: thiolysis
- After the beta carbon is oxidized to a keto group, the C2-C3 bond is weaker. **CoA-SH acts as nucleophile** and cleaves it (thiolysis).
- Products: **acetyl-CoA** + a fatty acyl-CoA **2 carbons shorter**.
- The shortened acyl-CoA goes through another turn; the acetyl-CoA goes to the Krebs cycle.
- Last turn of an even-chain fatty acid: the 4 C acyl-CoA is cleaved into **2 acetyl-CoA**. Nothing is wasted: all carbons end as acetyl-CoA and can be fully oxidized in the Krebs cycle.
- **[Notes]** The reactions of each turn:
  1. **Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase** oxidizes C2 and C3, giving **trans-delta2-enoyl-CoA**, with **FAD** as acceptor (FADH2). The electrons pass straight to the respiratory chain through the **electron transfer flavoprotein (ETF)**: 1.5 ATP. Three isozymes by chain length: **VLCAD** (12-18 C), **MCAD** (4-14 C), **SCAD** (4-8 C). (Lucía writes "LCAD" for the first.)
  2. **Enoyl-CoA hydratase** adds H2O to the double bond, giving **L-beta-hydroxyacyl-CoA**.
  3. **Hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase** oxidizes C3 (the beta carbon) to beta-ketoacyl-CoA, with **NAD+** as acceptor (NADH).
  4. **Thiolase** (acyl-CoA acetyltransferase): thiolysis (above).
- **[Notes]** The first three reactions turn a -CH2- into a carbonyl, making the C2-C3 bond unstable. They are analogous to **succinate dehydrogenase, fumarate hydratase and malate dehydrogenase** of the Krebs cycle.
- **[Lucía]** / **[Notes]** Clinical: **MCAD deficiency** is one of the most frequent genetic defects of fatty acid catabolism: fat accumulation in the liver, low blood glucose, vomiting and coma. Other defects in fat oxidation cause serious heart and skeletal muscle disorders. Link to breast milk in [[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]], section 4.

### Peroxisomal oxidation (study notes only; not in the recording)
- **[Notes]** Peroxisomes also oxidize fatty acids, mainly **very long-chain** ones, to shorten them; the shortened chains go to mitochondria (peroxisomes lack Krebs enzymes). Difference: in the first step FADH2 passes electrons directly to O2, making **H2O2**, removed by **catalase**.
- **[Notes]** **Phytanic acid** (branched; dairy, meat, fish) is oxidized there by **alpha-oxidation**.
- **[Notes]** Diseases: **Zellweger syndrome** (no peroxisomes), **X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy** (cannot oxidize very long-chain fatty acids), **Refsum disease** (cannot metabolize phytanic acid).

## 10. Unsaturated and odd-chain fatty acids
### Unsaturated (not examined in detail)
- Oxidized the same way, but the **cis** double bond needs extra steps to be converted to the **trans** form.
- One acyl-CoA dehydrogenase step is skipped (the double bond is already there), so one FADH2 less: **1.5 ATP less** per double bond at that position. Example: palmitoleoyl-CoA (16:1) gives 1.5 ATP less than palmitoyl-CoA (16:0).
- Polyunsaturated fatty acids yield even less energy.

### Odd-chain (lecturer: "not the most important part")
- The last turn starts with a 5 C acyl-CoA and gives **acetyl-CoA + propionyl-CoA (3 C)**.
- Propionyl-CoA also comes from **bacterial fermentation of dietary fibre** in the gut; it has biological activity (receptors) and is considered healthy.
- Fate: converted to **succinyl-CoA**, which enters the Krebs cycle. Do not learn the steps.
- Sources mentioned: plant foods and meat from herbivores.

## 11. Regulation (to be continued in Lec 5.10)
- Synthesis and oxidation of fatty acids are reciprocal: more synthesis, less oxidation, and vice versa.
- Linked to carbohydrate availability: more carbohydrate, more insulin, less glucagon, less mobilization and less oxidation.
- Mechanism: malonyl-CoA inhibits CAT I (section 6c and [[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]]).

## 12. Ketone bodies
- Part of normal physiology: a **small** fraction of the acetyl-CoA from beta-oxidation in the liver is diverted to ketogenesis.
- Three ketone bodies:
  - **Acetoacetate**
  - **Beta-hydroxybutyrate**
  - **Acetone**: volatile, exhaled through the lungs, not useful as fuel.
- **Key point (lecturer: important): ketogenesis rises when there is an imbalance between carbohydrate and fatty acid oxidation.**

### Rationale
1. Acetyl-CoA from beta-oxidation enters the Krebs cycle by condensing with **oxaloacetate** to form citrate, so acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate are needed in similar amounts.
2. When carbohydrate is scarce (fasting, low-carbohydrate diet, uncontrolled type 1 diabetes), the liver makes glucose by **gluconeogenesis**, which pulls oxaloacetate out of the mitochondrion (to PEP, then glucose).
3. Oxaloacetate falls, so acetyl-CoA cannot enter the Krebs cycle and accumulates.
4. The excess acetyl-CoA is converted into ketone bodies, which the liver exports as a **transportable fuel** for peripheral tissues.

**[check]** The recording says this happens with a "huge increase" and an "extreme increase in availability" of carbohydrates. From his own reasoning he means a **lack** of carbohydrate: the study notes say the route is especially intense in **continuous hypoglycemia**, when the liver must make glucose from oxaloacetate; the slides say ketone bodies form **when oxaloacetate is depleted**, which also **frees CoA** so beta-oxidation can continue.

### Ketogenic diet
- Very low carbohydrate, to raise ketone body production. Acceptable if balanced and supervised by a physician.
- Lecturer: low carbohydrate with "high amounts of protein and lipids". **[verify]** the protein part; not addressed in the slides or study notes.
- Risk: acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are **acids**, so high levels lower blood pH (**ketoacidosis**). **[Lucía]** Low-carbohydrate diets can also lead to ketoacidosis.

### Type 1 diabetes
- Blood glucose is high, but the glucose is not available to cells, so the body behaves as if fasting: the liver makes large amounts of ketone bodies to send fuel to peripheral tissues.
- **[Lucía]** / **[Notes]** In untreated diabetes the excess of ketone bodies lowers blood pH (**ketoacidosis**), which can lead to coma; the breath smells of acetone. **[Slides]** Extreme ketosis (untreated diabetes): blood ketone bodies about 90 mg/100 mL (normal under 3), urine 5,000 mg/24 h (normal under 125).
- **[verify]** He said patients "don't have or are insensitive to insulin"; the slides and study notes only say "untreated diabetes".

### What to know
The rationale, not each step. For reference, **[Lucía]** / **[Notes]**:
1. Thiolase condenses 2 acetyl-CoA into **acetoacetyl-CoA**.
2. **HMG-CoA synthase** adds a third acetyl-CoA, forming **HMG-CoA** (same intermediate as in [[Lec 5.11 Cholesterol metabolism and lipid transport]]).
3. HMG-CoA is split into **acetoacetate** + acetyl-CoA. Acetoacetate is reduced to D-beta-hydroxybutyrate (D-beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase) or decarboxylated to acetone.
4. Acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are soluble and reach peripheral tissues (skeletal and cardiac muscle, adrenal cortex; the brain for a short period when glucose is lacking) through the blood. There acetoacetate is activated to acetoacetyl-CoA by **beta-ketoacyl-CoA transferase**.
5. Thiolase cleaves acetoacetyl-CoA into 2 acetyl-CoA, which are oxidized in the Krebs cycle.
