[Slides] Learning goals:
- DNA in the linear genome is packaged into chromatin, and chromatin into chromosomes.
- Chromosomes occupy territories in the nucleus.
- Loops and compartments (formed at different levels of resolution) can be active or inactive.

## 1. The packaging problem
- Nucleus: about 5 micrometres (depends on the cell). Inside it must fit 23 pairs of linear chromosomes.
- Genome: 3,000 million nucleotides (3 x 10^9) per haploid set. Worked out, the whole genome (two copies) is about **2 metres** of very thin double-stranded thread. [Notes] agrees: "two meters of DNA in a diploid cell nucleus".
- Why not just cram it in at random (his "golf ball" demonstration with a piece of cord)?
  - It would waste space and be hard to fit.
  - Mainly, chromatin is not an inert molecule: transcription, replication and other processes run all the time, so the DNA must be accessible at very specific sites and distant elements must interact. An orderly arrangement makes that possible.
  - [Notes] Packaging must be **dynamic and reversible** so that transcription factors and other molecules can reach the DNA.
- Supercoiling (suggested by a student) helps: coiling his 2 m cord around a finger reduced it to about 1 cm. But it is not enough; there are higher levels.
- Key message: packaging is **hierarchical**, with several levels of compaction, ending in long loops of chromatin, and each chromosome occupies its own territory.

## 2. Chromatin and chromosomes
- **Chromatin** = DNA plus all the proteins that help package it densely in the nucleus. It is what is seen under the microscope.
- **Interphase** (G1, S, G2): no chromosomes are visible, only a nucleus with relatively relaxed chromatin. [Lucía] During interphase, chromosomes cannot be observed under the light microscope.
- **Mitosis (metaphase)**: highest level of packaging, about 10,000-fold compaction (lecturer's figure). Only then are chromosomes visible. [Lucía] Mitotic chromosomes are about 100 nm.
- Stained metaphase chromosomes show bands (example on the slide: chromosome 2). You see the two homologs (maternal and paternal), each with two sister chromatids after replication; before replication each homolog has one chromatid.
  - [Notes] Every locus is present in two allelic forms (one paternal, one maternal); after S phase each chromosome is made of two identical sister chromatids.
  - [Lucía] G-banding: lighter bands are less compact, darker bands more compact. p = short arm, q = long arm; bands are named by number.
- Cytogenetics gets its own lecture ([[Lec 9.1 Cytogenetics and genome analysis]]).

## 3. Chromosome territories
- Seen in interphase with **FISH** (fluorescence in situ hybridization), which "paints" the chromatin of each chromosome a different colour; the image is then converted to more contrasted pseudocolours. [Notes] Chromosome paints; see Figure 1 of the class notes.
- The technique was perfected about 20 years ago. It showed that chromatin is not a tangle of crisscrossed chromosomes: **the chromatin of each chromosome occupies its own territory** in the nucleus.
- Territories touch at their boundaries, so some intermingling at the edges is conceivable, but that has to be demonstrated. [Notes] Territories are "largely non-overlapping", with contacts at the boundaries.
- [Lucía] The two homologs do not necessarily occupy neighbouring areas of the nucleus.
- Positions are **not fixed** (chromosome 2 is not always in the same place in every cell or tissue), but there are general rules:

| Rule (lecturer) | Position |
|---|---|
| Large chromosomes | Usually towards the outside |
| Small chromosomes | Usually towards the inside |
| Chromosomes with active chromatin (expressed genes) | Inside, away from the nuclear lamina |
| Chromosomes with many silenced genes | Next to the nuclear lamina |

- Why positions vary between cell types: a skin cell and a neuron have the same genes but have activated or repressed different sets. A chromosome with many genes active in skin but not in brain might move towards the interior or towards the lamina depending on the cell type.

## 4. First level: nucleosomes and the 10 nm fiber
- The double helix is wound around a protein complex, the **nucleosome**: about **145 nucleotides**, **one and three-quarter turns** (lecturer's figures). Then the next nucleosome, and the next.
- Electron microscopy: "**beads on a string**" (like a pearl necklace). [Lucía] "modelo del collar de perlas".
- Nucleosome core = **histone octamer** = two identical tetramers, each made of **H2A, H2B, H3, H4**. [Notes] Two molecules of each of the four histones form the octamer (Figure 2).
- **Histone H1** sits outside and helps stabilize the nucleosome. [Lucía] H1 acts as a linker, giving stability between nucleosomes.
- This level is the **10 nm fiber** (10 nm thick).
- [Lucía] Histones have tails whose amino acids undergo modifications (see [[Lec 8.3 Epigenetics]]).

## 5. 30 nm fiber and the solenoid
- The 10 nm fiber is further bent and coiled. One of the first models was the **solenoid** (like copper wire wound round a cylinder to make an electromagnet), originally called the **30 nm fiber**.
- **[check]** Whether the solenoid exists in vivo:
  - Lecturer: more refined recent techniques suggest the solenoid is apparently **not** what happens in vivo; whatever the exact configuration, the fiber ends up folding into loops.
  - [Notes]: thanks to chromosome conformation capture techniques "we know that the 10-nm fiber is wound following the structure of a solenoid".
  - [Lucía]: second level = solenoid, giving the 30 nm fiber; third level = further coiling up to mitotic chromosomes.
  - The slide text does not settle it. Ask or check the slide figure.
- [Notes] The techniques used to study the spatial relationship of sequences are collectively called **chromosome conformation capture**.

## 6. Loops, TADs and compartments
- Scales (figure from a recent review, see the slide): nucleosomal scale, supranucleosomal scale (loops), nuclear scale (chromosome territories). The middle level was a "black box" until recent research.
- **TADs** = **topologically associating domains**: regions of DNA that associate in space. Inside a TAD, chromatin forms loops so distant parts can touch and interact physically. [Notes] Typically **500 kb** on average, with several loops of various sizes inside each TAD.
- TADs come in two "flavours":
  - **A compartment** = active TADs (active chromatin).
  - **B compartment** = inactive TADs, close to the nuclear lamina.
  - Active and inactive TADs carry different chemical modifications, which helps them cluster with their own kind ("like phase separation"). In the linear genome A and B TADs may alternate, but in 3D they cluster.
- **Compartment** = several TADs in the same functional state. Many compartments (active and inactive) make up the chromatin of one chromosome territory.
- [Notes] Compartments are about **5 Mb** and contain different TADs in different cell types. Compartments in the centre of the nucleus and on the outside of chromosome territories are generally active; those near the lamina or in the interior of territories are generally inactive.
- **[check]** Hierarchy: [Lucía] calls the TAD "the largest type of compartment in the hierarchy". The lecturer and [Notes] put compartments above TADs (a compartment contains several TADs). Follow the lecturer and the class notes.
- Result: chromosomes are isolated from each other, and inside each chromosome some regions are isolated from others.
- **TADs vs loops** (lecturer, agrees with [Notes]):

| | TADs | Loops inside TADs |
|---|---|---|
| Stability | Relatively fixed and stable during the life of a cell; similar between cells and tissues | Dynamic, fluid; change with gene expression |
| [Notes] | Highly conserved across cell types and species | Usually change during the cell cycle |

- Example: if a gene needs a given enhancer in one cell type, loops change to bring them together; in another cell type where the gene is silent, other elements are brought together instead. [Notes] Figure 4: different loop configurations allow different promoter-enhancer contacts.
- [Slides] Figure labels: metaphase chromosome 17, interaction frequency (low to high), TAD #1 and TAD #2. See the slide.

## 7. How TADs are made: loop extrusion (cohesin and CTCF)
- Current model: **loop extrusion**. Analogy: folding a thread and passing the fold through the eye of a needle, then pulling, makes a long loop.
- **Cohesin** extrudes chromatin. It is the ring complex seen in the meiosis class. It acts like a molecular motor, pulling chromatin through the ring to form a loop; smaller sub-loops form inside.
  - Components: SMC proteins (**structural maintenance of chromosomes**), e.g. SMC3, plus a protein transcribed as "Rav21". **[verify]** Check the name of this cohesin subunit; no vault source gives it. "Everything is in the notes", he says, but the class notes do not list the subunits.
  - Classroom demonstration with student volunteers as chromatin and a ring as cohesin.
- **CTCF**: a protein that binds a specific DNA sequence (about 20 to 30 nucleotides by the lecturer's estimate). Its sites are scattered throughout the genome. **[verify]** The expansion of the name is garbled in the transcript ("the CTCF binding factor"); no vault source gives it.
  - [Notes] Binding sequence: 5'-CCGCGNGGNGGC(G/A)(C/G)(T/C)-3'.
- **When extrusion stops**: when cohesin reaches **two CTCF molecules bound in opposite orientation** (pointing towards each other). They clash and extrusion stops. That defines the **boundaries of a TAD**: CTCF, CTCF and cohesin at the base of the TAD.
  - CTCF sites in the **same** orientation do not form a TAD boundary together. This is why a diagram can show three CTCF sites in one TAD: a site can only pair with one in the opposite orientation (arrows on the slide show orientation).
  - So there can be smaller TADs inside larger ones (sub-TADs, meta-TADs; names on the slide).
- In the linear genome the two CTCF sites are far apart; in the 3D TAD they are next to each other, with loops in between.
- [Notes] CTCF can **dimerize** when bound to different DNA sequences, mediating long-range looping. CTCF also positions nucleosomes and delimits heterochromatin, which tends to spread.
- [Lucía] CTCF behaves as an **insulator**; it forms a dimer that flanks compartments at the boundaries.

## 8. Why it matters: contacts and insulation
- **Bringing elements together.** Example (lecturer's): the insulin gene and an **enhancer** that may be a million nucleotides away. The enhancer must physically interact with the gene's promoter for insulin to be made. Only loop folding makes that possible. "Without this we are dead."
  - [Notes] Segments in the same TAD can contact each other even though they may be 1 to 2 Mb apart in the linear genome.
- **Insulation.** Example: gene A (insulin) with enhancer A (makes it pancreas-specific) and gene B (hemoglobin) with enhancer B (red-blood-cell-specific). If enhancer A reached gene B, you would have hemoglobin in the pancreas; applied to all genes, chaos.
  - Each gene and its enhancer sit in **their own TAD**. The two CTCF molecules form a **boundary element**: the enhancer cannot cross it.
  - Contacts between TADs are very infrequent but not impossible; contacts inside a TAD are favoured.
  - Second example from the slides: a mouse embryo gene expressed only in brain and one only in liver, separated into different TADs by CTCF sites (see the slide).
- [Notes] Insulators = the DNA sequences at TAD boundaries, which are CTCF binding sites.
- [Lucía] Disrupting a TAD border can cause disease: it creates bigger TADs and therefore more (aberrant) interactions, e.g. a brain enhancer contacting a liver gene.

## 9. Summary of the hierarchy (lecturer's closing recap)

| Level | Structure |
|---|---|
| 1 | DNA double helix |
| 2 | Nucleosomes, 10 nm fiber ("beads on a string") |
| 3 | 30 nm fiber (solenoid model; see [check] in section 5) |
| 4 | Loops inside TADs (dynamic) |
| 5 | TADs (stable; bounded by CTCF and cohesin) |
| 6 | A and B compartments (clusters of TADs) |
| 7 | Chromosome territories |
| Mitosis | Metaphase chromosome, highest compaction |

- Throughout, proteins keep accessing the chromatin; this overview is needed to understand gene regulation and "all of genetics".
