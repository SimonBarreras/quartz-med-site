## 1. Overview
- Cancer is a genetic disease, also influenced by external and environmental factors.
- Two families of genes are responsible: **oncogenes** and **tumour suppressor genes**.
- Plan: the two concepts; how oncogenes are generated and how tumour suppressor genes are mutated; drugs that target some oncogenes.
- Opening image: a breast cancer specimen, immunohistochemistry for **HER2**: huge expression, "this is what is driving this tumour". HER2 stimulates proliferation, so these tumours are highly aggressive.

## 2. Oncogenes
- **Oncogenes** = abnormal versions of **proto-oncogenes**. Proto-oncogenes = genes coding for **cell-cycle-promoting proteins**: normal function is to stimulate the cell cycle.
- Examples: **EGFR** (membrane receptor binding EGF, epidermal growth factor; stimulates proliferation), **HER2** (membrane protein, stimulates proliferation), and all proteins that move the cell cycle forward, e.g. **cyclins and CDKs** (G1 to S).
- Problem: **gain of function**: "they function too much".
- **Mutation types (EGFR as the example):**

| | Inactivating mutation | Hyperactivating (activating) mutation |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Most mutations, for EGFR and "for the whole genome" | Rare |
| Effect on protein | Messes up the protein; sometimes it disappears | Protein not destroyed; more active than normal |
| For EGFR | No problem for cancer: cells won't divide, or die | Receptor sends too many signals for cell division |
| Setting | | Lung cancer: many patients with EGFR-mutated tumours |

- **Oncogenes are dominant**: a mutation in **one allele** is enough to hyperstimulate the cell.
- [Lucía] EGFR hyperactivating mutations are present in 10-15% of lung cancers.

## 3. Tumour suppressor genes
- The opposite concept: genes coding for proteins that **slow down or stop** the cell cycle. Examples: **p53, p21, Rb**.

| Protein | Role (lecturer) |
|---|---|
| p53 | Apoptosis (student answer); regulates the DNA damage response: stops the cell cycle when needed so DNA can be repaired; if not repairable, apoptosis |
| p21 | Regulated (induced) by p53; a **CDK-cyclin inhibitor**: blocks the kinase activity of e.g. CDK4-cyclin D, so the cycle stops after DNA damage; once the damage is fixed, p21 expression disappears and the cycle continues |
| Rb | Blocks **E2F** and controls cell proliferation; if Rb is mutated, cells proliferate (tumours) |
- Mutations very frequent in **p53** ("the most mutated of the three"), also quite frequent in p21 and Rb.
- **Tumour suppressor genes are recessive**: usually **both alleles** must be mutated to lose the activity; with one mutation, the other copy usually compensates.
- [Lucía] Rb is bound to E2F; when phosphorylated it releases E2F, which acts as a transcription factor. Having one dysfunctional allele makes the other more prone to mutation and silencing.

## 4. How proto-oncogenes become oncogenes
| Mechanism | What happens | His example |
|---|---|---|
| **Point mutation** | Hyperactive protein | EGFR (section 2) |
| **Gene amplification** | Instead of two copies (two alleles), "five, six, ten, a hundred copies"; the sequence is normal, no mutation; overproduction of the protein; same effect: hyperactivity, signalling dysregulated, cells divide all the time | **HER2** in breast cancer (also ovarian cancer sometimes, and others, but mainly breast) |
| **Chromosome rearrangement: viral insertion near the promoter** | A virus infects the cell and inserts its DNA in the **promoter region** of a proto-oncogene ("it's not just an image, this happens"); expression is altered: many copies of mRNA and protein instead of normal regulated production | Gene A (generic) |
| **Chromosome rearrangement: fusion protein** | A translocation joins part of gene B (from another chromosome) to gene A: mixed mRNA and a **fusion protein**. Often abnormal and non-functional (the cell may die: "Who cares?"); the problem is when the fusion protein is **hyperactive** | **BCR-ABL** |
- **BCR-ABL:** the **Philadelphia chromosome**, from a translocation between chromosomes **9 and 22** (portions exchanged both ways), producing a small abnormal chromosome and a hyperactive fusion protein stimulating pathways of cell division. Very common in **chronic myeloid leukaemia**: the cause in a large percentage of patients. (See [[Lec 9.1 Cytogenetics and genome analysis]].)
- [Lucía] lists "deletions or point mutations in the coding sequence" as mechanism 1, and for the rearrangement, "relocation of a promoter region" near the gene.

## 5. How tumour suppressor genes are inactivated
| Mechanism | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Point mutation** | Non-functional gene (e.g. p53) |
| **Chromosome loss** (of a piece) | A missing region containing many genes, e.g. including p53, which is then absent |
| **Promoter methylation** | DNA methylation of the promoter silences the gene: the gene is normal and present, but no protein is made |
- Because both alleles must be affected, **combinations** inactivate the two copies: two point mutations (one per chromosome), point mutation plus chromosomal deletion, promoter methylation plus point mutation. Any combination affecting both alleles transforms a normal cell into a tumour cell.
- [Lucía] lists four: inactivating point mutations, chromosome deletion, chromosome loss (of a whole chromosome), silencing by DNA methylation.

## 6. EGFR: targeted therapy, oncogenic addiction and resistance
### The video (narration of a trial summary; "you don't have to learn anything" from it)
- A point mutation in the EGFR coding region, **T790M**, reduces binding of EGFR-TKI therapy to the receptor: less inhibition, cancer progression.
- **Osimertinib**: oral, irreversible EGFR-TKI, selective for both EGFR and T790M resistance variants, active against central nervous system metastases.
- Trial: 419 patients with T790M-positive advanced non-small cell lung cancer who progressed after prior EGFR-TKI therapy; 144 (34%) with asymptomatic CNS metastases; randomized to osimertinib daily vs IV platinum plus "hemotrexate" chemotherapy (as transcribed) every 3 weeks for 6 cycles, then "hemotrexate" maintenance. **[verify]** the name of the second chemotherapy drug.
- Median progression-free survival 10.1 months (osimertinib) vs 4.4 months (chemotherapy), hazard ratio 0.30. CNS metastases subgroup: 8.5 vs 4.2 months. Side effects: diarrhoea, rash, dry skin; severe myelotoxicity less common than with chemotherapy. Results at NEJM.org.
- His summary: osimertinib blocks the specific mutated EGFR found in lung cancer patients; it is better than chemotherapy and is used now in patients.
- [Lucía] First-line drug **gefitinib**, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI): blocks EGFR kinase activity and signalling. Up to 60% develop the T790M resistance mutation in the kinase domain, so the drug cannot bind. Osimertinib binds normal and mutated kinase domains and crosses the blood-brain barrier. Drugs ending in **-ib** are chemical compounds; **-ab** are therapeutic antibodies. (None of this is in the transcript.)

### Oncogenic addiction
- For some oncogenic mutations (e.g. EGFR), the tumour depends very much on the mutation: it is "addicted" to it.
- Blocking the mutated receptor makes the tumour **collapse**: a very intense response, massive cell death. Does not apply to all oncogenes, but to many.
- Response: the tumour shrinks very much, sometimes disappears; often something remains on the CT scan, but the response is "quite impressive".

### Resistance
- In most patients, after several months, the tumour **grows again**.
- In the clinic, EGFR is **sequenced** at the beginning for all such patients: no mutation, the therapy makes no sense; mutation, eligible for the drug.
- Sequencing the relapsed tumour often shows **another EGFR mutation** with the same effect (signals for division) that the drug does not affect.
- Origin (probably; "unknown at this point"): a small **resistant clone** with the second mutation already in the original tumour, while the dominant clone drove the original growth. The drug eliminates the dominant clone; the minor clone grows. Industry must then design a new drug for the resistant protein.
- Very common for cancer therapies in general, more specifically for **targeted therapy**.

| | Targeted therapy | Chemotherapy |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | One specific protein | All proliferating cells |
| His image | | "like a venom, like a poison" |

## 7. HER2 in breast cancer (second file)
- [Lucía] (gap fill) 25-30% of breast cancer patients overexpress HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2), in primary tumours and metastatic sites.

### The HER2 video (narration)
- HER2 membrane overexpression in cancer cells is much higher than in normal adult tissue; overexpression is found in primary tumour and metastases: rationale for anti-HER2 therapy at all disease sites.
- **Trastuzumab:** the first HER2 inhibitor registered for HER2-overexpressing breast cancer; a **recombinant monoclonal antibody** against the extracellular domain of HER2 (binds **domain 4**). Approved in the metastatic and adjuvant settings. Proposed mechanisms: antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, preventing formation of a truncated membrane-bound HER2 fragment, receptor downregulation; main mechanism thought to be binding to HER2 and inhibition of HER2 signal transduction.
- **Pertuzumab:** fully humanized monoclonal antibody binding **domain 2** (extracellular, essential for dimerization); sterically blocks ligand-induced homodimerization and HER2-HER3 heterodimerization (which activates survival signalling, e.g. PI3K-AKT). Trastuzumab has only a minor effect in the presence of ligand.
- Approved mid-2012 with trastuzumab and **docetaxel** for metastatic or locally recurrent unresectable HER2-positive breast cancer without prior anti-HER2 therapy or chemotherapy for metastatic disease.

### His comments
- Approved combination: two blocking antibodies on different domains plus docetaxel ("this taxane", one of the drugs that block the microtubules; transcript "macrubule decolonization", **[verify]** what he said about the mechanism). A trial combining them gave better response and overall survival.
- Cancer therapy usually combines drugs, but there is a **balance**: more drugs may work better, but toxicity can rise "exponentially" and become unbearable.
- **Antibodies bound to chemotherapy** (new drugs "combining antibodies bound to chemotherapy"; "a revolution also in oncology", "a very hot topic"; already approved): chemotherapy molecules bound to the antibody (e.g. trastuzumab). Injected in blood, the antibody goes quickly to the tumour and binds HER2; the cell internalizes HER2 + antibody + chemotherapy by **endocytosis**; endosome, lysosome; the drug is released inside the cell, blocks microtubules and the tumour cell collapses. Both effects: HER2 blocking plus targeted chemotherapy, avoiding chemotherapy circulating everywhere and hitting all dividing cells.
- [Lucía] The conjugated toxin is called **TDM1** (with trastuzumab); causes cell cycle arrest and apoptosis by inhibiting microtubule assembly.

## 8. Testing HER2: immunohistochemistry and FISH
- Before anti-HER2 therapy you must show the target is present.
- **Immunohistochemistry** first (easier): strong membrane staining in the primary tumour = eligible.
- **FISH** for gene amplification: two DNA probes, e.g. **green** for the **centromere** of the chromosome and **red** for **HER2**.
  - Normal cells: two green and two red signals per cell.
  - Some cells show only one or no signal: the tissue is a **section**, so part of the nucleus is not captured.
  - Amplification: many red dots per cell.
- Practice: IHC first; if the result is unclear ("superior" in the transcript, **[verify]**) they do FISH. With three or four copies the result is doubtful; with 20 or 100 copies, the woman is going to respond.
- [Lucía] The centromere probe labels **chromosome 17**, where HER2 is; cancer cells typically show one green dot followed by many red ones. **[check]** The lecturer expects two green signals per cell (fewer only from sectioning); Lucía describes one green dot.
