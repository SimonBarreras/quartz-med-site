## 1. Why we synthesize lipids
- Mainly in the **liver** (also the main site of glycogen synthesis), though all cells can make lipids.
- Uses:
  - **Energy storage** (TAG).
  - **Membrane lipids**: glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids (both contain fatty acids).
  - **Lipid anchors** for peripheral membrane proteins, e.g. farnesyl and palmitoyl groups. Examples: G-alpha and G-beta-gamma of heterotrimeric G proteins are anchored, not floating in the cytosol; **Ras** (an oncogene, at the start of signalling cascades) is held at the membrane by lipid anchors.
  - **Signalling molecules**: membrane phosphatidylinositol (PIP2) is the source of **IP3**, which triggers **Ca2+ release**.
- Link to cholesterol: the more acetyl-CoA accumulates (instead of being oxidized in the Krebs cycle), the more cholesterol and fatty acid can be made. More carbohydrate, more acetyl-CoA, more fatty acid and more cholesterol synthesis.
- **[Notes]** Other lipid roles: pigments (retinal, carotenes), cofactors (vitamin K), detergents (bile salts), hormones (vitamin D derivatives, steroids), messengers (inositols, eicosanoids), membrane anchors (myristoyl, palmitoyl). Lipid synthesis is endergonic and uses reducing power (NADPH). Most active synthesis: **liver** and **lactating mammary gland**.

## 2. Raw materials
- **Carbon source: acetyl-CoA**, from glucose oxidation (glycolysis + PDH) or beta-oxidation. Excess carbohydrate that cannot be stored as glycogen becomes fatty acids, then TAG.
- **Reducing power: NADPH.**
- **Energy: ATP** (for ACC).

### When acetyl-CoA is diverted to synthesis
- High **NADH/NAD+** and **ATP/ADP** ratios mean plenty of energy: time to store, not oxidize. These ratios are the main regulators of the Krebs cycle and partially inhibit it.
- As a result citrate and acetyl-CoA accumulate and are available for synthesis.
- The lecturer said citrate synthase activity falls, while his next step depends on citrate accumulating. **[Lucía]** When the ATP/ADP and NADH/NAD+ ratios are high the Krebs cycle slows, and **acetyl-CoA and citrate accumulate** in the mitochondria; citrate is then released to the cytoplasm. Lucía's Krebs section lists citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase among the regulated enzymes.

### Hormonal state (insulin vs glucagon)
- **[check]** Transcript says "the ratio between insulin and glucose"; it is the **insulin/glucagon** ratio (as in Lec 5.9).
- High insulin: PDH and ACC are very active, so glucose carbons go to acetyl-CoA and then to fatty acids.
- High insulin also "silences" the glucagon pathway in fat tissue: low cAMP, low PKA, low HSL activity, little TAG hydrolysis.

## 3. Moving acetyl-CoA out of the mitochondrion
Fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis happen in the **cytosol**, but most acetyl-CoA is in the mitochondrial matrix, and there is **no acetyl-CoA transporter** in the inner membrane.

### Citrate shuttle
1. Matrix: acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate to **citrate** (citrate synthase, as in Krebs).
2. Citrate leaves via the **tricarboxylate (citrate) transporter** of the inner membrane.
3. Cytosol: **citrate lyase** splits citrate into acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate (the reverse of citrate synthase).
4. Oxaloacetate must return, or the shuttle stops. Two routes:
   - Oxaloacetate is reduced to **malate** (malate dehydrogenase, uses NADH), and malate enters the mitochondrion through a malate transporter.
   - Or malate is converted by **malic enzyme** to **pyruvate** + CO2, producing **NADPH**. Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion through its transporter and **pyruvate carboxylase** turns it back into oxaloacetate.
- **[check]** The transcript places pyruvate carboxylase in the cytoplasm; the study notes and Lucía place it in the **mitochondria** (activated by high acetyl-CoA).
- **[Notes]** Cost: the shuttle consumes **2 ATP per acetyl-CoA** exported (one at citrate lyase, one at pyruvate carboxylase).

### The NADH-to-NADPH conversion
Adding malate dehydrogenase + malic enzyme: electrons taken from **NADH** end up in **NADPH**. This is a second source of NADPH, besides the pentose phosphate pathway.

### NADPH sources
- **Pentose phosphate pathway**: main source in hepatocytes; also in mammary glands.
- **Malic enzyme**.
- **[Notes]** Malic enzyme supplies about **half** of the NADPH for fatty acid synthesis; the rest comes from the pentose phosphate pathway.

## 4. Lactation link: medium-chain fatty acids and MCAD deficiency
- During pregnancy and lactation the mammary glands synthesize lots of fatty acids; human milk is rich in **medium-chain** fatty acids.
- Connection to remember: babies with **MCAD deficiency** (medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, a beta-oxidation enzyme) cannot obtain energy from medium-chain fatty acids, which is dangerous on a milk diet.
- Why medium-chain: **prolactin** rises in pregnancy and activates the **thioesterase** (TE) of fatty acid synthase early, so chains are released at 12 or 14 C instead of 16. Only in mammary glands; the liver in women still makes palmitate. Men and women have the same fatty acid synthase. **[Notes]** Maternal milk contains fatty acids shorter than 16 C because, with the hormonal profile of pregnancy and lactation, TE acts earlier.

## 5. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC): the key step
"The most important part of this topic."

**Acetyl-CoA + HCO3- + ATP to malonyl-CoA + ADP + Pi**

- Activation of acetyl-CoA by **carboxylation** of its methyl group, so units can be joined.
- The carboxyl comes from CO2 after hydration to **bicarbonate**.
- Cofactor: **biotin** (vitamin), as in other carboxylases.
- **[Notes]** The reaction is **irreversible** and is the **rate-limiting step** of fatty acid synthesis. ACC has **three activities in one multifunctional polypeptide**; biotin is bound to the epsilon-amino group of a lysine (like pyruvate carboxylase).

### Malonyl-CoA: the switch
- Malonyl-CoA **inhibits CAT I** (carnitine acyltransferase I), so long-chain fatty acids cannot enter the mitochondrion: **beta-oxidation stops**.
- CAT I activity is regulated only by malonyl-CoA.
- So: **more malonyl-CoA = more fatty acid synthesis and less fatty acid oxidation.** Both pathways never run at full speed at the same time.

## 6. Regulation of ACC
### Allosteric
| Effector | Effect | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Citrate** | Activates | Citrate in the cytosol means the mitochondria are full of energy. **[Notes]** Citrate also inhibits **PFK-1**, slowing glycolysis |
| **Palmitoyl-CoA** | Inhibits | Feedback: palmitate (16 C) is the main end product |

### Covalent (phosphorylation; very important in the liver)
- **Dephosphorylated = active. Phosphorylated = inactive.** Same strategy as PDH.
- **Glucagon** (low carbohydrate): GPCR, G-alpha-s, adenylyl cyclase, cAMP, **PKA**, which **phosphorylates and inactivates ACC**.
  - Less malonyl-CoA, so CAT I is released and more fatty acids enter the mitochondria for oxidation.
  - At the same time, in adipose tissue glucagon activates HSL and fatty acids are mobilized to peripheral tissues.
- **Insulin** (after a meal, high carbohydrate): activates a **phosphatase** that dephosphorylates ACC, making it very active.
  - Acetyl-CoA from the meal ("the cake") becomes malonyl-CoA, which inhibits CAT I and feeds fatty acid synthesis, then TAG or cholesterol.
  - This is why excess carbohydrate after a meal is mostly converted to fat.
- **[Lucía]** In muscle, ACC is additionally phosphorylated and inactivated by **AMPK**. Epinephrine, like glucagon, activates PKA and inhibits ACC.
- Clinical link: one of the connections between **obesity and hypercholesterolemia** (excess carbohydrate drives both fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis).

### Summary of the two states
| | Fed (high insulin) | Fasting (high glucagon) |
|---|---|---|
| ACC | Dephosphorylated, active | Phosphorylated by PKA, inactive |
| Malonyl-CoA | High | Low |
| CAT I | Inhibited | Active |
| Fatty acid synthesis | On | Off |
| Beta-oxidation | Off | On |
| HSL (adipose) | Off | On |

## 7. Fatty acid synthase (overview only)
- Human type I fatty acid synthase: one large complex with **seven activities**. **[Notes]** Precisely: one multifunctional polypeptide with **six active sites** plus a seventh domain without catalytic function, the **ACP**, whose **phosphopantetheine** prosthetic group anchors the growing chain.

| Acronym | Activity |
|---|---|
| KS | beta-ketoacyl-ACP synthase (condensation) |
| MAT | malonyl/acetyl-CoA-ACP transferase (loads substrates) |
| KR | beta-ketoacyl-ACP reductase |
| DH | beta-hydroxyacyl-ACP **de**hydratase |
| ER | enoyl-ACP reductase |
| ACP | acyl carrier protein (carrier, not an enzyme) |
| TE | thioesterase (releases the product) |

- **ACP** carries a **phosphopantetheine arm** that moves the growing chain between the active sites in each cycle.
- **[check]** Transcript calls DH a "hydrate activity" and TE "tireless trait"; the cycle includes a **dehydration** step (Lucía) and Lucía names the **TE domain**, which releases palmitate as a thioesterase.
- **[Lucía]** The ACP "arm" is part of coenzyme A; its function is to keep the fatty acid bound during the whole process.

### Each cycle: four reactions, the chemical reverse of beta-oxidation
| | Fatty acid synthesis | Beta-oxidation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Condensation | Oxidation (FAD) |
| 2 | Reduction (NADPH) | Hydration |
| 3 | **Dehydration** | Oxidation (NAD+) |
| 4 | Reduction (NADPH) | Thiolysis |

**[check]** The recording pairs synthesis "hydration" with beta-oxidation "dehydration". Lucía and the study notes give synthesis as condensation, reduction, **dehydration**, reduction, and beta-oxidation uses a hydratase (enoyl-CoA hydratase, [[Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies]]). So synthesis dehydrates and beta-oxidation hydrates; the steps appear in reverse order.

### First cycle in detail **[Notes]**
0. Loading: MAT transfers the acetyl group of acetyl-CoA to the KS thiol, and the malonyl group of malonyl-CoA to the ACP thiol.
1. **Condensation** (KS): malonyl is decarboxylated (the CO2 released is the one added by ACC) and its methylene attacks the acetyl on KS, giving **acetoacetyl-ACP** (beta-ketobutyryl-ACP).
2. **Reduction** (KR): the C3 carbonyl is reduced by **NADPH**, giving **beta-hydroxybutyryl-ACP**.
3. **Dehydration** (DH): water is removed between C2 and C3, giving **trans-delta2-butenoyl-ACP**.
4. **Reduction** (ER): the double bond is reduced by **NADPH**, giving **butyryl-ACP** (4 C).
- Butyryl is moved to KS so ACP can take the next malonyl; cycles repeat until 16 C, when **TE** releases palmitate.

### Products
- Main product: **palmitate (16:0)**, released as palmitate by TE (hence palmitoyl-CoA as the feedback inhibitor of ACC).
- Additional enzymes elongate it to stearate (18 C), 20 C, etc. **[Notes]** **Elongases** in the smooth ER and mitochondria, with the same chemistry as fatty acid synthase.
- **Unsaturated fatty acids**: we can introduce double bonds (do not learn the chemistry), but **not beyond C9**. So **linoleic** and **alpha-linolenic** acids are **essential** and must come from the diet.
- **[Notes]** **Fatty acyl-CoA desaturases** introduce cis double bonds by an oxidative reaction. Palmitate and stearate give **palmitoleate (16:1 delta9)** and **oleate (18:1 delta9)**. Mammals can add a double bond at delta9 but not beyond **C10** (the lecturer said C9), so they cannot make **linoleic (18:2 delta9,12)** or **alpha-linolenic (18:3 delta9,12,15)**; plants can. These are precursors of **arachidonic acid (20:4)**, the precursor of **eicosanoids** (prostaglandins, prostacyclins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes).
- **[check]** Transcript says "essential amino acids"; Lucía's notes: **linoleic and linolenic acid are essential fatty acids**, obtained from the diet.

### Overall balance **[Lucía]**
- Activation: 7 acetyl-CoA + 7 ATP + 7 CO2 to 7 malonyl-CoA + 7 ADP + 7 Pi.
- Synthesis: 7 malonyl-CoA + 1 acetyl-CoA + 14 NADPH + 14 H+ to palmitate + 8 CoA + 7 CO2 + 14 NADP+.
- Net: **8 acetyl-CoA + 7 ATP + 14 NADPH + 14 H+ to palmitate + 14 NADP+ + 8 CoA + 6 H2O**. A high-energy process, only carried out when possible. The study notes give the same balance.

## 8. TAG synthesis (study notes only; "not in depth" per the lecturer)
- **[Notes]** Fatty acids go either to **TAG** (storage) or to **phospholipids** (membranes). During growth (childhood, adolescence) much goes to phospholipids; afterwards most becomes TAG in adipose tissue.
- **[Notes]** Highest TAG synthesis: liver and adipose tissue. We store about **15 kg of TAG** against about **400 g of glycogen**.
- **[Notes]** Precursors: **glycerol 3-phosphate** (mostly from **DHAP** of glycolysis; in liver and kidney also from glycerol via **glycerol kinase**) and **fatty acyl-CoA** (made by the acyl-CoA synthetases of [[Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies]]).
- **[Notes]** C1 and C2 of G3P are acylated, giving **phosphatidic acid**, precursor of both TAG and glycerophospholipids. For TAG it is dephosphorylated to **DAG** and acylated at C3.
- **[Notes]** **Insulin** promotes conversion of glucose to fatty acids and TAG. Insulin-dependent diabetics cannot use glucose or make fatty acids from it, and have high fat oxidation and ketone body production.
- **[Notes]** **Triacylglycerol cycle**: about 75% of fatty acids released by lipolysis are re-esterified to TAG (partly in adipose tissue, partly in the liver after travelling in blood), keeping a steady pool of circulating fatty acids ready for energy needs.

Final message from the lecturer: what matters most is connecting glucose oxidation, fatty acid oxidation and fatty acid biosynthesis through ACC regulation.
