- The urea cycle steps in outline; three enzymes highlighted: **carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I** (regulation), **arginase** (makes urea), **ornithine transcarbamoylase** (most common defect).
- Diseases are used only as examples to understand normal physiology: "this session is not about diseases". Do not memorize disease names or symptoms; read and understand the table of genetic disorders.
- Cofactors, because their deficiencies have clinical consequences.

## 1. When are amino acids degraded?
- We **do not store proteins** (or nucleic acids), probably because the body does not want free amino groups around.
- Physiological:
  - Continuous **protein turnover** in cells: some amino acids are reused, the rest catabolized.
  - After a **protein-rich meal**.
- Pathological:
  - **Starvation**: amino acids are a main carbon source for making glucose, so proteins are degraded fast.
  - **Diabetes**: the body has glucose, but without the insulin signal cells respond as in starvation and degrade proteins to make glucose.
- **[Lucía]** Nitrogen compounds are not stored, so nitrogen must be supplied continuously in the diet (**nitrogen balance**: intake = excretion).

## 2. General strategy
1. **Hydrolysis** of proteins to amino acids by proteases (in the intestine for dietary protein, inside cells for endogenous protein): stage 1 of catabolism.
2. In most amino acids, the **amino group is separated** from the carbon skeleton, which becomes an **alpha-keto acid** (keto group on the alpha carbon).
3. The amino group is not released as free ammonium (toxic) but **transferred to alpha-ketoglutarate** (a Krebs intermediate; 5 C, carboxyl at both ends), which becomes **glutamate**. Glutamate collects the amino groups of all amino acids.
4. Amino groups are then either **reused** (e.g. glutamine donates amino groups for nucleotide synthesis) or **eliminated as urea** when not needed (e.g. after a high-protein meal).
5. Alpha-keto acids feed pathways around the Krebs cycle: energy, glucose, ketone bodies (section 8).

### Transamination
- Amino acid + alpha-ketoglutarate to alpha-keto acid + glutamate. Enzyme: **aminotransferase** (= transaminase).
- Amino groups are constantly moving between amino acids by transamination.
- Clinical: **transaminases** are measured in blood tests; liver or heart damage releases them into circulation.
- **[Lucía]** Transaminations are **reversible** (used in both degradation and synthesis); transaminases are cytoplasmic and need **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, from vitamin B6. Named examples: **AST** (aspartate transaminase) and **ALT** (alanine aminotransferase).

## 3. How animals excrete nitrogen
| Strategy | Product | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ammonotelic | Ammonium | Fish | Plenty of water to dilute it |
| **Ureotelic** | **Urea** | **Humans** | Not enough water to dilute ammonium |
| Uricotelic | Uric acid | Birds | Cannot afford to lose water |

- **Urea**: 2 nitrogen atoms per molecule, low toxicity, water-soluble. Made in the **liver**, released into blood, filtered by the **kidneys** into urine.

## 4. Transport of amino groups to the liver
All tissues degrade proteins, but only the liver makes urea, so nitrogen must travel to the liver.

### Glutamine (most tissues)
- Glutamate picks up a further amino group and becomes **glutamine** (two amino groups: alpha carbon and side chain).
- Glutamine is the **most abundant amino acid in blood**.
- **[Lucía]** Glutamine is a non-toxic carrier and also an amino donor for many biosyntheses. In liver mitochondria, **glutaminase** releases NH4+ from glutamine (giving glutamate), and **glutamate dehydrogenase** releases a second NH4+ from glutamate; the resulting alpha-ketoglutarate goes to the Krebs cycle or gluconeogenesis.

### Alanine (muscle): glucose-alanine cycle
1. During vigorous activity muscle makes a lot of **pyruvate** from glycolysis.
2. Pyruvate is **transaminated to alanine**. This removes pyruvate (keeping glycolysis going) and exports amino groups at the same time.
3. Alanine goes to the liver, where its carbon is used to make **glucose**, which returns to the muscle.
- Alanine is the **second most abundant amino acid in blood**.
- **[check]** The transcript says "in the liver, many of the pyruvates ... are transaminated into alanine"; the context and Lucía's notes put this step in **muscle** (enzyme **ALT**), and the reverse (alanine to pyruvate) in liver cytosol.

## 5. The urea cycle
- Discovered by **Krebs** (with another researcher), the same scientist as the Krebs cycle; the two cycles are connected. **[Slides]** Hans Krebs and **Kurt Henseleit, 1932**.
- Almost exclusively in the **liver**; **two compartments** (mitochondria and cytosol); **five steps**.
- Nitrogen sources: one N from **ammonium** (from glutamine/glutamate), the other from **aspartate**.

### Step 0/1: carbamoyl phosphate (mitochondria)
- Free NH4+ is toxic, so it reacts very fast and efficiently with **CO2 (as bicarbonate)** and **ATP** to form **carbamoyl phosphate**.
- Enzyme: **carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS I)**. "I" because a CPS II appears in nucleotide metabolism.
- Plenty of energy is spent so the reaction always runs forward. **[Lucía]** It uses **2 ATP**.
- Parallel with Krebs: before the cycle comes the "entry" molecule (acetyl-CoA for Krebs, carbamoyl phosphate here).
- Naming rule (lecturer): enzymes called **synthetase** use ATP; **synthase** typically does not. **[check]** The transcript has the two words garbled at this point; this is the version consistent with the rest of the lecture and with Lucía's names (carbamoyl phosphate *synthetase*, N-acetylglutamate *synthase*).

### Cycle
| Step | Reaction | Enzyme | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Carbamoyl phosphate + **ornithine** to **citrulline** | **Ornithine transcarbamoylase (OTC)** | Mitochondria |
| 3 | Citrulline + **aspartate** (second N) to argininosuccinate | Argininosuccinate synthetase | Cytosol |
| 4 | Argininosuccinate to **arginine** + **fumarate** | Argininosuccinate lyase | Cytosol |
| 5 | Arginine + H2O to **urea** + ornithine | **Arginase** | Cytosol |

- **Ornithine** and **citrulline** are alpha amino acids not found in proteins. The lecturer said ornithine is easy to remember because it resembles a standard amino acid, "only one carbon short". **[verify]** which amino acid (the transcript says "iso").
- Citrulline leaves the mitochondria and the cycle continues in the cytosol; ornithine re-enters the mitochondria; urea crosses the membrane into blood.
- Aspartate entry costs another ATP, giving AMP, "energetically like using two ATP". **[Lucía]** Argininosuccinate synthetase hydrolyzes two high-energy phosphate bonds.
- The lecturer left the fate of the aspartate skeleton as an exercise: it becomes **fumarate** (Lucía). **[Lucía]** Fumarate can enter the mitochondria and the Krebs cycle, regenerating oxaloacetate, the source of aspartate.
- **Arginine**: a standard protein amino acid that is also a urea cycle metabolite (guanidinium group). Arginase hydrolysis is spontaneous, but the enzyme speeds it up.
- **Arginase** is found mostly only in the liver, and only in the liver of ureotelic animals. **[check]** The transcript says only we "produce urease"; it means **urea** (Lucía: arginase is only in the liver of ureotelic animals).

### Connections with the Krebs cycle
Fumarate and oxaloacetate (aspartate) are Krebs intermediates, and glutamate becomes alpha-ketoglutarate: the two cycles exchange intermediates.

## 6. Balance and regulation
### Balance
- Urea synthesis **costs energy**: about 3 high-energy molecules per urea. "This is not free", but nitrogen must be eliminated efficiently.
- **[Lucía]** CO2 + NH4+ + 3 ATP + aspartate + 2 H2O to urea + 2 ADP + AMP + 4 Pi + fumarate (the slides give the same balance). Because it is so expensive, dietary protein is more important as a source of precursors than of energy.

### Long-term
- Liver levels of urea cycle enzymes adapt to the average diet (transcription and degradation of enzymes). High-protein diet or starvation increases them. Measuring them would tell you roughly how much protein someone eats.

### Short-term
- **Arginine** allosterically activates **N-acetylglutamate synthase (NAGS)**, which is not part of the cycle. More arginine means the cycle must speed up.
- NAGS: **glutamate + acetyl-CoA to N-acetylglutamate**.
- **N-acetylglutamate is the main (allosteric) activator of CPS I**, the main regulatory site of the pathway, which sits just before the cycle.
- Logic: a lot of glutamate means a lot of nitrogen to eliminate. **[Lucía]** N-acetylglutamate signals both enough energy (acetyl-CoA) and nitrogen to eliminate (glutamate).

## 7. Hyperammonemia and urea cycle defects (examples only)
- **Hyperammonemia**: high blood ammonium. Ammonium is toxic, especially **neurotoxic**, and can kill.
- Causes: **liver damage** (less urea production) or **genetic defects** of the urea cycle (rare but real).
- **[Lucía]** Symptoms: lethargy, tremors, slurred speech, blurred vision, vomiting, coma, death. Liver damage from alcohol, toxic chemicals, hepatitis. **[Slides]** Genetic causes: NAGS, CPS I, OTC (most frequent), ornithine translocase, argininosuccinate synthetase (citrullinemia), argininosuccinate lyase (argininosuccinic aciduria), arginase (hyperargininemia).

### Case 1: OTC deficiency
- Woman, 58, admitted for recurrent **asthmatic bronchitis**. Found: high ammonia (**[Slides]** 280 µmol/L), **cerebral oedema** on CT, **very low citrulline and arginine**, **raised uracil in urine**. She tended to avoid large protein intakes.
- **[check]** The transcript's "uracin" / "eulacin" is **uracil** (slides). The lecturer explained it rises because accumulated **carbamoyl phosphate** is diverted to nucleotide (pyrimidine) synthesis ([[Lec 5.12 Nucleotide metabolism]]).
- Reasoning (class exercise; exclude by what accumulates):
  - Arginase (step 5) defect: arginine would accumulate. Excluded.
  - Steps 3 or 4: citrulline would accumulate. Excluded.
  - CPS I (step 1): carbamoyl phosphate would not accumulate. Excluded.
  - So **step 2, OTC**: carbamoyl phosphate builds up and is diverted to nucleotide synthesis, citrulline and arginine fall, ammonia rises.
- OTC deficiency is the **most frequent** urea cycle defect. It is **X-linked**, so it is mostly manifest in men; she was a **symptomatic carrier**. **[Slides]** She had four male infants who died of OTC deficiency; her two adult daughters are carriers: one (healthy) lost a son, the other has mild intellectual disability and mild hyperammonemic episodes.
- Why it showed now (lecturer's speculation): infection causes tissue damage and fast protein degradation, unmasking the disease.

### Case 2: NAGS deficiency (pre-class reading)
- **[Slides]** Man in his early 30s after a **four-wheeler accident** and orthopaedic surgery. That night: confused, combative, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, generalized **tonic-clonic seizures**. History of chronic hypertension, persistent anaemia and nonspecific psychiatric problems. An "autoselective" vegetarian who systematically rejected meat.
- **[Slides]** Labs: **low blood urea nitrogen**, **ammonia 553 µmol/L**, **very high glutamine and high alanine**, **very low citrulline and low arginine**.
- **[check]** The transcript says "urea around 500"; the case report gives **ammonia** 553 µmol/L and **low** urea nitrogen.
- First guess (class and physician): **CPS I** defect, since nitrogen is not entering the cycle and stays in its carriers (glutamine, alanine) while everything after step 1 is low.
- Genetic test: CPS I activity was **normal**.
- Answer: **NAGS deficiency**. Step 1 is blocked, not because of its enzyme, but because its activator N-acetylglutamate is missing.
- Not because his low-protein diet left him unadapted: the short-term response is very fast. He avoided meat because protein raised his ammonia and made him feel ill.
- **[check]** The transcript switches between "male" and "she"; the case report: a **man**.

### Treatment (outline)
- Restrict dietary protein, but supplement **essential amino acids** (about half of the amino acids are essential; next week's lecture on amino acid synthesis). **[Lucía]** Also specific therapies for particular enzyme deficiencies.
- **[Slides]** Clinical management of hyperammonemia: dietary protein restriction, haemodialysis, **nitrogen scavengers** (sodium benzoate, sodium phenylbutyrate), liver transplantation.
- **[Slides]** Essential amino acids (Lehninger Table 18-1): histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine. Conditionally essential: arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline, tyrosine. Nonessential: alanine, asparagine, aspartate, glutamate, serine.

## 8. Fate of alpha-keto acids: glucogenic vs ketogenic
- Each amino acid is different; general classification by the fate of its carbon skeleton.
- **Glucogenic**: can be used to make glucose (via pyruvate or Krebs intermediates that lead to oxaloacetate). Examples: alanine to pyruvate by transamination; glutamine to glutamate to alpha-ketoglutarate to oxaloacetate to glucose.
- **Ketogenic**: give acetyl-CoA (or acetoacetyl-CoA), so they **can be used to make ketone bodies** (not that they always do). Acetyl-CoA carbons **cannot** make glucose.
- **Lysine and leucine** are the only purely ketogenic amino acids. Some amino acids are split into two fragments and are **both** glucogenic and ketogenic.
- Why acetyl-CoA cannot make glucose: in the Krebs cycle **two decarboxylations** remove 2 C, the same number that entered as acetyl-CoA, so there is no net gain of carbon (link: this answers the open question in [[Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies]], section 4).
- Student question: how can glycine become pyruvate? "A complex process"; not answered in the recording.
- The individual reactions are not required.

## 9. Cofactors of amino acid metabolism
| Cofactor | Origin | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)** | Vitamin (**[Lucía]** B6) | Cofactor of **transamination** (aminotransferases) and many other reactions |
| **Biotin** | Vitamin (**[Lucía]** B7) | **Carboxylations** (e.g. acetyl-CoA carboxylase, [[Lec 5.10 Lipid synthesis]]) |
| **Folates** (tetrahydrofolate) | Vitamin (**[Slides]** folate, B9) | Transfer of **one-carbon units** (at different oxidation states); example in nucleotide metabolism ([[Lec 5.12 Nucleotide metabolism]]). Pregnant women take folate supplements |
| **S-adenosylmethionine (SAM, AdoMet)** | Adenosine + methionine | Main **methyl group donor** (DNA, proteins, other molecules). **[Lucía]** Cofactor of the methyltransferases that methylate cytosine in DNA |
| **Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)** | | **Oxidation-reduction** reactions, e.g. phenylalanine hydroxylase |

## 10. Phenylketonuria (PKU)
- Most prevalent disorder in the table of genetic disorders of amino acid catabolism (read the table to understand it, not to memorize).
- Defect: **phenylalanine hydroxylase**, which hydroxylates **phenylalanine to tyrosine**, the first step of phenylalanine degradation.
- The enzyme is a **monooxygenase** (mixed-function oxidase): of the two O atoms in O2, one goes into the product and the other forms **water**, with electrons from **tetrahydrobiopterin**.
- Patients accumulate phenylalanine, which forms toxic compounds. **[Lucía]** Seizures, brain damage and intellectual disability.
- Common enough to be screened in **newborns** (heel-prick blood test); treatment must start from birth.
- **Aspartame** (sweetener) contains **phenylalanine**. Harmless unless you have PKU, which is why diet sodas carry the mandatory label "Contiene una fuente de fenilalanina". Patients need a specific low-phenylalanine diet plus amino acid supplements.
- Other example in the table: **argininemia** (arginase deficit, arginine accumulation, hyperammonemia). **[Lucía]** Others: tyrosinemia, homocystinuria, nonketotic hyperglycinemia, maple syrup urine disease; diagnosed by measuring amino acids in body fluids.
- **[Slides]** The table (Lehninger 18-2) also lists: albinism (tyrosinase), alkaptonuria (homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase), argininosuccinic acidemia, CPS I deficiency, homocystinuria (cystathionine beta-synthase), maple syrup urine disease (branched-chain alpha-keto acid dehydrogenase), **methylmalonic acidemia** (methylmalonyl-CoA mutase: propionyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, the odd-chain fatty acid step in [[Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies]]). PKU is the most common (under 8 per 100,000 births).
