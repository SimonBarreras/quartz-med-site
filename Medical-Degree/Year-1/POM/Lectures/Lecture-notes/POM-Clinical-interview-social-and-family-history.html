POM Clinical interview social and family history

Sources

  • Medical (automatic transcription, errors corrected): the whole workshop in one file. Starts cleanly with the objectives and ends cleanly with the exam information.
  • Structure: three simulated scenarios with an actor patient. Each is played by one or more student volunteers, followed by a group debrief led by the lecturer.
  • Gaps: the student-patient encounters are largely inaudible or garbled in the transcript (long runs of repeated filler, side conversations in Spanish). They are summarized only where the debrief confirms what was said. The debriefs are well transcribed.
  • The previous session (history of present illness, past medical history, the pain mnemonic SOCRATES) has no transcript or note; this session builds on it.
  • Coverage: transcript only. No slides, POM material or other notes in the vault or Drive.

  • Slides: “Jen will upload the slide” (harmful effects of smoking, standard drinking unit tables, extended social history items). None in the vault yet.
  • Textbook: an American book on the clinical interview is mentioned by students (hand-holding example, asking about suicidal thoughts); title not given.
  • Related: POM Goals of medicine and CanMEDS roles (communicator role).

The topics of today were “just to introduce” them. Further wording garbled (“it will start asking about leisure and economic status”) [verify].
  • There is also a separate multiple choice theory exam.
  • Harmful effects of smoking: “we want you to know for the exam what are the main harmful effects of smoking”; “not perfectly by far”, a general idea (section 4).
  • Pack years: “it’s for the exam, knowing how to calculate the pack years” (section 4).
  • Standard drinking units and the risk tables: “This will enter in the exam” (section 5).

  • Not to learn: the details of the lung cancer screening guideline (“I don’t want you to learn it. I just want you to understand why”) and the nicotine dependence questionnaire (“you don’t have to learn it”).

1. Objectives of the session

  • Continue to perfect the interview, building on last session (history of present illness, past medical history; past medical history was covered quickly at the end, so it is practised again).
  • New items: social history and family history.
  • Keep working on active listening and empathy.
  • Within social history: the sensitive topics, alcohol and drugs in general.

2. Scenario 1: pneumothorax follow-up (pulmonology office)

Case

  • Medical student in the pulmonology office. Patient comes for a checkup two months after being admitted for a pneumothorax (treated for four days, then home). This is the “official discharge”: checking everything is fine so the patient can go back to normal life.
  • She calls this a very standard procedure: patients often keep taking a medication they should not, or still have pain and must be seen again.
  • In real life the past medical history would be in the digital record (the patient was hospitalized); here you ask everything for practice.
  • Actor patient: young woman, sad that she must stay home while friends go out and school has started; hospital stay painful (chest tube) and lonely. Daily smoker since 16, about 10 cigarettes a day, did not stop during recovery, does not want to stop.

History of present illness at a follow-up: what to ask

ItemLecturer’s point
PainOne of the main symptoms of pneumothorax
Trouble breathingOne of the cardinal symptoms; respiratory distress: “have you had any more trouble breathing?”
Other symptoms”Any other problems” is fine, since it is not only about pain
Emotional impactIt was a traumatic, “pretty violent” episode
Effect on daily lifeE.g. told to rest, not going to school: relevant to deciding when she can go back
MedicationHas she continued or discontinued anything
Wound healingShe had a chest tube: healing, scarring
  • “These are basic questions, there’s not much more than that.”
  • Praise for the first student: did not interrupt the patient. The tendency is to cut patients off when they talk a lot; sometimes necessary if far too long, but here letting her talk was good.

Medication adherence

  • Patients may keep taking medication “forever” because they did not hear well or the doctor was too quick; others discontinue. So ask whether she has been taking any.
  • Painkillers are usually taken for the few days after the intervention, not two months.
  • Anecdote (poverty and medication): a student asked what to do when patients cannot afford drugs. “There’s nothing you can really do” in general; “a very frustrating and difficult reality for a doctor”. In her four years in primary care in very poor neighbourhoods of Buenos Aires, patients had no money for drugs; they had a set of basic drugs covered by the World Health Organization (wording garbled) [verify], but sometimes even those were missing. Some medication is secondary (e.g. cough medicine in a viral infection; painkillers are often for comfort), others are really important: then you try to get it for the patient through a social worker, or (in her clinic) donated medication kept for that situation. “It’s really context specific.”

Not going blank

  • A student went blank in the past medical history after the patient said “no” to surgery. Answer: learn the structure really well; “that’s what’s going to give you freedom to really interact with the patient and not be stressed out”.

Past medical history items

ItemLecturer’s points
Medical conditions (past and current)Ask first: more important than surgeries (a surgery may happen once; diabetes or asthma matter throughout). In five years of teaching, students “always forget about medical conditions”

  • Childhood illnesses were asked by a student; not discussed further.
  • Students’ laptops: “What I don’t really like are laptops” in the encounter; garbled [verify].

Family history

  • Ask an open, general question first (“any disease running in the family”): going straight to directed questions misses most of it.
  • But open questions can miss relevant illnesses: e.g. many patients do not consider hypertension a disease, especially if a parent’s is controlled with pills.
  • So ask in a directed way about the most prevalent diseases: diabetes, heart disease, cancer.
  • Then also diseases related to the case (here: any lung disease in the family).
  • Asking “what led up to the episode” belongs to the history of present illness (the fundamental question from last class: “do you relate it to anything?”), not to past medical or family history.

Introducing sensitive topics (social history)

  • Avoid “would you consider yourself a drinker?”: “we always try not to associate the identity of the person with a habit or a disease”.
  • First introduce the topic so it is not abrupt: e.g. “I’m going to ask you a few things about your lifestyle, and there might be a few sensitive questions, so I can help you.”
  • Then a simple, neutral question: “Do you drink alcohol?”
  • Social history is not only alcohol.

3. Social history: the items

  • In Spanish: “hábitos de vida y hábitos tóxicos”.
  • Items raised in class: diet, exercise, sleep, stress, occupation (profession, student, daily activity), smoking, alcohol, other drugs, leisure / entertainment, economic situation, sexual activity.
  • She separates items to ask always in practice from items to ask when relevant (economic situation “could be very relevant… but it could also not be”; sexual activity discussed here). The split is garbled in the transcript [verify].
  • Real practice: in the emergency department with abdominal pain you probably would not ask about alcohol unless relevant; experience tells you. For now, always ask the core items so you practise them.
  • The slides add further aspects of social history: spirituality, social support system (“could be very relevant, but not all of it”).

4. Tobacco

Harmful effects of smoking (class brainstorm; slides to follow)

AreaPoints from the class
PneumothoraxSmoking is a risk factor (“I did not tell you”; she will add it to the scenario next time)
Emphysema (transcribed “impicillin”)Loss of functional lung tissue from inflammation, trapped air, poor oxygenation. Part of COPD, which includes it and chronic bronchitis. Heavy smokers at the end of life with oxygen tanks: lungs so destroyed they desaturate when walking
Lung cancer”That’s what everybody has in their head”
Other cancersAnything in contact with smoke and its toxic metabolites: throat, mouth, tongue, oesophagus; bladder cancer is “a very known one” (contact with the metabolites)
Lung fibrosisCould be a final stage of any inflammatory lung condition, “but it’s not a disease in itself” (passage partly garbled) [verify]
Cardiovascular disease”The number one killer of our society”: heart disease, stroke, heart attack, peripheral arterial pathology (arteries clogged by cholesterol); smoking is one of the main risk factors
PregnancyDefects in pregnancy; after birth, breastfeeding (passage garbled) [verify]
Passive exposure at homeHouse air is contaminated even if the parent smokes outside (residues on clothes); small children have more respiratory infections and more otitis. Counsel the parents to limit or stop
  • A student asked whether nicotine is a “gateway” like alcohol or marijuana. Lecturer: the literature describes an association between substance use in general, so if one is positive, ask about the others (alcohol, marijuana, other drugs).

What to ask a smoker

QuestionWhy
Since whenExposure (with amount)
How much (cigarettes per day)Exposure
Has she ever considered quittingReadiness; ask before offering help
Has she tried before, what therapy (e.g. nicotine patches or pouches)Previous attempts
Moments when she smokes more (stress, social)“Getting to know your patients so that you can tailor your counselling”
How dependent she isDetermines whether medication is used and what type of therapy
Other substancesAssociation between substance use
  • Dependence indicators: amount per day; frequency (back to back); whether it is hard to be in a closed place without being able to get out (e.g. cinema); smoking first thing in the morning (“their body asks for it”; same in alcohol abuse).
  • A primary care questionnaire for nicotine dependence (name garbled as “Yaki”) [verify]; “you don’t have to learn it”. Items she read: how soon after waking you smoke; difficulty refraining where forbidden (church, library, cinema); which cigarette you would hate to give up (the first in the morning indicates severe dependence); cigarettes per day; smoking more frequently at certain times; smoking when ill (“it’s not natural for the body to want a cigarette when you’re ill”).

Pack years

  • Merges duration and amount into one measure of exposure:
    • pack years = years smoked x cigarettes per day / 20 (20 = cigarettes in a pack)
  • Class example: 8 years (since 16) x 10 cigarettes per day / 20; the class answer is transcribed “four or five years” [verify].
  • It is not packs per year or per week: “it’s just a measure… to compare patients”. Second example: 30 years of 5 cigarettes a day gives 7.5.
  • Variable consumption (e.g. 5 years at 20 a day, then less): fraction it and calculate each period.
  • Why compare exposures: science needs it for evidence-based guidelines, to know the risk at 1, 10, 20 pack years and decide whom to screen (e.g. a yearly scan for lung cancer), rather than improvising by intuition. “It’s a strong decision”; there is radiation.
  • Guideline she quoted: a patient with 20 pack years, aged 50 to 80 (the range where lung cancer usually develops), and an active smoker should be told to have a yearly CT scan (transcribed “HHS scan”). A following fragment, “because it’s not useful. It doesn’t diminish mortality”, has lost its context [verify]. Not to learn: “I just want you to understand why you’re doing the things you’re doing.”

Counselling by stage

  • Scenario 1 lesson: the student said “I’m going to talk to my attending and see how we can help you” before asking whether she wanted to quit; the patient said she did not want to. Be slower: first ask if she has ever considered quitting.
  • Precontemplation (not considering it): give a little information, “see if it grows into a plant”, without spending too much time; insisting makes the patient defensive. A few sentences: it is harmful, it is a risk factor for the pneumothorax and its recurrence, we are here to help if you give it some thought. A student’s point accepted: since she is recovering, at least advise her to limit smoking.
  • Contemplation (“I’m considering it, but not now”): give more information, tell her how you can help, plan a “when” for the next consultation.
  • “These are all stages that have been studied” in smoking.
  • General message: smoking is harmful in general, not only now; advise reducing if the patient cannot quit; explain cumulative effects.
  • Your role as a medical student: take notes and pass it on to the attending, not counsel as the doctor.

5. Scenario 2: sadness and trouble sleeping (outpatient clinic)

Case

  • Patient has just arrived; you do not know why. Find out why, and do the full introduction, identification, chief complaint, fundamental questions, plus social and personal history.
  • Actor patient: trouble sleeping, sad mood, studying for a public position. Over three attempts: a motorbike accident about a year ago, then her best friend died of cancer; sadness for about one to two months (time frames differed between attempts). Drinks every day: a beer in the morning, a beer while studying in the afternoon, two glasses of wine with a friend.

Reading and naming the emotion

  • Student noticed the sadness from body language: no eye contact, looking down, long time to answer, even to give her name and date of birth.
  • He said “I see you’re sad”: praised as honest and “a very intelligent move”. Otherwise you do the whole consultation without naming it, “an elephant in the room”.
  • He also said this was a confidential space (setting the stage) and asked softly whether there was a specific reason; she answered “life”.
  • Why she does not say more: it is hard to share with a stranger after three minutes; in a depressed mood “you don’t feel like connecting with anyone. That’s a characteristic of depression.” Adapt your rhythm and make space.

Strategies to get the story out (from the debriefs)

StrategyExample or point
Rephrase and insist once more”Has anything else happened that you would like to share?”
Short, directed questions”Is it a family issue?”
Explore the feeling”Are you sad all the time?”, “Have you been feeling depressed?” (name it)
Encourage continuationIf you interrupted her: stop and say “please continue”
EchoRepeat her “and also…”
Silence and waitingDepressed patients need “even more space, even more slow”; silence “actually shows that you’re a professional”. Do not move on in the interview when you know something is unsaid
Respect a refusalAt some point “I’m not going to insist anymore”; “a delicate balance”
Use her nameHelps create the connection
Ask why nowShe has felt this way for a while: what led her to consult now
  • The student who waited through the silence got the information (“If I interrupted… she would have lost her”).

Physical touch

  • Students saw hand-holding in “an American book” and were unsure. Class discussion on cultural differences (Latin countries felt to be more physical).
  • Lecturer: possibly not in a first encounter in an outpatient clinic; you do not know the person (she may dislike physical contact). With a bond, e.g. when someone dies, a hug is “totally normal and natural if you feel like it”. It depends on your own style: “know who you are and do what you feel is natural”; not touching does not make you a bad doctor.

Showing empathy

ChannelPoints
Verbal”I’m very sorry to hear that”
Non-verbalPosture (leaning forward shows engagement; a remark on distance and crossed arms is garbled)
Tone and paceMatch the patient’s level of voice and pace; “mirror” body language subtly (used in psychology), not acting depressed. Mismatch example: a slow, soft, sad patient and a loud, fast “so now I’m going to ask you about your diet”
Offer help”Would you be interested in talking to someone?”, the same kind of question as “would you be interested in quitting smoking?”
SupportAsk whether she has told friends or family; is anybody there to listen. Tells you how much help she needs

Normal grief or depression?

  • “Grieving can last up to two years” and still be normal (to give an idea).
  • Not normal: lost interest in everything, nothing brings pleasure, not meeting friends.
  • A student cited the textbook: ask about suicidal thoughts. Lecturer: “have it in your mind to ask if you feel that could be a concern”; be careful but it is important.

Severity adapted to other complaints

  • The 0 to 10 severity scale is for pain; adapt the idea of intensity. For sleep: how rested she feels on waking, how sleepy during the day, hours slept now versus before (sleeping two hours = very sleep deprived).

Alcohol: asking and documenting

  • Patients first say “the usual”: alcohol carries stigma, patients fear judgement and get defensive. Be subtle, respectful and neutral, but never settle for “the usual” or something superficial: you need the information to know if she is at risk.
  • Do not suggest numbers (“like four? like one?“): ask an open question. If you feel she is not telling you, ask “do you drink anything else other than beer?”
  • Never write “she drinks a lot”: “what’s a lot?”
  • Document:
ItemWhy
AmountAs for smoking
Type of drinkDifferent alcohol concentration
Time of dayDrinking first thing in the morning suggests dependence
PatternDaily, social, or nothing during the week and very drunk at the weekend (a known pattern, still harmful)

Why alcohol matters in this patient

  • Alcohol and sadness form a vicious circle (sadder, drink more), with risk of dangerous situations if there are suicidal thoughts (student’s answer, accepted).
  • She consulted for trouble sleeping: possibly rumination over the tragedy; possibly seeking a sleeping pill prescription; alcohol abuse may relate to the sleep problem.
  • Alcohol plus sleeping pills: “not a good idea”. Point of a complete history: “Just like the allergies, we could kill our patient… because of these interactions.”

Standard drinking unit (SDU, unidad de bebida estándar)

  • “The new way that we assess the risk”; before it was grams. 1 SDU = 10 g of alcohol.
  • Concentrations given in class (approximate, garbled): beer about 5 to 12 (“depends on the beer”), wine about 13, whiskey/liquor transcribed as “47 and above” and then “25 and above” [verify].
  • Equivalences given: a small glass of wine = 1 SDU = 200 ml of beer = 50 ml of liquor [verify against slides].
  • Grams of alcohol: stated as “milliliters of alcohol multiplied by the density of alcohol divided by 100”. In the worked example the volume, the concentration (5) and a density factor (transcribed “zero”) were multiplied [verify the exact formula on the slides].
  • Worked example (simplified, not the patient’s real intake): 3 beers a day, about 1 litre at 5: 40 g per day = 4 SDU per day. The risk classification is per week, so multiply by 7; the class answer is transcribed “twenty” [verify].
  • Classification: for a woman this level is dangerous (“Para una mujer…”), and it would be dangerous for a man too (thresholds not stated; see slide) [verify].
  • Mixed drinks: calculate SDUs for each drink and add them, or add the grams and convert.
  • Why tables: “to help us not improvise”, to know the risk based on evidence.

6. Scenario 3: abdominal pain (emergency department)

Case

  • Medical student in the emergency department; patient with abdominal pain: use the history of present illness with the mnemonics from last class, then the complete clinical history: chief complaint, history of present illness, past medical history, social history, family history.
  • Actor patient: 24 years old; pain for two days, started in the centre and moved to the right lower quadrant, constant, worse standing and better sitting; had taken something that morning without relief (garbled) [verify]; allergy to nuts; vaccines up to date; drinks sporadically at weekends; had not gone to the bathroom for two days. The script was appendicitis; the actor forgot to mention fever and nausea.
  • Peers’ feedback: good non-verbal attitude (posture, clear speech, tone, pace), organized and complete. Suggested extra question: has she ingested anything lately that could be related.

Structure of the clinical note

  • Identifying information first, then a headline summarizing the chief complaint: a quick idea of what happened that day, for you or a colleague reading it months later.
  • Headline options from class: “three-day abdominal pain” (the case says two days [verify]); “acute abdominal pain” (gives a sense of onset and time frame); “abdominal pain in the right lower quadrant”.
  • Praised: it said what she said and did not give a diagnosis.
  • In the clinical note you do not write the SOCRATES letters; you write a paragraph. Not everything in the note is structured.
  • Do not use shorthand such as “allergy: -” in a clinical note (she uses it on the board only to save time).

SOCRATES applied

LetterIn this case
S SiteRight lower quadrant (right iliac region)
O OnsetTwo days ago; onset also means sudden or gradual. Last class: brain haemorrhage = very sudden onset. Here not asked; it would be gradual, which fits appendicitis (gets worse and worse)
C CharacterNot clearly obtained
R RadiationNot radiation: it started periumbilical and moved to the right lower quadrant (typical appendicitis presentation). Radiation = pain in both places at once, e.g. chest pain in a heart attack also felt in the arm
A Associated symptomsForgotten in the script: nausea and fever (“also very typical”). Not going to the bathroom for two days: do not write “constipation”, which is a diagnosis with criteria (she may normally go every three days); write that she has not gone for two days
T TimingConstant (“there’s no timing because it’s constant”). In other mnemonics “constant” goes with character; depends on the mnemonic
E Exacerbating / relievingWorse standing, better sitting (patient’s words; the debrief wording is confused)
S SeverityNot asked; the script was 7 to 8 out of 10
  • Could also ask about previous consultations.

Past medical history in this case

  • Order: medical conditions first, then surgery, allergies (nuts), medication (in general, not only the painkiller for this pain; it “shouldn’t be that she only answers paracetamol”), vaccines, transfusions, trips.
  • Vaccines relevant here: an infection she would not get if vaccinated could cause abdominal pain; and she may go to surgery if it is appendicitis, so everything must be in order.
  • Trips could be relevant: parasites in some countries, endemic illnesses; e.g. dengue is more and more common and can present with severe abdominal pain and fever. “You can’t rule out dengue.”
  • Family history was asked too early: put it after the personal history (first you are more interested in the patient than her family).
  • Social history covered diet, exercise, alcohol, smoking, other drugs: “90% of what you ask for”.

Signposting

  • Advice to everybody: say where you are going: “I’m going to ask you a few questions about your past medical history”, “…about your family history”. It helps you be organized and helps the patient know where she stands; otherwise the patient can get disoriented.
