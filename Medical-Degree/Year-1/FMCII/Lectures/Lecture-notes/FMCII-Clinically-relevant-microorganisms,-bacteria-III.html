1. What an antibiotic is [p2]

  • Definition [p2]: chemical substances, natural, semi-synthetic or synthetic, that inhibit the growth of bacteria or kill them. Examples: penicillin, streptomycin [p2]; he added tetracyclines and said there are “not hundreds, not thousands” of antibiotics already discovered, “an endless number”.
  • Most antibiotics have a natural origin; there are also semi-synthetic and fully synthetic molecules.

2. Selective toxicity [p2]

  • Definition [p2]: the ability to suppress or kill an infecting bacterium without toxicity to the host. Slide example: at therapeutic doses gentamicin kills bacteria but is not toxic to eukaryotic mammalian cells.
  • Anecdote, antibiotic discovery in the US: on a discovery project they screened half a million molecules against one bacterium and found around 100 that seemed very effective at killing it. All 100 were also toxic to mammalian cells. “Finding this selectivity is extremely difficult.”
  • His other illustration: bleach and soap are great antibacterials, but obviously unusable because they are highly toxic to human cells.
  • The structural basis for selectivity is given later in the lecture: bacterial DNA polymerases, RNA polymerases and ribosomes differ from the host’s (section 6). “If we were the same, then these activities would be toxic for the human cells.”
  • [Lucía, p3] makes the related point that Gram-negatives are more resistant to antibiotics because many antibacterial drugs target the peptidoglycan wall, which is thin in Gram-negatives.

3. Bactericidal vs bacteriostatic [p2]

EffectHis example
BactericidalInduces cell death: viability declines after the antibiotic is addedPenicillin
BacteriostaticInhibits cell growth: the growth curve flattensTetracycline (transcript “the recycling”)
  • On the growth curve from FMCII Clinically relevant microorganisms, bacteria II (lag, logarithmic, stationary phases, viable bacteria on the y axis), the untreated culture grows normally; adding a bactericidal agent makes viability decline, adding a bacteriostatic one stops the growth.
  • The definitions are not absolute [p2]: a bacteriostatic antibiotic may kill some susceptible species, and a bactericidal one may only inhibit growth of some species, or be bactericidal only at high doses. “But for the most part” each antibiotic is generally one or the other.

4. Measuring antibiotic activity: MIC and MBC [p3-5]

4a. The clinical situation

  • A patient in the ICU is seriously ill with an infection. The doctor takes a sample, the bacterium infecting the patient is isolated and sent to the microbiology lab, which tests a set of antibiotics against that particular strain, so the doctor can choose.
  • The MIC is a number that links one particular strain to one antibiotic: one strain tested against 10 antibiotics gives 10 MICs.
  • The number on its own “means nothing”: it has to be converted into a clinical category (section 4e).

4b. Definitions [p3]

Definition [p3]
MIC, minimum inhibitory concentrationThe lowest concentration of an antimicrobial that will inhibit the visible growth of a microorganism after overnight incubation
MBC, minimum bactericidal concentrationThe lowest concentration of antimicrobial that will prevent the growth of an organism after subculture on to antibiotic-free media (that is, the lowest that kills)
  • MICs are the gold standard for determining susceptibility [p3].

4c. Method 1: broth dilution [p3]

Steps as he and the slide give them (his worked example: Staphylococcus aureus against vancomycin):

  1. Prepare two-fold dilutions of the antimicrobial (128, 64, 32, 16, 8 µg/ml and so on) in liquid growth medium dispensed in tubes; all tubes have the same volume, only the concentration differs.
  2. Inoculate each tube with the same standardized microbial inoculum prepared in the same medium. He recalled the standard as “something like 10^7 bacteria per ml” [verify]: he was not sure, and the slide only says “standardized”.
  3. Mix and incubate overnight under suitable conditions.
  4. MIC = the lowest concentration that completely inhibits growth as detected by the naked eye (no turbidity). Slide example: MIC = 8 µg/ml. There is a breakpoint tube where growth stops.
  5. MBC = determined after broth dilution by subculturing a sample from the no-growth tubes on non-selective agar plates after 24 h (he described plating 100 µl on petri dishes). The MBC is the lowest concentration giving no growth after incubation. Slide example: MBC = 32 µg/ml.
  6. Interpret: susceptible, intermediate or resistant.

PrincipleFilter paper discs containing a single antibiotic concentration are placed on an inoculated agar plate; the agent diffuses into the agar and inhibits growthA strip with an increasing concentration gradient from one end to the other is placed on an inoculated agar plate: a combination of the disk diffusion test and the dilution methods
Read-outMeasure the diameter of the zone of inhibition in mm. His example: 15 mmThe point where the border of the zone of inhibition touches the strip defines the MIC; the zone is pear-shaped because the top of the strip holds the highest concentration. 4e. Interpretation: SIR categories and breakpoints [p5]

  • To convert inhibition zone diameters or MIC values into SIR categories (Susceptible, Intermediate, Resistant), cut-off values (breakpoints) must be defined for the organism under study [p5].
  • Breakpoints are published for clinically relevant organisms by the CLSI (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute) [p5]; he added that the booklet is updated every year and you go to the page for your organism, in the example S. aureus.

5. Mechanisms of action of the major antibacterial agents [p5]

  • Principle: antibiotics inhibit an essential target, macromolecule or process; block that enzyme or that synthesis and the bacterium cannot grow, or is killed. [p5] Antibiotics inhibit essential processes such as cell wall synthesis, plasma membrane integrity, nucleic acid synthesis, ribosomal function, folate synthesis and are classified by their mechanism of action.
  • The three major groups (the exam flag):
GroupTargetHis remarks
1. Inhibitors of cell wall (peptidoglycan) biosynthesisPeptidoglycan synthesisPeptidoglycan is a very complex molecule with many enzymes and therefore many targets; a few enzymes in the pathway have still not been targeted, so novel antibiotics are still being discovered against it. Works because a bacterium is not viable with a leaky or faulty peptidoglycan: it has to be intact
2. Inhibitors of protein synthesisRibosomes: some hit the 50S subunit, some the 30SA huge number of antibiotics; still uncovering novel ones. “These are big molecules, big RNA-protein complexes. There is a lot of points where we can inhibit”
3. Inhibitors of DNA and RNA synthesisNucleic acid synthesisThe third of the three most populated groups
  • Other groups he mentioned without detail: folate biosynthesis and agents that interfere with the integrity of the membrane.

6. Genetic information in bacteria: basic facts [p6]

  • [p6] No nuclear membranes; one or several naked DNAs, often circular.
  • The chromosome: “think about a rubber band”, a circular closed molecule. Cut and stretched it is very long: he gave the length as 1 micrometre = 1,000 nanometres, against an E. coli cell of roughly 1 micron. [verify] The transcript then says “so we have a chromosome that is 1,000 micrometres”: the figures as transcribed are inconsistent, and the slide only states that the chromosome is much longer than the cell, so it needs to be tightly packed. Take the concept, not the number.
  • Plasmids: a bacterium may have one, two, three, more, or none. Genome = chromosome + plasmids, if any (expanded in section 8).
  • Differences from eukaryotes [p6], the basis of selective toxicity (section 2):
FeatureBacteriaEukaryotes
RNA polymerasesDifferent from eukaryotic ones
Ribosomes70S80S
Gene organizationGenes often grouped into operons sharing function or coordinating their control
  • He added DNA polymerases to the list of structurally different machinery: these enzymes “perform similar functions or the same function, but they are structurally different”, and the regulation differs.

7. Operons [p6-7]

7a. The two arrangements [p6]

Monocistronic mRNAPolycistronic mRNA
Structure [p6]One gene, one promoterMultiple genes, one promoter
TranscriptionRNA polymerase binds the promoter and transcribes a single mRNA, translated into one proteinRNA polymerase binds the one promoter and transcribes a single mRNA encoding several independent products
Definition [p6]Operons are groups of genes that are transcribed in a single mRNA
  • “Cistron is an old-fashioned word for gene”: in the fifties and sixties genes were called cistrons.
  • Roughly 50% of bacterial genes are arranged in operons (“not an exact figure”).
  • Number of genes per operon: the average is about three, ranging from two to six, or even twelve.

7b. Why operons exist: compaction and coordination

  • Compaction: one promoter drives several genes, so you do not need three independent promoters: “a way to save DNA space”.
  • Coordination (“perhaps the most important one”): you want several products produced simultaneously because they are needed at the same time.
  • [p7] In general an operon will contain genes that function in the same process:
Category [p7]His example
Enzymes from the same pathwayThe tryptophan (trp) operon: one promoter and five genes, trpE, trpD, trpC, trpB, trpA, the five enzymes of the tryptophan biosynthetic pathway, starting from chorismic acid through multiple steps, each catalysed by a different enzyme. They must be produced together to complete the synthesis
Subunits of enzyme complexesATP synthase: a complex enzyme of different subunits that must interact to form the whole enzyme, so they are encoded in one operon. “If we have a multi-component enzyme with different subunits, then all of them will most likely be encoded in a single operon”
Complex macromolecules: peptidoglycan, capsular polysaccharidesLPS: synthesized in the cytoplasm, then translocated across the inner membrane and exported to the outer membrane, to end up on the surface. Each set of steps is encoded in operons; the same holds for capsular polysaccharides and peptidoglycan

8. The bacterial genome: chromosome vs plasmids [p7-8]

  • Definition [p7]: the total collection of genes carried by a bacterium, both on its chromosome and on its extrachromosomal genetic elements, if any.
  • Replication: the chromosome has a single origin of replication (ori), and replication is bidirectional from it. Plasmids carry their own ori [p7]. In binary fission (FMCII Clinically relevant microorganisms, bacteria II), replication of the plasmids and of the chromosome is coordinated, so both daughter cells receive the same genetic information.
Chromosome [p7]Plasmids [p7]
PresenceAlways presentNot always present
GenesEssential genes; genes important for pathogenesisOnly non-essential genes
RoleProvide a selective advantage: antibiotic resistance mechanisms, toxins, virulence determinants
MobilityCan be transferred to other bacteria; can also be lost
  • His numbers for E. coli: about 4,700 genes on average (bacteria generally in the 3,000-5,000 range), of which maybe 350-400 are completely essential for survival. Examples of essential products: DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase, enzymes for LPS biosynthesis, enzymes for flagellum biosynthesis. “These are great candidates for antibiotic discovery.”
  • Why nothing essential sits on a plasmid: a plasmid may be lost when the bacterium replicates, since replication is not a perfect process, so a bacterium “will never encode anything essential” there. His aside: in the lab they try to get rid of plasmids and it is almost impossible, but loss does happen in nature.
  • Episomes [p8]: plasmids that can integrate into the host chromosome. The slide’s example is the E. coli F plasmid; he called it the fertility plasmid. The mechanism he gave: the plasmid has a region of DNA homologous to a region in the chromosome, so by homologous recombination it can integrate (“jump inside”). Once integrated it is copied with the rest of the genome when the cell divides, and it can also excise.
  • [Lucía, p11] makes the same point from the pathogenesis side: virulence factors can be encoded in the genome but are usually acquired from the environment in the form of plasmids (mobile elements).

9. Genome plasticity [p8-10]

9a. Mutations [p8]

CauseDetail
SpontaneousOccasional errors by DNA polymerase during replication [p8]DNA replicates with fairly high fidelity but mistakes happen: inserting the wrong nucleotide (base mispairing), or too many or too few nucleotides (insertions and deletions). His examples: a T in the template should get an A, a C should get a G, but sometimes the wrong one goes in, or one is skipped, or two go in instead of one
InducedAgents that directly damage the DNA [p8]Various chemical agents or radiation, for example UV radiation; these increase the mutation rate
  • Mutation rate [p8]: varies substantially among taxa; in bacteria roughly 1 mistake per 10^8-10^10 nucleotides.
  • His gloss on why that is not as small as it sounds: bacteria divide very quickly and a culture contains billions of cells dividing, so the mutations accumulating in a single culture are “fairly high”.
  • [p8] The variations in DNA sequence (mutations, insertions, deletions, duplications, rearrangements) lead to differential expression of genes and proteins. He mentioned duplication of regions of DNA and rearrangements (the orientation of the DNA changing) without detail.

9b. Vertical vs horizontal [p9]

Vertical evolutionHorizontal gene transfer
What happensA mutation in the genome (for example a point mutation in a non-essential gene) is conserved and passed to both daughter cellsOne bacterium transfers DNA to another in real time, for example in the gut where bacteria sit close together
Requires replication?Yes: it requires the bacterium to replicate, and it requires timeNo: it happens in a single step between two mature cells, with no growth needed
ResultThe mutation is maintained generation after generationThe recipient’s genome has changed by acquiring a piece of DNA from a donor
  • [p9] Many bacteria, especially many pathogenic species, are “promiscuous with their DNA”; exchange may be advantageous for the recipient, especially if the DNA encodes antibiotic resistance; the transferred DNA can be integrated into the recipient chromosome or stably maintained as a plasmid and passed on to daughter bacteria.

9c. Mechanisms of gene transfer [p10]

MechanismWhat happens
ConjugationA donor and a recipient connected by a conjugative pilus (from FMCII Clinically relevant microorganisms, bacteria II); the donor transfers plasmids, and can also transfer chromosomal DNA
TransductionInfection by a phage (bacteriophage), a virus that infects bacteria, which injects a piece of DNA into the bacterium
TransformationA nearby bacterium has died, leaving fragments of free DNA; the bacterium picks up some of those fragments
Transposition”Jumping genes”: a piece of DNA sitting in one place jumps to a completely different location, including from the chromosome into a plasmid and from a plasmid into the chromosome. Such an element is a transposon; the discovery won a Nobel Prize
  • Figure convention to remember: blue = recipient, yellow = donor, red = the foreign DNA.
  • His summary: “the bacterial genome can change anytime. It’s not really fixed.”

10. Worked example: horizontal gene transfer and the E. coli pathotypes [p10]

  • [p10] Uptake of mobile genetic elements by commensal E. coli leads to acquisition of specific virulence attributes (an E. coli pathotype), which gives an increased ability to adapt to new niches and to cause a broad spectrum of disease: enteric/diarrhoeal disease, urinary tract infections (UTIs) and sepsis/meningitis.
  • The mobile elements named on the slide: plasmids, bacteriophage, pathogenicity islands (transposon). He stressed these do not have to be memorized, and that they mostly encode toxins.
  • The process: we carry E. coli as commensal microbiota in the gut and are healthy. The commensal acquires one or several mobile elements by transformation, transduction or conjugation, and evolves into a pathotype: a lineage specialised in causing a particular kind of infection.
  • The two groups of pathotypes:
GroupPathotypesDisease
Intestinal (diarrhoeagenic)ETEC, EPEC, EIEC, EHEC [p10]Diarrhoea, each one somewhat different (for example some with blood), depending on the elements picked up
Extra-intestinalUPEC [p10]; NMEC (named by the lecturer only)UPEC: urinary tract infections. NMEC: meningitis
  • Why he chose this example: it is “a real example of the evolution of horizontal gene transfer happening in the real world”. The figure is from 2015 and is already out of date: new pathotypes are emerging, and combinations of pathotypes are appearing. “This highlights the importance of horizontal gene transfer in bacterial virulence.”
