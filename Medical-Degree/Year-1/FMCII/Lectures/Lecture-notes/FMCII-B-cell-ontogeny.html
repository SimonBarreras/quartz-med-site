1. Overview: three steps of lymphocyte maturation [Slides p3-4]

StepWhatWhere (B cells)
1. Early maturationDifferentiation and proliferation of precursorsBone marrow
2. Antigen receptor gene expressionV(D)J recombination (FMCII Genetics of immunoglobulins), receptor on the surfaceBone marrow
3. Selection of the mature repertoireKeep receptors that can recognize foreign antigens; remove those that recognize self (else autoimmunity): central toleranceStarts in the bone marrow, finishes in the spleen
  • [Slides p3] Bone marrow precursors to mature lymphocytes with Ag receptors against non-self Ags. [Slides p4] It begins in the bone marrow and is completed in the spleen. The same scheme applies to T cells (next week).
  • No mature B cells in the bone marrow, only the first stages; mature B cells are found in the spleen.
  • Each stage gives the cell new genes expressed (through transcription factors), so new proteins, new functions, and new surface markers that let us identify the stage. [Slides p5] Stages defined by transcription factor expression, Ig gene rearrangements, cell-surface markers (flow cytometry).

2. Early maturation in the bone marrow [Slides p5-6]

  • Stem cells: can give rise to any kind of hematopoietic cell (today: the B cell line).
  • Stromal cells (supporting cells) provide:
SignalHow
Soluble moleculese.g. growth factors, secreted
Membrane ligandsBound to the stromal cell: direct cell-to-cell interaction
ChemokinesAttract cells along a concentration gradient; some chemokines are concentrated in the bone marrow, so cells migrate and stay there. Example: some antibody-secreting plasma cells live in the bone marrow because chemokines attract them
  • First proliferation, then receptors: about 10^8 different B cells are needed, so at least 10^8 undifferentiated cells, each giving a cell with a different receptor.
  • IL-7 induces precursor cell proliferation.
  • Clinical, X-linked immunodeficiency: several X-linked syndromes are immunodeficiencies. A mutation in a gene for a cytokine receptor (e.g. the IL-7 receptor) means the precursor cannot receive the signal: no proliferation or differentiation, so an immune defect. [Slides p6] Defects in a component of cytokine receptors (including IL-7R).
  • [Lucía] calls IL-7 “one of the most important transcription factors” [check]: the lecturer and the slides treat it as a growth signal acting through a cytokine receptor.

3. Pro-B cells: the heavy chain [Slides p7]

  • The heavy chain comes first, because it is the most important component of the antigen receptor: its variable domain binds antigen and its constant domains give the functional properties.
StageEvents [Slides p7]
Early pro-BD-JH recombination completed
Late pro-BPax5 transcription factor expressed (lecturer: “a transcription factor that is expressed in B cells”): promotes VH to DJH recombination (full VDJ); CD19 expression begins; production of Igα and Igβ begins
  • CD19: the first time a cell of the B lineage expresses it; kept throughout development. [Lucía] Once a cell shows CD19, it is no longer a precursor.
  • Igα and Igβ: the membrane Ig’s transmembrane/intracellular part is very short (20-25 amino acids), not enough to signal. So the receptor is accompanied by Igα and Igβ, with longer intracytoplasmic tails that transmit the signal. The same is true for T cell receptors. As soon as there is an Ig receptor, these molecules are needed, otherwise the Ig would be useless.
  • Massive cell death: P and N additions, deletions etc. often give a non-functional chain (premature stop codon, shorter, or not folding). Then the second allele is tried; if that also fails, the cell dies. About two-thirds of heavy-chain rearrangements are unproductive [Slides p7: “Unproductive rearrangements (2/3)”, “toll to be paid for having diversity-inducing mechanisms”]. [Lucía] Same.
  • Answer to the earlier student question (why such a huge repertoire): many receptors never fold properly or die, so a large reserve is needed to end up with functional antibodies.

4. Pre-B cells: pre-BCR, then the light chain [Slides p8-9]

4a. Early pre-B

  • Marker: the heavy chain on the surface with a surrogate light chain (not a real light chain) [Slides p8: VpreB and λ5] = the pre-B cell receptor (pre-BCR). Something similar to an antibody that can receive signals, but not a real one.
  • CD25 (IL-2 receptor α chain) [Slides p8] expressed: the cell can now respond to the growth factor IL-2.
  • Consequences [Slides p8]:
    • RAG inhibition: rearrangement of the heavy chain on the other chromosome is stopped: a single heavy chain = allelic exclusion.
    • Cell survival and cell proliferation: until now the cell was preparing the machinery to receive signals; now it can receive them and proceed.

4b. Late pre-B

  • Light-chain rearrangement starts and is completed; with the heavy chain made at the pro-B stage, plus Igα and Igβ, this gives a full antibody, chronologically always IgM. The cell becomes an immature B cell (surface IgM only) [Slides p8].
  • Little or no cell death at the pre-B stage [Slides p8: “No cell death occurring at pre-B cell stage”; Lucía: “almost no cell death”]; the lecturer’s sentence is garbled but says it is not important here, as it is for the heavy chain. [Lucía] More chances (four) to make a functional light chain.
  • Light-chain order [Slides p9]: RAG activation, kappa allele 1, kappa allele 2, then lambda alleles; as soon as one gives IgM, light-chain rearrangement is inhibited: a single light chain, allelic exclusion. Each B cell makes kappa or lambda, never both (isotype exclusion of the light chain).
  • Kappa:lambda: lecturer and [Slides p9] 2/3 kappa, 1/3 lambda, because there are more kappa segments. [check] The genetics lecture slides gave the serum ratio as 60:40; [Lucía] gives both (“2/3… (60%)“).

5. Selection of the mature repertoire: autotolerance [Slides p10]

  • The immature B cells together can recognize “almost everything”: foreign and self molecules. Cells recognizing self are not needed and are dangerous (autoimmune disease), so they are removed: generation of autotolerance (tolerance = not responding to something).
  • Trigger [Slides p10]: positive (multivalent, high-avidity) recognition of “autoAg” in the bone marrow or spleen by immature B cells. Self molecules are often multivalent (repeated), so the interaction with IgM can be very strong.
  • Outcomes:
StepWhat
1. Receptor editing (light chain) [Slides p10]A second chance: RAG reactivation and a new V-J recombination on the light chain (example on the slide: V9J3 Cκ), choosing a new V and a new J. If the new light chain with the old heavy chain no longer recognizes self, the cell lives: tolerance
2. Cell death = negative selection [Slides p10]If the new combination still recognizes self, the cell dies
  • “In many cases, second chances”: “you have always the possibility of doing it correctly”.
  • It is a biochemical matter of the strength of the interaction:
Signal through the receptorResult
None (receptor recognizes nothing, or no receptor)Death [Slides p14: “death by neglect”]
Too strong (high affinity for self)Receptor editing; if still too strong, death
IntermediateSurvival, proliferation, maturation
  • Student question: is weak binding enough to mature? “Weak means at least something, which is really good. The problem is when you have nothing.”

6. Maturation in the spleen [Slides p10-13]

  • B cells leaving the bone marrow are still functionally immature: IgM but not IgD; negative selection is still operative in the spleen [Slides p11]. They mature as they move through the spleen.
  • Inside the spleen: immature B cell, then transitional 1 (T1), transitional 2 (T2), then mature (naive) B cell once it fully expresses IgD in addition to IgM. Mature B cell = IgM + IgD (“they need both”).
  • Where they go: from the transitional stages they move to the B cell follicles of the spleen (garbled mention of the “T-cell area of the spleen” first [verify]) [Slides p13: mature primary B cells migrate to splenic lymphoid follicles].
  • Student question, “mIgM”: m = membrane. At these stages there is no antibody secretion: all Ig is on the cell surface.
  • How one cell makes both IgM and IgD [Slides p12]: the rearranged DNA still has all the C regions. A primary transcript containing VDJ + Cμ + Cδ is made; then mRNA processing chooses μ or δ, so the mature mRNA gives IgM or IgD.
    • Specificity is chosen at the DNA level (V(D)J recombination). IgM vs IgD is not recombination: it is chosen at the RNA level. Different processes at different moments in the cell’s life.
    • [Lucía] Once VDJ is established, only the constant region changes from here on. Immature cells have low IgD, mature cells high IgD.
  • Mature naive B cells [Slides p13]: high levels of IgM/IgD on the surface; recirculate between blood and lymphoid organs; with T-cell help respond to antigens by producing antibodies; half-life ~4.5 months in the periphery (lecturer: “about four months”).
    • They have never seen real antigens, but are now ready to become effectors.
    • They live in the lymphoid follicles of the spleen or other secondary lymphoid organs (e.g. lymph nodes) and circulate continuously in the blood.
    • Why circulate? The antigen does not come to you; by trafficking around the body the chances of encountering it increase.
    • On meeting their antigen, with help from other cells (future lectures), they differentiate into antibody-producing cells (transcript “plants”: plasma cells [verify]). New B cells are generated all the time.

9. Summary [Slides p17]

  • B cell development is a differentiation process generating mature B lymphocytes from precursors.
  • It takes place in the bone marrow and secondary lymphoid organs (spleen).
  • It includes proliferation, gene rearrangement, receptor expression, negative selection and acquisition of new functional properties.
  • It is associated with phenotypic changes (the B cell receptor and other molecules such as CD19 and CD20).
