1. Periplasmic space [Slides p2]

  • The gap between the plasma membrane and the cell wall in Gram-positives; in Gram-negatives, the space between the inner and outer membranes.
  • Gel-like; in E. coli more than 60 different proteins, all there for a reason:
FunctionLecturer[Slides p2]
Folding and quality controlProteins exported across the plasma membrane (to the periplasm or the outer membrane) are folded here; misfolded ones are degradedFold and quality control of proteins exported across the inner membrane
TransportExport of many proteins; import of nutrientsInside the cell: nutrients; outside: waste products
Synthesis of envelope macromoleculesMany enzymes for LPS synthesis (transcript “LPS and LPS”: the second is probably peptidoglycan)Peptidoglycan and LPS synthesis
Others(not mentioned)Flagella structure; secretion systems

2. Unusual cell walls [Slides p2-3]

  • Most bacteria are Gram-positive or Gram-negative, but some medically very important bacteria have unusual walls.

2a. Mycobacteria [Slides p2]

  • Main pathogen: Mycobacterium tuberculosis (others mentioned: M. bovis, M. leprae, transcript “bobbies”, “deprive” [verify]). “The biggest killer in history”: more deaths than from any other bacterium known, and still killing many people.
  • Wall: very hydrophobic, waxy, impermeable: excludes many molecules, including many antibiotics and disinfectants: part of why it is such a killer.
  • Lipid content of the cell wall:
Lecturer[Slides p2][Lucía]
Gram-negative~20%~25% (“in others”)
Gram-positive~5%
Mycobacteria~60%≈60%40% [check]
  • Three lipid sources:
    1. Mycolic acids: fatty acids with a 60-90 carbon chain, linked to the peptidoglycan by arabinogalactan (a polysaccharide) [Slides p2: C60-90; thick layer].
    2. Lipoarabinomannan.
    3. Surface glycolipids [Slides: “other glycolipids”].
  • [Lucía] Similar to Gram-positives but does not retain the Gram stain.

3. Secretion systems [Slides p3-4]

  • Bacteria make many products in the cytoplasm that must be secreted, many of them toxins (examples: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, both producing many toxins). Gram-positive or Gram-negative, they need to export them across the envelope.
  • Secretion systems [Slides p4]: molecular complexes embedded in the envelope that export substances to the outside or into another cell (eukaryotic or prokaryotic). 11 families (T1SS-T11SS) described so far, in both Gram-positives and Gram-negatives.
  • Mostly they act like a channel. Examples from the figure: type 1 (protein exported through one channel), type 2 (similar, different components), type 5 (one channel across the inner membrane, another across the outer membrane). Exact structures not needed.
  • Types 3, 4 and 6 (flagged): behave like a needle: they punch the membrane of a eukaryotic cell or another bacterium and inject toxins directly into its cytoplasm (type 6 into another bacterium) [Slides p4: T3SS, T4SS, T6SS; effectors].
    • Why: an immune evasion mechanism: the toxins are never outside the cell, so never recognized by antibodies (he referred to a previous class).

5. Outside the cell wall: capsule [Slides p5-6]

  • Structures outside the cell wall [Slides p5]: 1. capsule, 2. pili, 3. flagella.
  • Composition: outside the cell; ~99% polysaccharides: a repeating unit of one to four (or more) sugars, well organized, surrounding the whole cell “like an orange”, isolating it from the environment. Sometimes proteins or amino acids [Slides p5: sometimes peptides/proteins; well organized and not easily removed].
  • Not part of the cell wall: it gives no significant structural strength [Slides p5].
  • Gram-positive: capsule outside the peptidoglycan; Gram-negative: outside the outer membrane/LPS [Slides p6, figure].
  • Functions (“capsule, capsule, capsule… always critical”; he worked 25 years in microbiology research):
FunctionLecturer[Slides p5]
Protects against phagocytosisYesYes
AdhesionAn adhesin, “like a glue”, sticky: binds biotic and abiotic surfacesAssist in attachment to surfaces
BiofilmsImportant component: bacteria embedded in exopolysaccharideRole in development and maintenance of biofilms
ExclusionAgainst bacteriophages (viruses that kill bacteria), detergents, other chemicalsExcludes viruses and detergents
Desiccation(not mentioned)Prevents bacterial desiccation
  • Encapsulated pathogens [Slides p6] (flagged):
CapsuleBacteria
PolysaccharideNeisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Escherichia coli
Protein (exceptions, not to memorize)Bacillus anthracis: poly-D-glutamic acid, a single amino acid in the unusual D configuration; anthrax, “perhaps the deadliest bacterial infection”, mortality “might be 75%“. Yersinia pestis (bubonic plague): F1 capsular antigen, long fibres of protein monomers
  • Not every isolate is encapsulated: e.g. some N. meningitidis isolates are non-encapsulated. Encapsulated isolates are more virulent by default: N. meningitidis with capsule in the spinal fluid of an ICU patient “raises all the red flags”.
  • Virulence factor [Slides p6]: resist phagocytosis and sometimes treatment.
  • Vaccine component [Slides p6] (“the good news”): for a new bacterial problem, the capsule polysaccharide is the first vaccine candidate, “by far” the best (reasons not explained). He worked in vaccines for many years.
    • N. meningitidis: very deadly, progresses very quickly; five serotypes, each with a different capsule: A, C, W, Y and B. A tetravalent vaccine (A, C, W, Y) made of the purified capsular polysaccharides (then conjugated: “you don’t have to remember it”) [Slides p6: A, C, W, Y polysaccharide vaccine].
    • Type B: its capsule is very similar to one of our own structures, so the body does not make antibodies against it. After much effort, a protein-based type B vaccine came in 2015.
    • S. pneumoniae also has a capsule-based vaccine.

9. Reactive oxygen species and detoxification [Slides p9]

  • Any bacterium that uses O2 or lives in its presence can accumulate reactive oxygen species (ROS).
  • ROS = free radicals (molecules with an unpaired electron) that contain oxygen: superoxide anion, (hydrogen) peroxide, hydroxyl radical (as on his slide). Highly reactive: destroy lipids, nucleic acids, proteins, “anything”. They build up because of partial oxidation during growth (he did not explain further).
  • Enzymes:

10. Growth and binary fission [Slides p10-11]

10a. Binary fission [Slides p10]

  • Asexual reproduction. Contrast:
SexualAsexual (binary fission)
ParentsTwo, different genetic backgroundsOne
OffspringShare information from both parents but differ from each other (unless identical twins)Identical to each other and to the parent (“in theory”: not exactly identical)
  • “Growth” in microbiology = an increase in the number of cells, not in their size: the culture becomes more and more turbid [Slides p10].
  • Steps (an oversimplification of a hugely complex process):
    1. DNA replication: a single chromosome with a single origin of replication; replication is bidirectional.
    2. Cell elongation; the two chromosomes migrate towards the two poles.
    3. Septum formation: the septum narrows.
    4. Cell separation: two daughter cells.
  • [Slides p10] Each daughter cell receives a chromosome and enough copies of all other constituents to live independently.
  • Plasmids have their own origin of replication and also replicate as the chromosome does.
  • Generation time (not to memorize, except the principle): E. coli 20-30 min in optimal conditions [Slides p10: ~20 min]; the world record 10 min (transcript “Vibrillin atranges” [verify] the species); up to 2 weeks (transcript “Hycobacterium deprive”, probably Mycobacterium leprae [verify]). Depends on the bacterium and the conditions [Slides p10: nutritional, genetic factors, temperature etc].

10b. The bacterial growth curve [Slides p11]

  • Inoculate a culture (e.g. 100 bacteria), incubate with oxygen on top, measure the optical density (OD) at different times and plot it: the classical growth curve.
PhaseLecturer[Slides p11]
1. Lag (waiting, adaptation)Flat for minutes or hours: no division; sensing the new medium, producing enzymes and metabolitesNo growth; time between inoculation and start of growth; biosynthesis of new enzymes and required metabolites
2. Exponential1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32…: very rapid; plenty of nutrients, optimal temperature, oxygen: “everyone is happy”Most rapid growth; constant growth rate; cells typically healthiest
3. StationaryA critical element runs out (e.g. all the glucose, no carbon source; or all the O2): some die, some divide; roughly flatGrowth rate zero, some grow, others die; essential nutrient used up, waste accumulates, or O2 runs out; metabolism much reduced
4. Decline (death)Left longer, cells start to die; e.g. waste products accumulate and acidify the mediumIf incubation continues, cells eventually die
  • Anecdote (his lab, “yesterday”): a culture stopped growing; the measured pH showed the medium had acidified from waste products.

11. Planktonic vs biofilm growth [Slides p11-12]

  • Planktonic: bacteria growing independently, in suspension, in nature or in an infection (e.g. bacteria isolated in a bloodstream infection) [Slides p11].
  • Biofilms [Slides p11]: adherent bacterial communities embedded in a polymer matrix. With a surface and fluid, bacteria tend to bind (biotic or abiotic surfaces):
    1. Single bacteria attach and start producing polysaccharide (sticky) to hold on.
    2. They divide and stay attached: two-dimensional growth.
    3. Microcolonies: three-dimensional, growing upwards.
    4. Large structures, sometimes mushroom-shaped: the mature biofilm.
    5. The mature biofilm releases cells that colonize somewhere else.
  • [Slides p12] Scanning electron micrographs: S. aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus cereus (see the slide).
  • Where [Slides p12]: red = biofilms on tissues (biotic), purple = on implanted material (abiotic). His examples: wound infection; thick biofilms in the lungs of patients with cystic fibrosis (transcript “60-philosis” [verify]); medical implants, very common now: breast implants, pacemakers, catheters, prostheses.
  • Problem: biofilms are recalcitrant, difficult to eliminate [Slides p11: protected from desiccation, antimicrobials, the immune system and antibiotics: recalcitrant infections].
  • [Lucía p20-21] Biofilm growth can cause chronic infection, increases virulence, protects against the immune system and complement; cells at the bottom grow more slowly (less access to nutrients), so are less sensitive to antibiotics.

12. [Slides only] Endospores: not lectured yet [Slides p13-15]

On the slides after the point where he stopped; expect them next class. Listed so the note is complete:

  • Endospores: dormant, tough, non-reproductive structures of certain Gram-positives (Bacillus, Clostridium), formed in response to unfavourable conditions (e.g. nutrient limitation). Resist heat, desiccation, UV light, radiation, chemical agents, enzymes. Position, shape and size of the spore in the cell help identify the bacterium.
  • Sporulation: generally 6-8 hours; normally begins when growth stops for lack of nutrients; complex multistage process.
  • Layers (outside in): exosporium, protein coat (keratin-like), outer membrane, cortex (loosely cross-linked, modified peptidoglycan), spore wall (normal peptidoglycan), inner membrane, core. Core: complete chromosome, minimal proteins and ribosomes, SASPs, high Ca-dipicolinic acid (Ca-DPA), very low water, thermostable enzymes, impermeable layers.
  • Clinically relevant spore formers: Bacillus anthracis (anthrax; bioterrorism, 2001 attack), Clostridium tetani (tetanus), Clostridioides difficile (antibiotic-associated diarrhoea), Clostridium perfringens (gas gangrene, food poisoning), Clostridium botulinum (botulism, food spoilage). Hard to combat because the spores are hard to kill: autoclaves to sterilize material with spores.
  • [Lucía p6] Also Bacillus cereus (uncooked rice). See the slides for the figures.
