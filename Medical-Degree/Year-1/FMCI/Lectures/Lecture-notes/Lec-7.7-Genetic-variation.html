[Slides] Learning goals:

  • Individual genomes differ in single-nucleotide polymorphisms and in larger structural variants.
  • These variants have profound implications for predisposition to disease.

1. Why study genetic variation

  • Once the reference genome exists, the next question is where individual genomes differ.
  • For medicine: these differences underlie different susceptibility to disease. Not only single-gene genetic diseases, but common diseases with a genetic predisposition (diabetes, Alzheimer’s at 65, heart disease, cancer). This has been the focus of genetic research for about the last 20 years; covered in a future class and next year.

2. Classifying variants: small scale vs structural

  • The threshold is arbitrary: 50 nucleotides.
Small-scale variationStructural variation (SV)
SizeUnder 50 ntOver 50 nt
ExamplesSingle nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions, indels, microsatellitesMobile element insertions, large deletions, inversions, copy number variants (CNVs)
  • Some variants change the total amount of DNA, some do not:
    • No change in DNA content: a single nucleotide substitution (one person has T, another C); an inversion (a segment in opposite orientation), which may still affect how genes in the area work.
    • Change in DNA content: insertions (e.g. T vs CT), deletions, mobile element insertions, CNVs.
  • [Notes] The two broad categories are changes that do not affect DNA content and changes in copy number. In the 1000 Genomes Project, 1 to 50 nucleotides = small-scale; over 50 bp = structural variation (because short-read sequencing could not detect larger indels well). In the past, SV meant 1 kb or longer.

3. Types of small-scale variant

Reference genome on top, “my genome” below (examples on the slide; [Notes] Figure 1).

  • Deletion polymorphism: a segment missing. A deletion is not necessarily a disease mutation; it depends where it is (it may be outside a gene).
  • Insertion polymorphism: the reverse.
  • Indel: a deletion and an insertion at the same site (lecturer’s example: a stretch replaced by GCA). [Notes] Figure 1C: deletion of GGTTC plus insertion of CA. [Notes] “Indel” is also used broadly for any small insertion, duplication or deletion (1 to 50 nt in the 1000 Genomes Project).
  • Microsatellite (short tandem repeat): e.g. a CA dinucleotide repeated in tandem (“CACACA”), with 12, 15 or 20 repeats in different people. [Notes] Microsatellites vary in copy number but are classified as indels; Figure 1D: 12 vs 11 copies of CA.

Polymorphism and allele frequency

  • Polymorphism = a genetic element that appears in many different forms in the population.
  • Worked example (lecturer): 50 people in the room, chromosome 1, two alleles each = 100 alleles. Sequencing finds 96 alleles with the gene and 4 with a deletion: deletion frequency 4%. So it is a population polymorphism, not a unique mutation causing disease.
    • There is always a frequent allele (here: having the gene) and a rare allele (the variant). What matters is the frequency of the rare allele.
    • If 20% carry it, it is probably not very bad for health. If only two people carry it, it might be one reason they develop, for instance, diabetes. “So rare that it approaches the definition of a mutation.”
  • [Notes] Traditionally, a variant with population frequency over 0.01 (1%) was a polymorphism, below 0.01 a rare variant (arbitrary cut-off). The term is declining. Current classes: common (over 5%), low frequency (1 to 5%), rare (under 1%). In medicine, “polymorphism” often means a variant that does not cause disease and “mutation” a disease-causing one.

Microsatellites and copy number polymorphism

  • Recall from 7.5 (not recorded): satellite, minisatellite and microsatellite DNA, tandem repeats.
  • New point: in some tandem repeats the number of repeats differs between people (e.g. 3 in one person, 5 in another, 2 in another). That number is the polymorphism.
  • Microsatellite size (class Q&A): a student said “less than 100 base pairs”; he corrected that this is the total array; the repeat unit is small. He first thought 1 to 6; a student said 1 to 4 bp and he accepted it. [Notes] (Lecture 7.5 table): microsatellite arrays under 100 bp; repeat unit often 1 to 4 bp; widely dispersed throughout all chromosomes.
  • Typical example he gives: CAG repeated in tandem (a trinucleotide). Many microsatellites are polymorphic (10, 8, 12 copies…).
  • Uses: very useful for forensic DNA analysis (TBL 7.3). Some are implicated in cancer (“you will study that throughout your degree”).
  • Large repeats: an element of about 100 nt repeated in tandem whose number of repeats differs between people is also a copy number variant. The term CNV is usually reserved for the large ones, which are structural variation. [verify] The transcript calls these “segmental mutations”; probably “segmental duplications”, but the transcript is garbled.

4. How much variation? Numbers

  • Genome: 3 x 10^9 nucleotides.
  • SNVs per genome: about 4 to 5 million compared with any other person (the transcript’s “5 trillion” is a transcription error; he says 4 to 5 million several times).
  • Genomes are about 99.85% identical: roughly one difference every 700 nucleotides (elsewhere in the lecture he says one every 500 to 1,000, and 700 to 800).
  • Small-scale variation involves about 5 Mb of DNA, mostly SNVs.
  • Structural variants: about 2,000 per genome (textbook figure), much fewer than SNVs but much larger, so they involve more DNA: about 10 to 20 Mb. Latest studies claim about 20,000 SVs per genome.

  • [Lucía] A total of 68,000 SVs have been described in humans. About 100 million positions vary among all humans on the planet.

5. SNPs / SNVs

  • Historically called SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms), pronounced “snip”. Because “polymorphism” suggests something benign and some SNPs increase disease risk, the more neutral single nucleotide variant (SNV) is now preferred: a variant may be bad, good, or do nothing.
  • Each allele is double-stranded, but only one strand is written for clarity. At a given position one chromosome may have T and the homologous one C: a C/T polymorphism.
  • Heterozygote = the two alleles differ (TC or CT, which count as the same). Homozygote = identical (TT or CC).

Hardy-Weinberg (introduced here; detail in TBL 7.3)

  • Genotype frequencies: p² + 2pq + q² = 1, where p and q are the frequencies of the two alleles.
  • Lecturer’s example: T frequency p = 0.8, C frequency q = 0.2. Predicted: TT = p² = 64%, CC = q² = 4%, heterozygotes 2pq = 32%. (The transcript says “6.4%” for TT; 64% is the value that makes the three add up to 100%.)
  • Not in the class notes text for 7.7; to be covered in TBL 7.3 Forensic DNA analysis.

Reading SNVs on a sequencing trace

  • The sequencer gives a trace of coloured peaks, one per nucleotide (example on the slide).
  • One peak (e.g. only A) = both alleles the same, homozygous.
  • Two overlapping peaks (e.g. blue and red, C and T) at the same position = heterozygous. It is a polymorphism if it also appears in other people.
  • [Lucía] Single peaks = homozygous; smaller double peaks = two different nucleotides (maternal and paternal).
  • Asked if the class had seen sequencing techniques: no.

What the SNVs do

  • Of the 4 to 5 million SNVs in a typical genome:
    • About 10,000 are potentially dangerous because they lie inside genes and change an amino acid.
    • Others in genes are silent: the genetic code is degenerate (two different codons can code for the same amino acid), so the amino acid does not change. (The class had the genetic code lecture later that day.)
    • About 25 to 30 have previously been associated with disease in other people (“30”, then “25” in the transcript). Whether you will develop that disease is an open question.
    • Many do not overlap genes but overlap regulatory regions (promoters, regions dictating how genes are expressed).
  • [Lucía] 25 to 30 disease-associated SNPs per person; about 500,000 overlap regulatory regions.

6. Geographic distribution of variation

  • Data from a project that sequenced millions of SNVs in many people from many parts of the world. [verify] The transcript calls it “a map project”. [Notes] Figure 2 is from the 1000 Genomes Project (2,504 individuals, 26 populations); [Notes] also mentions the HapMap project. Check which one the slide figure is.
  • Populations on the figure (lecturer): European (Italy, Caucasians living in Utah, Great Britain, Finland); East Asian (Chinese, Japanese, Vietnam); South Asian (Bangladesh, Punjab…); Central and South American (Mexico, Puerto Rico, Colombia, Peru); African (Yoruba from Ibadan in Nigeria, Sierra Leone, African Americans).
  • Finding: far more genetic variation in Africa than in the rest of the world.
  • Student suggestions discussed: sun exposure (no: about the same across the equatorial region), retroviruses (no: these are SNVs), large continent with varied climates, environment, more people.
  • Explanation (lecturer): out-of-Africa and the bottleneck effect.
    • Anatomically modern humans originated in Africa (earlier species: heidelbergensis, erectus). All humans were in Africa until about 100,000 years ago; only a few thousand.
    • Our species left Africa in several waves: first to South Asia, then the Far East, the last into Europe (then under glaciation), and eventually across the Bering Strait.
    • Only a few groups of a few hundred people left, carrying only some of the variants present in Africa: a bottleneck effect. New variants arose later, but never matched the variability inside Africa, which kept accumulating its own.
    • So two people living 100 km apart in Africa are genetically more different than the lecturer and a Chinese person.
  • Why Central and South America show a wider spread: admixture. Present-day populations mix native genomes with genomes that arrived from Europe and Africa (colonization by Spaniards, Vikings before, and migration from Africa; a student suggested slavery) in the 15th to 17th centuries: introgression of new variants. Modern migration is also changing global variation.
  • [Notes] Individuals show 3.90 to 5.05 million variant sites per genome. African populations show considerably greater diversity, consistent with out-of-Africa; Central and South American populations show elevated diversity that roughly correlates with the degree of recent African ancestry (Figure 2).
  • [Lucía] Two people from the same African city differ more than two individuals from different parts of the world.

7. Structural variants and homozygous gene loss

  • SV types: inversions, deletions, mobile element insertions, CNVs (summary figure from the textbook on the slide).
  • Mobile element insertions: some people carry insertions of retrotransposons that others lack; a child may carry a new insertion not present in the parents. A very common type of SV that increases DNA content (e.g. a 200 to 300 nt element copied to a new location). [Slides] “One AluY new germ-line insertion per 20 births” (Lec 7.5 section).
  • Large deletions often contain one or two genes. A heterozygous carrier has one copy instead of two; a homozygote has no copy of that gene.
  • Each of us has on average about 100 genes homozygously deleted, and these are healthy people.
  • Why are we healthy?
    • The obvious answer (student): the genes may be redundant; other genes do the same thing.
    • The other way round (lecturer): perhaps we are not so healthy after all. These deletions might be what gives Alzheimer’s or diabetes in 10 to 20 years. Not a congenital syndrome, but something subtle that increases the risk of common diseases.
  • [Notes] About 100 protein-coding genes are homozygously deleted in healthy individuals, suggesting they are non-essential; they usually belong to large gene families and may be redundant or have late-onset effects that do not substantially reduce fitness. It was unexpected that a “normal” genome has about 2,000 SVs; large gains or losses had been assumed to be pathogenic.
  • [Notes] Balanced SVs (same DNA content): inversions (a fragment rotated 180 degrees between two double-strand breaks) and balanced translocations. Inversions can still cause disease: hemophilia A, 45% of cases due to an inversion in the factor VIII gene on the X chromosome. Unbalanced SVs: mostly CNVs (copy number of a sequence over 50 nt, arising by tandem duplication; often multiallelic) plus large insertions and deletions (1 or 0 copies). Also called copy number polymorphisms (CNPs) or low-copy repeats (LCRs).

8. Unequal crossing over and genomic disorders

  • In meiosis, homologous chromosomes align and recombine. Repeated sequences such as segmental duplications (nearly identical copies) can cause misalignment of the chromatids.
  • A crossover in the misaligned region = unequal crossing over: one chromatid ends up with a duplication, the other with a deletion of the region.
  • These cause syndromes called genomic disorders. Example in the notes: DiGeorge syndrome, a deletion syndrome caused by unequal crossing over between segmental duplications.
  • People with such a deletion have the disease, so it is not a polymorphism.
  • [Notes] Structural variants (a few kb to a few Mb) are too small to see under the microscope; a 3 Mb deletion or duplication is invisible on a standard karyotype but can alter the dosage of dozens of genes. Example: microdeletion syndromes such as DiGeorge (chromosome 22).
