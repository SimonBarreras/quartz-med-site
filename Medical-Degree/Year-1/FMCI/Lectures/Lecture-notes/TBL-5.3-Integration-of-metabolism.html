Exam flags from the lecturer

  • Why fatty acids cannot give net glucose (section 5): “a typical question at the exam”.
  • Odd-chain fatty acids as a glucose source: “another typical question”, though “not strongly important”.
  • Advice: draw the cycles (Cori, glucose-alanine, fasting) on a small piece of paper to fix them.

1. Cori cycle (muscle and liver)

  • Triggered by intense exercise: muscle energy comes from glycogen to glucose to pyruvate.
  • There is not enough O2 to reoxidize the NADH to NAD+, but glycolysis needs cytosolic NAD+. Escape route: pyruvate is reduced to lactate with those electrons.
  • Lactate still contains carbon and energy, but cannot be used in the muscle: it travels in the blood to the liver, which makes glucose by gluconeogenesis and sends it back to the muscle. This closes the cycle.
  • [Lucía] Lactate goes into circulation so as not to lower the pH in the muscle cell.
  • Blind-cycle exercise from class: the right-hand tissue (muscle) uses glucose, makes fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, splits it into glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and DHAP, then to phosphoenolpyruvate, pyruvate and K = lactate.

2. Fuels of muscle

  • Strong exercise (start of exercise): glycogen and glucose, producing lactate (Cori cycle).
  • Light activity or rest: mainly fatty acids, a little glucose, and a small amount of ketone bodies (at rest, with balanced nutrition, little ketogenesis).
  • Phosphocreatine: a quick reserve; its phosphate rephosphorylates ADP, catalyzed by creatine kinase.
  • Clinical: high blood creatine kinase means tissue damage, mainly muscle (also heart, e.g. heart attack).

3. Liver in prolonged fasting and untreated type 1 diabetes

  1. Low blood glucose is sensed by the pancreas, which releases glucagon.
  2. In adipose tissue, glucagon binds its GPCR: G-alpha exchanges GDP for GTP and separates from beta-gamma; G-alpha-s activates adenylyl cyclase; cAMP activates PKA, which activates hormone-sensitive lipase. TAG is broken down and fatty acids are released (Lec 5.9 Fatty acid catabolism and ketone bodies).
  3. Fatty acids reach the liver, are activated to acyl-CoA, cross the carnitine shuttle and are oxidized to acetyl-CoA.
  4. The liver is also doing gluconeogenesis, which uses oxaloacetate. Acetyl-CoA cannot enter the Krebs cycle, so it is converted into ketone bodies, exported as fuel for peripheral tissues.
  • Untreated type 1 diabetes: no insulin, so high blood glucose but none inside the tissues, because insulin is needed to bring glucose transporters to the membrane. The body behaves as in fasting.
  • Ketone body production must be monitored: high levels lower blood pH (ketoacidosis).
  • [check] The lecturer called glucagon’s receptor “the beta-adrenergic glucagon receptor”; beta-adrenergic receptors bind adrenaline. In the 5.9 lecture he described it as the glucagon receptor (both are Gs-coupled GPCRs; Lucía: glucagon and epinephrine both trigger lipolysis).
  • [Lucía] Fatty acids travel bound to serum albumin; in the liver acetyl-CoA becomes ketone bodies (hydroxybutyrate), converted back to acetyl-CoA in the brain or other tissues. Diabetics make them because their body does not detect the glucose in the blood.

4. Fuels of the brain

  • Mainly glucose: on average 100-200 g per day (depends on size and activity).
  • Without enough glucose, the brain uses beta-hydroxybutyrate (and acetoacetate) for a short period. Brain, muscle and heart can use ketone bodies for a short time.
  • Ketone bodies: acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. No tissue is adapted to use acetone.
  • [check] The transcript lists “hair” among the tissues using ketone bodies; it is the heart.

5. Can we make glucose from these carbons? (typical exam questions)

Carbon sourceNet glucose?Why
Alanine (keto acid: pyruvate)YesPyruvate is gluconeogenic
Aspartate (keto acid: oxaloacetate)Yes
Glutamate (keto acid: alpha-ketoglutarate)YesNot directly to PEP, but via succinyl-CoA, succinate, fumarate, malate, oxaloacetate
Even-chain fatty acids (acetyl-CoA)NoSee below
Odd-chain fatty acids (last turn gives propionyl-CoA)Yes, the propionyl partPropionyl-CoA becomes succinyl-CoA, which gives net oxaloacetate

Why acetyl-CoA gives no net oxaloacetate: acetyl-CoA (2 C) condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate, then isocitrate; isocitrate dehydrogenase and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase each release one CO2. So the 2 C that entered leave before oxaloacetate is regenerated: no net gain (Lec 5.8 Amino acid catabolism and the urea cycle, section 8).

Student question: “doesn’t pyruvate have the same problem?” No, because pyruvate used for gluconeogenesis does not enter the Krebs cycle: in the mitochondrion it is carboxylated and leaves as malate, which is reoxidized to oxaloacetate.

  • [check] The transcript says pyruvate “is carboxylated to malate”; Lucía’s gluconeogenesis notes: pyruvate carboxylase turns pyruvate into oxaloacetate in the mitochondria. Review gluconeogenesis (lecture-5-5-gluconeogenesis-and-glycogen-metabolism-notes) for the malate step.

6. Glucose-alanine cycle in fasting

  • In long fasting (hypoglycemia) with continued activity, muscle uses alternative fuels: fatty acids and proteins.
  • Protein use starts with transamination; the nitrogen goes to the liver, the only tissue that converts it into urea.
  • The resulting keto acids feed gluconeogenesis (section 5).

7. Receptor review (GPCRs)

  • Main receptor types: GPCRs, tyrosine kinase receptors, ion channels (ligand-gated), and intracellular receptors (e.g. steroid hormones).
  • GPCR = G protein-coupled receptor: 7 transmembrane domains, coupled to heterotrimeric G proteins (not monomeric ones such as Ras, which GPCRs can only affect indirectly).
  • Signalling depends on the G-alpha type:
    • Gs: activates adenylyl cyclase (cAMP up).
    • Gi: inhibits adenylyl cyclase.
    • Gq: activates phospholipase C, which hydrolyzes PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate), producing IP3. IP3 opens Ca2+ channels of the endoplasmic reticulum; free Ca2+ activates other proteins (e.g. PKC) and membrane channels.
  • cAMP is a soluble second messenger: the signal moves from the membrane into the cytoplasm. It acts on proteins with cAMP-binding domains, chiefly PKA (cAMP-dependent protein kinase).
