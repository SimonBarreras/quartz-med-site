2. Gefitinib: what kind of inhibitor

  • EGFR is a tyrosine kinase receptor.
  • Gefitinib competes with ATP inside the catalytic (kinase) domain of EGFR: a competitive inhibitor.
  • Is it also allosteric? Students said yes; the lecturer: no, “because it binds at the same place where the substrate” binds. Definitions to know for the exam.
  • [Notes] gefitinib inhibits the receptor by blocking ATP binding to the intracellular kinase domain.
  • Chemical structure: hydrophobic (“a lot of phenol groups”), so it crosses the membrane; this matters because the ATP site is intracellular. Inside, it binds the kinase domain and competes with ATP. Only for tumours carrying the sensitizing mutation.

3. Next generation sequencing (NGS) and precision medicine

  • Precision medicine is based mainly on NGS.
  • Before: about 500 nucleotides per week, using radioactivity; very costly and slow.
  • The Human Genome Project (human genome and other genomes, e.g. pathogens) drove new methods; at the beginning of the 21st century NGS appeared.
  • Now: a full genome in less than a week; hotspot genes in less than 24 hours: a very quick molecular diagnosis and quick precise treatment.
  • NGS: highly sensitive, many genes at once, a very small sample (small biopsy), cost-effective, time frame compatible with clinical use.
  • Developers recognized this year with the Princess of Asturias award: Shankar Balasubramanian and a second name garbled (“pastor mayor”) [verify].
  • This type of lung cancer is not sensitive to chemotherapy such as cisplatin (very toxic, used for other tumours). Without targeted options: “we don’t have anything for you… probably you will die.”
  • Panel: for this type of adenocarcinoma, a panel named Oncomine: transcribed as 165 genes with hotspot mutations and 303 genes with focal CNV (he did not remember the term’s translation) [verify] the figures. Different adenocarcinomas differ; the panel identifies the specific type.
  • [Lucía] NGS can test thousands of genes in a very small sample (high sensitivity).

4. Review: membrane receptors and signalling

  • Three main types of membrane receptors: GPCRs, enzyme-linked receptors (e.g. receptor tyrosine kinases), ion channel receptors. [Slides U4] same three groups (ligand-gated ion channels, GPCRs, enzymatic or catalytic). Intracellular receptors are a separate type.

GPCRs

  • The receptor is a seven-transmembrane-domain protein coupled to a heterotrimeric G protein (alpha, beta, gamma subunits). Students: the subunits are not part of the receptor itself.
  • Ligand binds outside, conformational change, GDP exchanged for GTP on alpha; alpha-GTP is released from beta-gamma and from the receptor.
G alphaEffect
GsStimulates adenylyl cyclase: cAMP increases
GiInhibits adenylyl cyclase: cAMP decreases
GqActivates phospholipase C (PLC), which splits PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate) into DAG (diacylglycerol: hydrophobic, stays in the membrane) and IP3 (hydrophilic): IP3 increases
  • “There are many others but these three are the principal ones.”

Receptor tyrosine kinases (EGFR)

  • Enzymatic activity: tyrosine kinase.
  • Ligand binding, then cross-phosphorylation; then docking of molecules, driven by GRB2.
  • GRB2 attracts SOS, a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF): exchanges GDP for GTP.
  • RAS, at the membrane, is a small G protein that works like the G alpha subunit; GDP to GTP makes RAS active.
  • Cascade: RAS activates RAF, RAF (a kinase) phosphorylates MEK (with intermediates), MEK phosphorylates ERK. ERK goes to the nucleus and changes the expression pattern.
  • Genes activated: inhibition of apoptosis, metastasis (proteases of the extracellular matrix), angiogenesis (more nutrients and oxygen), evasion, proliferation: a more competitive tumour cell.
  • [Slides U4] RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK steps: 1 ligand (EGF) binds EGFR; 2 receptor activation by cross-phosphorylation; 3 RAS activated (GDP to GTP); 4 RAF; 5 MEK; 6 ERK (MAPK); 7 ERK nuclear translocation, gene activation, protein synthesis; 8 negative feedback.

Kinases

  • Kinase activity: phosphorylate (and activate) a substrate. Types: tyrosine kinases, serine/threonine kinases.
  • [Slides U4] kinases transfer phosphate groups from ATP to a substrate; phosphorylation can activate or inactivate proteins.

Ion channels

  • Two types by trigger: voltage-gated and ligand-gated.

5. The EGFR mutation

  • Team task: find the EGFR gene and locate the mutation. Demonstration: search Ensembl (transcript “Sambo”) for EGFR, select a transcript (the number of transcripts is garbled [verify]), get the cDNA sequence and its translation.
  • NGS found a mutation at nucleotide ~2570 (as transcribed) in a codon (CTG) for leucine, changed to a codon for arginine: a missense mutation. Amino acid position garbled (“850…”, later “888”, with the ATP binding site at “855”) [verify]. [Lucía] the mutation is L858R in exon 21.
  • Leucine to arginine: a strong change, “probably because of the charge” (also size).
  • Exon distribution in this lung cancer (as transcribed):
ExonAlterationFrequency
19In-frame deletions49%
20Duplications or insertions9%
21This point mutation39%
  • [Lucía] L858R in exon 21 is the mutation in 40% of the cases caused by point mutations; also in-frame deletions; hotspots are regions of oncogenes where activating mutations are frequent.
  • Profile: typical in non-smokers, mainly women (“Don’t worry… don’t start to smoke please”). [Lucía] adenocarcinomas, non-smokers, Asian and young females.

Where is it and what does it do?

  • UniProt predicts the protein’s domains from the amino acid sequence: the mutation is very close to the ATP binding site, in the protein kinase domain.
  • So the mutation affects ATP binding. More or less ATP binding? Less would not cause a tumour; the receptor is overactive, so the lecturer: the mutation increases the affinity for ATP and increases kinase activity: promoting ligand binding, dimerization, autophosphorylation and cascade activation.
  • [check] [Lucía] with L858R, Km goes up, but kcat increases even more, so efficiency increases; so strong it does not even need the ligand for dimerization and activation. [Slides U4] the mutation increases kinase activity and reduces dependence on the ligand; proliferative signalling can persist without ligand.
  • It is an activating mutation: more downstream signalling; the last effector is the transcription factor ERK, so more ERK reaches the nucleus.

ERK in the nucleus

  • ERK must be phosphorylated. Does it then cross the nuclear pore on its own? The lecturer: a protein (transcript garbled) binds phosphorylated ERK and a chaperone transports it through the pore [verify].
  • In the nucleus a transcription factor binds a specific promoter, recognizing a consensus sequence (otherwise it would bind everywhere), and activates transcription.

6. From mutation to drug

  • Basic research plus clinical knowledge: explain the problem to chemists and biochemists, who find the 3D structure of the site; with algorithms (AI) find molecules that fit there (here, the ATP binding site).
  • The molecule should be small and specific, to avoid off-target binding.
  • Then cellular assays, animal assays, clinical trials (phase 2, phase 3…).

7. Resistance

TypeDefinition (student, accepted)
PrimaryPresent from the start
AcquiredGained over time, after treatment
  • This patient: acquired resistance (two years of response).
  • Cause: not the drug becoming inactive but a second mutation, close to the first: at position 790, T790M (threonine to methionine; transcript “Pleramine” for threonine). Somatic missense: the codon was ACG (threonine); the C has been changed to a T, and the new codon codes for methionine.
  • About 60% of these tumours acquire this second mutation. [Lucía] T790M accounts for 60% of secondary resistance mutations; missense somatic; in the tyrosine kinase domain.
  • What to do: sequence again: a small biopsy, results in hours.
  • Consequence (student, accepted): the new change alters the shape of the site where the inhibitor binds: gefitinib no longer fits in the pocket, but ATP still does, so kinase activity is not inhibited.
  • In vitro assay (cells): black = wild-type EGFR: not sensitive to gefitinib; green = initial mutation: sensitive; red = both mutations: behave like wild type.
  • [Lucía] primary resistance can be caused by mutations in KRAS (pathway always active independently of EGFR, so EGFR TKIs have no effect), Mdm2.

8. Osimertinib

  • A second TKI for tumours with the secondary mutation: same binding site, but it fits much better into the rearranged site.
  • Before osimertinib she also had brain tumours (difficult in lung cancer); after treatment the brain lesions were no longer visible (timing transcribed “30, 13 days after” [verify]). Response in August 2015.
  • Both drugs were then used (gefitinib and osimertinib), “because we can have some cells that only were the first ones” (lecturer accepted a student’s answer).
  • [Lucía] osimertinib blocks the ATP site even with T790M; the tumour DNA must first be sequenced; it crosses the blood-brain barrier and reaches brain metastases; resistance to osimertinib can develop, so sequence again.

9. A third mutation: BRAF

  • If a third mutation appears downstream of EGFR in the cascade: a student drew the cascade (receptor and phosphotyrosine, GRB2, SOS as GEF, RAS exchanging GDP for GTP, RAF with kinase activity phosphorylating MEK, MEK phosphorylating ERK).
  • Why does a BRAF mutation confer resistance to EGFR TKIs? TKIs inhibit at the receptor; a mutation in RAF bypasses that step: the cascade is activated directly, whatever happens at the receptor; SOS and RAS activation are no longer needed.
  • After clinical discussion: dabrafenib (direct BRAF inhibitor) and trametinib (MEK inhibitor): blocking downstream.
  • The more steps blocked, the more toxic the treatment, because other cells also need this pathway.
  • Initial response, then progression; she died. “We gave her four years of life”, years of good life with her family. The tumour keeps trying to bypass the drugs because it is highly dependent on this pathway.
  • [Lucía] an additional activating RAF mutation was detected; treated with dabrafenib and trametinib plus osimertinib; this is targeted therapy, a type of personalized or precision medicine.

10. Oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes

  • Tumour suppressor genes protect the genome: they help reduce the number of mutations; the more mutations accumulate, the higher the chance of a tumour. Part of ageing: the activity of these genes decreases. Example: p53.
  • Oncogenes: mutated forms of normal genes (proto-oncogenes) that drive tumorigenesis. EGFR, RAS and RAF in this case.
  • First human oncogene identified: RAS, in the 1980s, by three Spanish researchers at the National Cancer Institute in Maryland: one named Mariano (very well known; a director of a Spanish institution: garbled), Eugenio Santos (still living), and a third (name not recorded). The lecturer worked with one of them.
  • Anecdote: in the US, a New York colleague greeted him every morning with a joke the lecturer later turned into a “mutation” pun (passage garbled).
