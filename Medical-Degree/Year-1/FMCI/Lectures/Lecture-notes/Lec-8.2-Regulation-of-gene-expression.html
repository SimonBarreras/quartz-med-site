1. Why gene expression must be regulated

  • Humans and other mammals and birds have a very complex system for regulating gene expression, first because we are multicellular with different tissues.
  • A neuron is a neuron and not a skin cell because, from the same genome, different sets of genes have been activated or repressed; that leads to differentiation.
  • In the embryo (from one cell to all tissues), at specific times (e.g. day 21) and in specific parts, some genes are turned on and others off. The same happens in the stem cells of adult organs.
  • Main sequences regulating expression: promoters. But chromatin is tightly packed and transcription factors need access, so chromatin configuration also influences expression.
  • Plan of the lecture: opening and closing chromatin (histone acetylation); enhancers, which give tissue-specific and time-specific expression (promoters are usually general); the chromatin conformation needed for enhancer-promoter contact; newer mechanisms: microRNAs (“a Nobel Prize about two or three years ago”) and long non-coding RNAs (“probably a Nobel Prize in five to ten years”).
  • [Notes] Radio metaphor: the basal transcription apparatus is the ON switch (ubiquitous factors on the core promoter); additional circuits act as the volume control (non-ubiquitous factors on other cis elements, co-activators and co-repressors, lncRNAs and microRNAs). Post-transcriptional control and chromatin domains also matter.

2. Cis vs trans regulation

  • Terms borrowed from chemistry (cis and trans configurations of molecules).
  • Cis: the regulatory element is on the same DNA molecule as the gene it regulates. Example: genes A and B with their promoters, and a long-distance element (e.g. an enhancer) on the same DNA that interacts with the promoter.
  • Trans: the regulator comes from a different molecule, e.g. a gene on another chromosome. Example (anticipating microRNAs): another gene makes an RNA that binds the mRNA of gene A and destroys it, so no protein is made. It regulates A not at the promoter but by breaking down its mRNA.
  • Transcription factors are also trans-regulatory elements: proteins made elsewhere that bind specific DNA sequences.
  • [Notes] Box 1: a cis-acting DNA element acts only on the DNA molecule it sits on (the promoter of the paternal insulin gene on chromosome 11 regulates only the paternal allele). A cis-acting RNA sequence regulates the transcript it is on (often in UTRs). A trans-acting regulator (protein or RNA) diffuses and regulates both alleles of distant genes, often many genes. Transcription factors, lncRNAs and microRNAs are encoded away from their targets, so they are trans-acting.

3. The core promoter (recap of transcription)

  • The transcription start site (TSS) marks the beginning of the first exon. Upstream is the core promoter, about 100 to 200 nucleotides, where RNA polymerase II and a whole complex of proteins (transcription factors) bind.
  • TATA box, bound by TATA-box binding protein (TBP).
  • Another regulatory element whose name is garbled in the transcript (“brain, whatever”). [verify] Possibly the BRE: [Notes] Figure 2 lists BRE, TATA box, Inr and DPE as core promoter elements.
  • GC box: very rich in G and C, usually about 100 nucleotides upstream of the TSS; bound by the transcription factor SP1.
  • These sequences bound by transcription factors make up the core promoter: “this is what really turns on this gene”.
  • Covered in detail in the transcription lecture by Dr. “Matthias Avila” (transcript spelling). [verify] name.
  • Transcription factor families: some bind the double helix through a motif garbled in the transcript; others are leucine zippers (“like zippers, with many leucines”). [Notes] Figure 5: zinc fingers and leucine zippers; a factor recognizes a short sequence of 4 to 9 nucleotides; only a tiny fraction of potential binding sites are used, because the site must be accessible and binding is combinatorial.
  • [Lucía] TBP binds the TATA box, SP1 binds GC boxes; when the factors assemble, RNA polymerase is phosphorylated, binds and initiates transcription.

4. Enhancers and the mediator complex

  • New point: long-distance regulation. Far upstream (kilobases or even megabases away) are enhancers, which bind transcription factors.
  • The mediator complex (“the name says it all”: it brings two things together) binds the transcription factors on the enhancer and those on the promoter: protein-protein interactions.
  • Often the core promoter alone is not enough: it is “ready, waiting for the final push”. The push comes when the enhancer binds its transcription factors, recruits mediator, and the final configuration triggers expression.
  • Problem: an enhancer up to a million nucleotides away can only reach the promoter if chromatin forms loops (cohesin, see Lec 7.4 Levels of chromatin packaging).
  • [Notes] Enhancers amplify transcription, silencers repress it; both are often far from the promoter, even inside introns of the same or other genes. The intervening DNA must loop out (Figure 4B). Boundary elements (insulators) block inappropriate enhancer-promoter interactions; CTCF acts as insulator at loop domains and TADs.
  • [Lucía] Enhancers about 100 kb upstream; activators bound to enhancers form enhanceosomes, which bend DNA by looping and interact with co-activators such as HATs; contact with the promoter through Mediator triggers assembly of RNA polymerase and general transcription factors.

5. Opening chromatin: histone acetylation

  • For transcription factors to bind, compacted chromatin (nucleosomes, solenoid…) must be opened, relaxed at that point. The most important modification for opening or closing chromatin is histone acetylation.
  • Histone octamers have N-terminal tails that protrude; they contain several lysines.
  • Lysine can be acetylated by histone acetylases or deacetylated by histone deacetylases.
  • Mechanism: DNA is negatively charged, lysines positively charged; the attraction draws DNA and histones of neighbouring nucleosomes together. Acetylation removes the positive charge, releasing the interaction: open chromatin.
  • Open, relaxed regions = acetylated histones (he mentions “lysine 27”, to be seen in the next lecture); compact regions = deacetylated histones.
  • “This is the first thing you need”: without access to the DNA there is no way to regulate anything.
  • [Notes] Chromatin packaging imposes a basic level of repression. Open chromatin regions are detected as DNase I hypersensitive sites (DHS) (DNase I can only cut accessible DNA). Accessible sites include all gene promoters (about 70,000) plus millions of regulatory elements (enhancers, silencers), which explain cell-type-specific expression.
  • [Lucía] HATs (histone acetyltransferases) and HDACs (histone deacetylases). DHS: 3,000,000 found; very tissue-specific (50% active in one cell line, about 1% (4,000) in all). [check] She gives each DHS as “100-200 Mb” and about 18.7% of the genome devoted to regulation; “Mb” is likely a slip for bp, and the class notes do not give these figures.

6. Example: PAX6 and its remote enhancers

  • PAX6 is very important for eye formation in humans and most animals (“since octopus” in the transcript [verify]); during development it organizes the whole eye and must be expressed in the eye-forming structures. “I think we have this in the notes.”
  • PAX6 lies next to ELP4, an unrelated gene with many exons and a long intron.
  • Inside the long intron of ELP4 are two elements regulating PAX6:
    • promoter of PAX6 contacts one element: expression specifically in the lens (crystalline);
    • contacts the other: expression specifically in the retina.
  • So tissue-specific regulatory elements can be far away, even inside an intron of another gene. Contact needs proteins on the enhancer (transcription factors), the RNA polymerase II complex on the promoter (dozens of factors), and the mediator complex.
  • [check] The transcript says PAX6 “only has one exon”; [Notes] Figure 4A says PAX6 is drawn as a single box including exons and introns only for clarity (30 kb gene).
  • [Notes] Figure 4A: about 260 kb region; PAX6 is mutated in aniridia.

7. TADs and CTCF boundaries

  • Enhancer-promoter contact is possible only if the conformation allows it, e.g. both in the same TAD. Across a boundary the interaction is not possible.
  • Recall from the chromatin lecture: the TAD boundaries (yellow arrows on the slide) are CTCF binding sites.
  • Example (slide figure): two TADs, each with a gene and its enhancers; one gene expressed in the kidney, the other in the brain.
  • If a mutation prevents CTCF binding at the boundary, the two TADs merge into one large TAD; sequences can now interact across it, and a gene can be driven by the other gene’s enhancer: wrong expression (e.g. in the brain).
  • Promoters and enhancers are fine; only the chromatin conformation is wrong, yet expression changes in important ways.
  • [Lucía] Same example with brain and liver; flanked TADs do not contact each other; CTCF disruption gives a large TAD where enhancers activate additional genes, with physiological consequences.

8. MicroRNAs: post-transcriptional silencing

  • Very short RNAs (miRNAs), part of the short non-coding RNA genes (see Lec 7.6 Protein-coding and non-coding genes).
  • Known first in plants; nobody suspected them in humans until the human genome was sequenced and short genes making non-protein RNAs were found.

Biogenesis

  1. A short gene (single exon) is transcribed into an RNA of about 100 to 150 nucleotides (“100 million parts” in the transcript).
  2. The RNA folds on itself (complementary bases, some mismatches, a bubble) into a hairpin.
  3. In the nucleus, the Drosha complex trims it into the pre-microRNA.
  4. Exported to the cytoplasm, where Dicer (“because it dices”) cuts it further, releasing the loop and leaving a microRNA duplex: two strands, not perfectly complementary but stable.
  5. One strand is degraded; the remaining single strand is the active microRNA.
  • [Notes] Mature miRNAs are typically 20 to 22 nucleotides; Dicer cleaves asymmetrically leaving overhanging 3’ ends; the degraded strand is the passenger strand, the remaining one the guide strand.

Action

  • The active miRNA binds the 3’ UTR of target mRNAs (the transcribed region after the stop codon), by partial complementarity: not 100%, but enough bases to bind despite mismatches.
  • Binding prevents translation or leads to degradation of the mRNA: less protein. MicroRNAs are inhibitory.
  • One miRNA can act on several genes, and one mRNA can carry sites for several different miRNAs. We have hundreds to thousands of miRNA genes.
  • Another layer: even with a perfect promoter, if the mRNAs are degraded there is no protein.
  • [Notes] Pairing of the seed sequence (about the first 8 nucleotides from the 5’ end) is important; mismatches are tolerated elsewhere. Several hundred miRNA genes, often tissue-specific; at least 50% of protein-coding genes are thought to be regulated by miRNAs. Example: PTEN mRNA has binding sites in its 3’ UTR for seven miRNA families.
  • [Notes] Box 2: related RNA interference (Dicer, siRNA, Argonaute, RISC) protects cells against viruses and transposons. [Lucía] RISC (RNA-induced silencing complex) performs the silencing once the miRNA finds its target.

9. Competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs): “sponges”

  • Pseudogenes (copies of real genes that do not work) are sometimes transcribed into an mRNA that makes no protein. lncRNAs are transcribed, spliced, exported to the cytoplasm, but not translated.
  • One function: acting as sponges for microRNAs. A lncRNA may contain short regions perfectly complementary to a miRNA; several miRNA molecules bind it and are removed from the cytoplasm. They can no longer reach their target gene (e.g. insulin), which is then not inactivated and is expressed.
  • “You have the inhibitor and you have inhibitors of the inhibitor.”
  • Called ceRNAs: competing (they compete with the mRNAs for miRNA binding) endogenous (made by the cell itself) RNAs.
  • Summary: gene expression must be regulated within very narrow limits, hence so many layers: core promoter, enhancers, chromatin configuration, then destroying mRNAs and “destroying the destroyers”. Networks refined over millions of years give the right levels of our 20,000 protein-coding genes.
  • [Notes] Example: PTEN (10q23, a tightly controlled tyrosine phosphatase; aberrant expression common in cancers) is regulated by the processed pseudogene PTENP1 (9p21), whose non-coding RNA keeps many PTEN 3’ UTR miRNA sites and sequesters those miRNAs (Figure 7). ceRNAs include pseudogene RNAs, lncRNAs and circular RNAs (human fibroblasts make more than 25,000 different circular RNAs).
  • [Lucía] lncRNAs, pseudogenes and circular RNAs (from alternative splicing of exons or from introns) all act as sponges.

10. lncRNAs recruit chromatin-modifying complexes (lead-in to epigenetics)

  • Another lncRNA function: binding a region of the genome with one domain and, with another domain, binding chromatin-remodelling (chromatin-modifying) complexes.
  • These complexes are enzymes that add or remove chemical groups on histone tails. Brought to a promoter by the lncRNA, they can add groups that prevent transcription; other modifiers remove them and allow transcription.
  • Next lecture: all the histone modifications (acetylation already seen) that affect transcription.
  • [Notes] Long ncRNAs recruit chromatin-modifying complexes to defined loci, often repressive ones, without needing base pairing (Figure 8). Most act in cis; some in trans, e.g. HOTAIR (from the HOXC cluster at 12q13) silences genes in the HOXD cluster on chromosome 2. Table 1 (examples): Airn, Kcnq1ot1 (imprinted domains), Xist (almost all genes on the inactive X, via PRC2 and H3K27me3), HOTAIR (PRC2, LSD1).
