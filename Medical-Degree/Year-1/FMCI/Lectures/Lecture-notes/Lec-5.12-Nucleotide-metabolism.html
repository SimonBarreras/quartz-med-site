1. Why nucleotides matter

  • DNA and RNA (replication, transcription); ATP as energy currency; parts of cofactors (CoA, NAD, FAD).
  • All our tissues can synthesize nucleotides.
  • [Slides] Also: GTP as energy carrier; SAM as cofactor; activators of biosynthetic intermediates (UDP-glucose, CDP-DAG); second messengers (cAMP, cGMP).
  • [Notes] There are no nucleotide stores (except ATP), so cells must make them continuously. [Slides] Nucleotide pools are kept low; their synthesis may limit rates of transcription and replication.

Clinical relevance: drug targets

Pathogens and fast-dividing cancer cells need a continuous nucleotide supply, so blocking synthesis is a treatment strategy. Problem: these drugs are not specific; they hit healthy cells too.

  • [Slides] Many parasites (e.g. malaria) lack de novo pathways and rely only on salvage, so salvage inhibitors are promising antiparasite drugs.

2. De novo purine synthesis (adenine and guanine)

Two strategies: de novo (from scratch, the main pathway) and salvage (section 5).

Ingredients

  • Activated ribose (PRPP), from ribose 5-phosphate of the pentose phosphate pathway.
  • Amino acids as N donors: glutamine, aspartate, glycine.
  • One-carbon units: CO2 and formyl-tetrahydrofolate.
  • A lot of ATP.

Origin of ring atoms (memorize the structure)

  • N1 from the amino group of aspartate.
  • C2 and C8 from two formyl-tetrahydrofolate molecules.
  • The other ring atoms: see the “Origin of Ring Atoms in Purines” figure on the slides (the figure did not come through in the text extraction, so it is not transcribed here).

Folic acid and pregnancy

  • The formyl groups come from tetrahydrofolate, made from folic acid (vitamin B9).
  • During embryogenesis (e.g. neural tube closure) cells divide very fast and need many nucleotides, so women are advised to take folic acid, ideally before pregnancy.
  • Lack of folate during neuro-embryogenesis can cause spina bifida. [Notes] Folate deficit causes anencephaly, spina bifida, premature births and megaloblastic anaemia, among others.
  • Folate-rich foods (from class): mainly dark green vegetables (spinach, broccoli, asparagus), lentils, avocado, oranges, liver, eggs.

Overview of the pathway (what you need to know)

  1. Ribose 5-phosphate is activated by pyrophosphorylation at C1 to PRPP (5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate). [Slides] Enzyme: PRPP synthetase, using ATP (giving AMP). PRPP is needed for both purines and pyrimidines.
  2. Committed step: an amino group from glutamine is added to C1 of PRPP, by glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase (the target of the glutamine-analog drugs).
  3. The ring is built on the ribose, step by step (do not learn the steps), until the first intermediate with a complete purine ring: IMP (inosine monophosphate, inosinate), whose base is hypoxanthine.
  • [check] The slides say synthesis begins with the reaction of PRPP “with Glu”; the lecturer and the study notes both give glutamine (Gln) as the donor.

  • Then kinases: AMP to ADP (kinase), ADP to ATP mainly by oxidative phosphorylation; GMP to GDP to GTP by kinases.
  • Rule: kinases use ATP as the phosphate donor, which is another reason oxidative phosphorylation is so important.

3. Regulation of purine synthesis (feedback inhibition, 3 levels)

  1. Ribose 5-phosphate to PRPP (commits ribose to nucleotides in general). Regulated by energy: more ATP, more nucleotide synthesis; low energy, less. Example: poor nutrition in fast-growth periods (infancy) impairs growth, e.g. of bone. [Notes] The reaction needs ATP, and ADP inhibits it. [Slides] PRPP synthesis is inhibited by ADP and GDP.
  2. Glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase (commits PRPP to purines): inhibited by IMP, AMP and GMP. All three high: complete inhibition (synergy). Only IMP, or only AMP or GMP, high: weaker inhibition, so the other branch can still be made.
  3. The branch point (balance between AMP and GMP, since DNA needs similar amounts of each):
    • [Notes] AMP inhibits adenylosuccinate synthetase, so IMP goes to GMP; GMP inhibits IMP dehydrogenase, so IMP goes to AMP.
    • The lecturer phrased this as “high AMP promotes GMP synthesis and high GMP promotes AMP synthesis” (same effect).
    • The cross-use of energy donors (GTP for AMP, ATP for GMP) helps keep the two balanced.

4. Catabolism of purines (gap in the recording; from slides and notes)

The recording stops as the lecturer starts this pathway. [Slides] / [Notes]:

  1. Dephosphorylation of AMP/GMP by 5’-nucleotidase.
  2. Adenosine is deaminated to inosine by adenosine deaminase (ADA); the ribose is removed, giving hypoxanthine. Guanosine gives guanine, then xanthine.
  3. Xanthine oxidase oxidizes hypoxanthine to xanthine, and xanthine to uric acid, the final product, excreted by the kidneys.
  • [Notes] Dietary nucleic acids are broken down by pancreatic nucleases and phosphodiesterases; very little of the absorbed bases is incorporated into tissue nucleic acids, since needs are met by de novo synthesis and salvage.
  • [Slides] Pyrimidine catabolism leads to NH4+ and urea.

Uric acid and gout

  • Uric acid has very low solubility; when its concentration rises it precipitates, mainly in the joints: gout (Spanish gota). It also damages the kidneys.
  • Lower uric acid by:
    • Diet: less food rich in nucleotides. Lecturer: red meat and seafood (lobster, prawns). [Slides] Seafood and liver, and avoidance of fructose. [Notes] Diet excessively rich in glandular tissue, liver and crustaceans.
    • Allopurinol: inhibits xanthine oxidase, at both steps (hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid). More hypoxanthine and xanthine accumulate instead; they are more soluble than uric acid. “Not the perfect strategy.”
  • [Slides] Painful joints (often toes) from sodium urate crystals; mainly affects males; may involve genetic under-excretion of urate and/or overconsumption of fructose.
  • [Notes] Uric acid accumulates in joints and kidney, causing inflammation and nephritis.

5. Salvage of purines

  • The lecturer’s favourite strategy: recapture free bases from degradation before they become uric acid.
  • Two advantages ([Notes]): less uric acid to excrete and less energy than de novo synthesis. Hence the need for a good balance between de novo and salvage.
  • Student question: does salvage inhibit uric acid production? No: it lowers the amount of substrate (intermediates) available to become uric acid.
  • Mechanism: the base condenses with PRPP.
    • APRT (adenine phosphoribosyltransferase): adenine + PRPP to AMP + PPi.
    • HGPRT (hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase): hypoxanthine + PRPP to IMP + PPi; guanine + PRPP to GMP + PPi.

Lesch-Nyhan syndrome (start of this section not recorded)

  • [Notes] HGPRT deficiency. A genetic disease of children with extreme symptoms: intellectual deficit, hostile behaviour, self-mutilation (fingers, lips). Lost salvage causes an extreme rise in uric acid, giving gout and strong CNS toxicity.
  • [Notes] Purine salvage is very active in the fetal and postnatal brain, erythrocytes, platelets, bone marrow and lymphocytes.
  • From the recording: neurons are disrupted and cannot work properly, hence the aggressive behaviour.

6. De novo pyrimidine synthesis

Precursors (fewer than for purines)

  • Aspartate (4 ring atoms), glutamine (N, via carbamoyl phosphate), CO2 (one carbon).
  • Different strategy: first the ring is built, then ribose is added (from PRPP). [Slides] The ring is made as orotate.

Steps (outline)

  1. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II (CPS II): glutamine amide N + CO2 + ATP to carbamoyl phosphate. Like the first reaction of the urea cycle, but a cytoplasmic isoform (CPS I is mitochondrial; Lec 5.8 Amino acid catabolism and the urea cycle). Pyrimidine synthesis is entirely cytoplasmic.
  2. Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) adds aspartate.
  3. Linear process (not required step by step) to UMP (uracil = 2,4-dioxopyrimidine).
  4. UMP to UDP to UTP by kinases; UTP is aminated to CTP (cytosine = 4-amino-2-oxopyrimidine).
  • [Notes] CPS II uses 2 ATP. In humans the first three enzymes (CPS II, ATCase, dihydroorotase) form one multi-enzyme complex, CAD. Dihydroorotate is oxidized to orotate, which binds PRPP to give orotidylate; decarboxylation gives UMP. UTP to CTP is catalyzed by cytidylate (CTP) synthetase, with glutamine as amino donor and ATP.

Regulation

  • CTP inhibits ATCase (feedback inhibition); ATP activates.
  • [Notes] ATP removes the inhibitory effect of CTP: energy is available and the cell is ready to replicate. During apoptosis, caspase 3 degrades the CAD complex, stopping pyrimidine synthesis.

7. Deoxyribonucleotides: ribonucleotide reductase

  • No need to start again: ribonucleotides are converted to deoxyribonucleotides by replacing the 2’-OH of ribose with H.
  • One enzyme, ribonucleotide reductase, converts every ribonucleoside diphosphate (ADP, CDP, GDP, UDP) to its deoxy form. The real mechanism is more complex; only this overall reaction is asked.
  • [Slides] The two H atoms come from NADPH, carried by thioredoxin or glutaredoxin (glutathione).

Regulation (very important)

  • Activity site: ATP activates; dATP inhibits. dATP is the main inhibitor (link to SCID, section 9).
  • Specificity site: the enzyme chooses which substrate to reduce, to keep balanced dNTP pools. E.g. if dGDP is low, the enzyme increases its affinity for GDP.
  • [Notes] ATP or dATP increase affinity for CDP and UDP; dTTP favours GDP; dGTP favours ADP. [Slides] The enzyme oligomerizes to change specificity. [Notes] Structure: alpha2-beta2 heterodimer; alpha subunits catalytic, beta subunits generate radicals.

8. Thymidine nucleotides: dTMP synthesis (likely exam question)

  • dTTP is needed only for DNA (thymine replaces uracil), so replicating cells need it.
  • Thymine = 5-methyl-2,4-dioxopyrimidine: thymine is uracil with a methyl on C5.
  • Path: dUMP to dTMP to dTDP to dTTP (the last two by kinases). [Slides] dUMP comes from dUTP via dUTPase (“roundabout pathway”).

The micro-cycle

  1. Thymidylate synthase transfers a methyl group from methylene-tetrahydrofolate to dUMP, giving dTMP. The folate becomes dihydrofolate (DHF).
  2. Folate is scarce in cells, so it must be regenerated:
    • Dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) reduces DHF to tetrahydrofolate (THF).
    • THF is converted back to methylene-THF. [Notes] By serine hydroxymethyltransferase.
  • So folic acid is needed for purines and for dTMP, which is why it matters so much during embryogenesis.
  • [check] The transcript calls the donor “methyl tetrahydrofolate”; the study notes and slides give methylene-tetrahydrofolate, as the lecturer himself says later.

Cancer strategy

  • Blocking only dTTP synthesis is enough: no dTTP, no DNA replication, no cell division.
  • Fluorouracil: inhibits thymidylate synthase. [Slides] It is converted by the salvage pathway into FdUMP, which is the actual inhibitor. “A poison for cancerous cells, but also for healthy cells.” Less used today.
  • Methotrexate: inhibits DHFR, blocking folate regeneration. Still widely used, also in inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (immune system over-activation). [Slides] Methotrexate and aminopterin are DHF analogs (competitive inhibitors); trimethoprim targets bacterial DHFR.
  • [Notes] These inhibitors are not selective for tumour cells, so they have major side effects.

Folic acid deficiency

  • [Slides] / [Notes] Less thymidylate synthesis raises dUTP, so uracil is incorporated into DNA. Repair enzymes remove it by cutting the strand, causing breaks and mutations; associated with cancer, heart disease and neurological impairment.

9. SCID: severe combined immunodeficiency

  • Patients cannot mount an immune response.
  • Cause: mutation in adenosine deaminase (ADA).
  • Mechanism: adenosine nucleotides accumulate, so dATP accumulates; dATP inhibits ribonucleotide reductase, so dNTPs are not made.
  • Activated lymphocytes must proliferate (clonal expansion), which needs DNA replication, so they fail: immunodeficiency.
  • First disease treated by gene therapy: a girl received the human gene carried by a virus at the end of the last century and is still alive. [verify] the lecturer’s “around 50 years old”.
  • Also called “bubble child” syndrome (there is a film with John Travolta; not exam material).
