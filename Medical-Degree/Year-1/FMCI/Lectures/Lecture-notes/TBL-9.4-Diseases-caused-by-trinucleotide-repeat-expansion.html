1. Session format

  • Individual quiz on ADI first (only about 20 students on time), then the team quiz (one member of each team logs in), then an exercise in ADI on diagnosis.
  • Anecdote: 5 November, Guy Fawkes (“gunpowder, treason and plot”): the plot to blow up Parliament; bonfires across the UK.
  • Why study these diseases: “Basically they are interesting”: a special pathogenic mechanism, the expansion of trinucleotide repeats, and another application of microsatellites (short tandem repeats, used in forensic genetics because they are polymorphic).

2. Mechanism: strand slippage and mismatch repair

  • During replication of a tandem repeat (CAG CAG CAG…), the newly synthesized strand can misalign because the repeats are similar; it slips back by one or two repeats, forming a loop that is still stable because it is complementary. The polymerase continues, and the new strand ends up with a longer tract of CAGs.
  • These loops should be repaired by mismatch repair (MMR), whose proteins (MutS alpha and MutL alpha, as transcribed) recognize mismatches or loops. MMR is not 100% effective: some loops are not repaired, and a tract of five repeats may become seven after replication.
  • [Notes] when the polymerase meets short tandem repeats, the growing and template strands can pair out of register (replication slippage); unrepaired, this gives expansions or contractions: dynamic mutations. A loop in the growing strand gives an insertion; a loop in the template strand gives a deletion (figure with CA repeats).
  • Somatic cells: cells that divide a lot are most affected (more replication). Example: a colon cancer caused by mutations in the MMR system, with many expansions in the colonic epithelium.
  • Germline: the diseases in this TBL are caused by expansions in the germline, during gametogenesis, in transmission from parent to child. Germline expansion may also explain the polymorphism of repeats in the population.
  • Parent of origin: “more cell divisions in the paternal line, so more likely to be expanded in the paternal line in general.” See section 4 and the [check] in section 7.
  • All these diseases are neurodegenerative: selective death of neurons in some area of the brain, with symptoms depending on which neurons die.

4. Anticipation

  • A clinical feature: symptoms appear at an earlier age in successive generations, usually with more severity.
  • Example (Huntington): father onset at 45, son (if he inherits the mutation) at 38, grandson at 32.
  • Molecular explanation: a longer expansion. “The father had 40, his son would have 50.” These are dynamic mutations: more repeats give a more severe and earlier presentation; during the germline the sequences expand once they reach a threshold (“13 point” in the transcript) [verify].
  • Anecdote: the lecturer was in Cambridge around 1993, when the first such gene was cloned and the mutation was found to be a trinucleotide expansion; “no one ever thought about the existence of this mechanism.”
  • Parent of origin (CAG diseases): expansion is usually in the paternal line; mothers have fewer germ cell divisions, so less risk; more divisions in the paternal line give more chances of slippage and mispairing. [Lucía] the same: expansions take place mainly in spermatogenesis.
  • [Notes] anticipation results from the tendency of the repeats to expand in each successive generation.

5. How polyglutamine causes disease

  • Not a typical mutation (gene silenced, protein absent): the protein is made, with a polyQ tract longer than it should be.
  • Toxic gain of function / dominant effect: expanded proteins aggregate with one another, and the aggregates are toxic and kill neurons; they “dominate over the normal proteins” even with normal protein present. One expanded allele is enough, so the diseases are autosomal dominant. [Notes] gain of function: deletion of the gene does not cause the same disease as the CAG expansion; hence autosomal dominant.
  • In the team quiz he stressed that CAG codes for glutamine (“You should know this”), and glutamine is Q in the one-letter code: poly-Q.
  • Video (Nature; narration): Huntington disease is an inherited degenerative disease of the central nervous system; symptoms usually begin in adulthood (movement, cognition, behaviour); no cure, treatments only reduce symptoms. The normal function of huntingtin is not fully understood but matters for nervous system development. More than 40 CAG repeats give a protein with a long glutamine chain, which “invariably causes disease”. Mutated huntingtin is cut into fragments by caspases, calpains and other proteases; the most studied fragment (transcribed “HTTX1-1” [verify] the name) can take different conformations and forms aggregates including amyloid fibrils, as in Alzheimer, Parkinson and ALS.

6. Team quiz comments

  • Mechanism of expansion: replication (strand slippage); all 10 teams right.
  • Huntington: CAG in the coding region (polyglutamine). In Huntington’s there is no loss of transcription: “There is a protein. The protein is wrong.” In Fragile X the protein is not produced because of promoter silencing.
  • PolyQ diseases are all autosomal dominant.
  • Mechanism of neurodegeneration in CAG expansions: “methylation of the gene promoter” is “nonsense” for CAG expansions (that is for Fragile X); and the expansion is in the coding region, not the 5’ UTR: “bad, bad, bad performance”.
  • Fragile X: full mutation, gene shut down at about 11 weeks of gestation. [Notes] FMR1 alleles with >200 CGG repeats undergo epigenetic silencing of the promoter at ~11 weeks of gestation.
  • RAN translation: “something probably new to you, because it goes against… all the dogma.” Not discussed further (section 10).

7. Non-CAG diseases: premutation and full mutation

  • Normal range similar to CAG diseases, but the full mutation can reach thousands of repeats.
  • Transition from normal to full mutation is preceded by a premutation: e.g. grandfather 35, son 45 (in between), grandson suddenly 800.
  • The mechanism is not clear: strand slippage cannot explain expansions of hundreds of repeats, so another mechanism is thought to be involved, possibly recombination (transcript “pre-combination”). [Notes] unequal recombination has been proposed; a full mutation can never arise directly from a normal allele; the probability of expansion to full mutation increases with the length of the repeat. [Notes] premutation ranges: 38 to 55 in DM1, 55 to 200 in Fragile X (FRAXA).
  • Anticipation in all except Friedreich’s ataxia. [Lucía] the same.
  • [check] parent of origin. Lecturer: expansion “in the paternal line in general” (section 2); in Sherman’s pedigree he describes a normal transmitting male whose allele “expanded a little bit” in his daughter (section 9). [Lucía] in non-CAG expansions the expansion is more frequent in the maternal line (unequal crossing over in oogenesis); a premutated male passes on the premutation without expansion. [Notes] does not state the parent of origin for this group.

8. Fragile X syndrome

  • The most common non-CAG disease, with myotonic dystrophy 1.
  • Name: years ago, in cultured cells under stress, one X chromosome sometimes showed a constriction that looked like it would break: a fragile site. It is the location of the trinucleotide. The cause was found in the 1990s, when the genes were cloned: very long CGG expansions.
  • Gene FMR1, CGG in the 5’ UTR; protein FMRP (fragile mental retardation protein).
  • Silencing mechanism (lecturer): as the gene is transcribed, the nascent mRNA with hundreds of repeats binds on itself and forms a hairpin; this structure recruits methylases that put silencing marks on the promoter nucleosomes: methylation of lysine 9 and lysine 27, and no trimethylation of lysine 4 or lysine 36 (activating marks). Transcription starts, the gene is epigenetically silenced, and in the end there is no transcription and no protein.
  • [check] [Notes] the nascent CGG-repeat RNA interacts with the template DNA strand, forming an RNA-DNA duplex (R-loop) stabilized by its G/C content, which elicits epigenetic silencing; >200 repeats may be needed for the RNA to reach back to the DNA. [Lucía] the mRNA interacts with its own DNA, forming an RNA-DNA loop that attracts epigenetic repressors. The lecturer described a hairpin of the RNA on itself.
  • FMRP function: binds many different mRNAs and shuttles them from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and through the neuron (to the dendrites). Without it, neuron function is severely compromised. In the team quiz a garbled remark (“still a [transcription] factor”) [verify]; he then described FMRP as an RNA-binding shuttle.
    • [Notes] FMRP is an RNA-binding protein (RGG domain) that binds mRNAs with G-quartets; involved in the biogenesis of many mRNAs critical for neuron function. [check] [Lucía] writes “TG-quartets”.
  • “Entirely different mechanism from polyglutamine expansion diseases.”
  • Inheritance: X-linked (FMR1 is on the X). Boys (XY) have only one X. Heterozygous women have a normal X making some protein, and X inactivation can change this, so fewer females are affected, though some are; “some sort of X-linked dominant inheritance, or partial dominant”. [Lucía] X-linked dominant with incomplete penetrance; one X with the expansion leads to Fragile X syndrome (mental retardation).
  • Premutation carriers: now that carriers reach their 80s and 90s, some males show neurological problems, “not exactly Fragile X syndrome, but something similar”, maybe due to a small expansion over the years. [Notes] premutation males often develop FXTAS (Fragile-X-associated tremor and ataxia syndrome); premutation females are at risk of premature ovarian failure; believed due to a toxic RNA gain of function. [Lucía] neurological symptoms at a very advanced age (e.g. tremors).

9. Sherman’s paradox

  • An idealized pedigree (not a real family; on ADI) with empirical percentages from many Fragile X families, from before the gene was known. Named after Sherman (“like the general in the American Civil War”).
  • Observations:
    • Normal parents have a normal daughter whose children can be affected; sons about twice as often as daughters, pointing to X-linked inheritance.
    • Normal daughters with affected siblings have a much higher risk of affected children; in the next generation the risk for normal carriers is even higher. Risk increases in successive generations.
    • Normal transmitting males (NTMs): a normal male can have affected grandchildren two generations later, always through a daughter, which seemed impossible for an X-linked disease.
  • Explanation by repeat number (lecturer’s figures): normal up to about 50-55; premutation; full mutation of hundreds or thousands; only the full mutation causes disease. A healthy mother with a premutation has a relatively high risk of children with a full mutation. Her healthy daughter has a longer premutation, so a higher risk (garbled: “40%, like 20%… increased like four times” [verify]; see the pedigree). A healthy woman with about 90 repeats is still premutated but much closer to the full mutation (“the abnormal range begins at 200, more or less”), so more offspring get the full mutation.
  • The NTM carried a low premutation (garbled “60, 90, 70” repeats [verify]); it expanded a little in his daughter (still premutation, healthy) and then to a full mutation in her children.
  • [Lucía] the premutated alleles expand until reaching >90 in the third generation; the probability of passing from premutation to full mutation increases with repeat size. See the [check] on parent of origin in section 7.

10. RAN translation (not discussed; from [Notes] only)

  • [Notes] Repeat-Associated Non-ATG translation: the repeat RNA structure allows abnormal translation products that do not depend on an AUG start codon. It can produce homopolymeric proteins in all three reading frames; with antisense transcription as well, RAN translation can potentially produce six different proteins. Figure 5: counting the toxic CAG and CTG transcripts and the AUG-initiated polyQ protein, “potentially nine toxic entities”. Some expanded trinucleotides form a hairpin resembling an IRES that initiates translation.

11. Myotonic dystrophy type 1

  • Repeat CTG (CUG in the mRNA), in a UTR; does not code for any amino acid.
  • [check] location. Lecturer: listed it with Fragile X “in the 5’ UTR”; at the end “in [?] prime UTR” (garbled). [Notes] 3’ UTR of DMPK (Table 1 and Figure 2B).
  • Mechanism: the expansion sequesters splicing factors; many other genes are then spliced abnormally. The expansion “does not really shut down this gene” (DMPK, dystrophia myotonica protein kinase).
  • Symptoms: muscle, eye, heart.
  • Inheritance: autosomal.
  • [Notes] DM1 is autosomal dominant; the protein product seems unaffected; (CUG)n forms stable hairpins that accumulate in RNA foci and sequester CUG-binding proteins, e.g. MBNL1 (Muscleblind-like), needed for correct splicing of muscle transcripts such as the CLCN1 chloride channel; this explains arrhythmia, cataracts and myotonia. The pathology is indirect, unrelated to the function of the mutated gene.

12. Exercise: molecular diagnosis by PCR and electrophoresis (ADI)

  • Sizing repeats is done as for microsatellites in forensics: PCR, then electrophoresis.
  • Primer placement (a student drew it wrong): primers must not sit in the CAG repeats, because “there are thousands of CAG, CAG, CAGs in the genome” and you would get thousands of PCR products. They go in the unique flanking regions, found only once in the genome, so that only this microsatellite is amplified.
  • Example: with 40 repeats the PCR product (including the primers) is 320 bp; with 43 repeats, “323 base pairs” as transcribed [verify] (3 extra trinucleotides).
  • Polyacrylamide gels are needed to separate fragments differing by three nucleotides (very small pore size). DNA moves to the positive electrode; small fragments move faster (further from the well), large ones slower. Today a capillary filled with polyacrylamide with a DNA detector gives a peak per allele (as in forensics).
  • One person has two alleles; for these autosomal dominant diseases only one needs the expansion.
