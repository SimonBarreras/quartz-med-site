1. Cytogenetics and the karyotype

  • Unit 9 is about classical genetics: transmission of genetic traits and diseases (autosomal dominant, etc.). This first lesson: cytogenetics, the analysis of genetics with the microscope, plus some molecular techniques.
  • Goals he states: what a normal karyotype is and how it is made; what abnormalities (numerical or structural) can be found; how they arise.
  • Karyotype: an ordered representation of all the chromosomes of a cell at mitosis. Cells are made to divide and stopped at a particular phase of mitosis; DNA is stained with Giemsa, which colours some parts darker: the bands.
  • Band meaning (lecturer): dark bands = usually inactive, compact chromatin; clear bands = relaxed chromatin. [verify] He names the clear ones “R bands” and the dark ones “G bands”, then says “the dark ones are called R bands”; the transcript is inconsistent.
  • [Notes] G-banding: trypsin then Giemsa, which binds preferentially AT-rich regions: dark bands = Giemsa-positive, AT-rich, gene-poor; light bands = GC-rich, gene-rich. Preparations from peripheral blood white cells stimulated to divide (phytohaemagglutinin) and arrested with colcemid; metaphase or prometaphase.
  • [check] [Lucía] writes that “darker G bands correspond to more gene dense regions” and then that darker bands are compacted and repressed; the first statement contradicts [Notes] and the lecturer.

Ordering the chromosomes (1 to 22, then the sex chromosomes)

  • Size, from largest (chromosome 1) to smallest. The smallest is actually chromosome 21; people originally thought 22 was smaller.
  • Banding pattern (distinguishes chromosomes of similar size).
  • Position of the centromere: metacentric (two arms of the same size) or submetacentric; most chromosomes have a short arm on top and a long arm below. Short arm = p (“petit”, small in French); long arm = q.
  • [Lucía] Also lists telocentric; ideogram = map of a single chromosome.

2. Acrocentric chromosomes

  • (Answering Pedro.) Acrocentric = the short arm is very tiny, “just like a blob of DNA” (satellite and ribosomal DNA).
  • Group D: 13, 14, 15; group G: 21, 22. The Y is sometimes considered acrocentric.
  • Important because they undergo Robertsonian translocations (section 7).
  • [Notes] Each short arm has about 30 to 40 large tandem repeats encoding the 28S, 18S and 5.8S rRNAs; the five rDNA regions congregate at the nucleolus, which favours Robertsonian translocation.

3. ISCN nomenclature

  • Needed to communicate findings (you cannot email “a little region somewhere in chromosome 9”): the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN). “You have in the notes how it works.”
  • [Slides] Format: total number (of centromeres), sex chromosomes (comma, no space). The total is counted by centromeres because it can be hard to tell how many chromosomes there are. Normal female 46,XX; normal male 46,XY.

Bands and sub-bands

  • Each arm is divided into regions defined by the banding pattern, numbered individually: q21 is read “q two-one”, not “q twenty-one” (saying “twenty-one” is a mistake): long arm, 2, then 1. The lecturer calls these band 2, sub-band 1.
  • [Notes] Box 1: arm, region, band, sub-band, sub-sub-band, numbered by proximity to the centromere (region 1 closest). 4q21 = long arm of 4, region 2, band 1; 4q21.22 = “two-one-point-two-two”. Proximal = closer to the centromere, distal = closer to the telomere. [check] terminology: the lecturer’s “band and sub-band” for q21 corresponds to [Notes] “region and band”.
  • Resolution: depends on when mitosis is stopped. Earlier = less compacted chromosomes = more bands = more resolution; with about 500 bands per karyotype you can define sub-bands (e.g. q21.1). Very high resolution is difficult and rare. Figure: the same chromosome at three resolutions.
  • [Notes] Figure 1b: about 400, 550 and 850 bands per haploid set.
  • Ideograms: the drawings in books and papers; not the real thing but the ideal banding pattern of each chromosome.

4. Classifying abnormalities

  • Two types: numerical and structural.
  • [Notes] Also by distribution: constitutional (all nucleated cells, present from very early development: abnormal gamete, fertilization or early embryo) vs somatic/acquired (only some cells: a mosaic). Structural abnormalities come from chromosome breaks that are not repaired; numerical ones often from segregation errors.
  • [Notes] Chromosomal aberrations are the main cause of embryo loss in the first three months; up to 20 to 30% of spontaneous abortions until week 20 show one, around 5% later.

5. Numerical abnormalities (recorded part)

  • Aneuploidy: “euploidy” = the right number; an- (Greek negative) = not the right number of chromosomes.
  • Polyploidy: whole sets, e.g. triploidy (3n), tetraploidy (4n). More common is one chromosome extra or missing.
  • Trisomy and monosomy are the most common aneuploidies:
ConditionKaryotype
Down syndromeTrisomy 21
Edwards syndromeTrisomy 18
Patau syndromeTrisomy 13
Turner syndromeMonosomy X (women with one X)
Klinefelter syndromeMales with two X instead of one (XXY)
  • Trisomy 21 is the most common because 21 is the smallest chromosome, so an extra copy “is not so bad”; you could not have a trisomy of chromosome 1 (the largest: excess of genes). Trisomies 18 and 13 are less common.

  • [Notes] Nondisjunction: homologues fail to separate at meiosis I, or sister chromatids at meiosis II or mitosis; meiotic nondisjunction gives gametes with 22 or 24 chromosomes (nullisomy or disomy), hence monosomic or trisomic zygotes; mitotic nondisjunction gives mosaics. Tetraploidy is rarer, always lethal, from failure of the first zygotic division (endomitosis).

Cancer cells often show extreme aneuploidy.
  • [Notes] Mixoploidy: mosaicism (cell lines from one zygote) or chimerism (from different zygotes).

7. Structural abnormalities (second recording)

Types listed (start lost): … and translocations, reciprocal or Robertsonian.

Isochromosomes

  • Two identical arms: one arm lost, the other duplicated. [verify] The transcript then says “the most frequent one is the same”; probably i(Xq), as in [Notes].
  • [Notes] Rare except i(Xq) and i(21q) (an occasional cause of Down syndrome).

Deletions

  • Terminal: a break and everything beyond it is lost.
  • Interstitial: the lost part is inside an arm (e.g. one band).
  • The microdeletions of the genomic disorders (e.g. DiGeorge) belong here; often not visible on the karyotype, needing more sophisticated techniques (Lec 7.7 Genetic variation).
  • Example: cri du chat (French, “cry of the cat”): terminal deletion of the end of the short arm of chromosome 5 (del(5p)); the pitch of the babies’ cry resembles a cat’s. It takes a trained cytogeneticist, who has seen thousands of karyotypes, to see it.
  • [Slides] TERMINAL DELETION del(7)(p22;pter).

Duplications

  • One segment duplicated: the same banding pattern appears twice.
  • Example: dup(7)(q21;q23) ([Slides]): duplication of chromosome 7 in brackets, then in brackets the regions involved separated by a semicolon. (Transcript reads “from Q1 to Q3”.)
  • Aside: as doctors you may ask an AI to explain such lab reports, “but it’s nice to know whether AI got it wrong, which usually gets it wrong.”

Inversions

  • Need two breaks; the segment between them is inverted.
  • Both breaks on the same arm (same side of the centromere): paracentric.
  • One break in each arm (either side of the centromere): pericentric. Pericentric ones are usually larger than paracentric.
  • If the segment is lost instead of inverted: interstitial deletion.

Ring chromosomes

  • Both terminal segments lost and the broken ends of the long and short arms fused: a ring with one centromere.
  • [Notes] Figure 4: ring chromosomes with a centromere are stable in mitosis; the acentric distal fragments are lost.

Reciprocal translocations

  • A piece of one chromosome passes to another and vice versa: a break in each chromosome.
  • Stable when the exchanged pieces are the fragments without centromere, so each product keeps one centromere.
  • Naming: each product contains parts of both chromosomes; it is named by the centromere it carries. E.g. chromosomes 2 and 13: the product with the centromere of 2 is der(2) (derivative 2), “not a chromosome 2”; the other is der(13).
  • If the exchange gives one chromosome with two centromeres (dicentric) and one with none (acentric), these are unstable and lost in mitosis.
  • [Notes] Translocation needs the two breaks in close proximity; chromosome territories matter (e.g. 4, 13 and 18 at the nuclear periphery often translocate with each other). Specific translocations are common in certain cancers.

Robertsonian translocations (centric fusion)

  • Only the acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22). They fuse by their centromeres (centric fusion).
  • The short arms are only satellite and ribosomal DNA, so losing them is no problem.
  • Products: a dicentric chromosome with both centromeres together, stable (works as a single centromere); and an acentric fragment (short arms, rDNA) lost in mitosis.
  • Meiosis problem: e.g. a 14;15 fusion pairs with the normal 14 and the normal 15, forming a trivalent; segregation can give offspring with trisomies or monosomies (“complex… I don’t have time to explain that”).
  • [Notes] Figure 5B; Table 1: 45,XY,der(14;21)(q10;q10) balanced carrier; a carrier’s child may have Down syndrome with 46 chromosomes. [Lucía] A carrier may pass missing or extra DNA, giving monosomies or trisomies.

How structural abnormalities arise (student question)

  • Not mutations in the usual sense: they happen spontaneously and are very rare.
  • In meiosis, homologous recombination begins with double-strand breaks; normally they are processed correctly, but rarely two nearby DSBs are joined wrongly, fusing two chromosomes. “No biological process is 100% efficient.”
  • [Notes] Breaks come from unrepaired DNA damage or faults in recombination (mispaired homologues, unequal crossing-over, intrachromatid recombination). Breaks in G2 are chromatid breaks; breaks in G1 not repaired by S become chromosome breaks.

8. Molecular cytogenetics: FISH

  • Banding resolution is limited; small deletions cannot be seen. Molecular techniques give much better resolution. “The one you have to know about”: FISH, fluorescence in situ hybridization.
  • Hybridization: denature DNA (separate the strands) by heat (as in PCR), pH change or chemicals; then let it renature. With a probe (a short DNA fragment whose genomic origin is known; Spanish “sonda”) present, cooling slowly lets the probe strands anneal to their matching site in the genome.
  • Probes are fluorescently labelled. Fluorescence: the molecule absorbs light of one wavelength and emits at another (he contrasts it with phosphorescence).
  • On the slide: the cell’s DNA is denatured on the slide, the probe anneals to its identical region, and a coloured signal appears under a fluorescence microscope. DNA counterstained blue with DAPI.
  • On metaphase chromosomes each signal is a double dot (two sister chromatids).
  • Example, chromosome 15: two probes, one for the centromere of 15 (shows both chromosomes 15 are present) and one for a region on 15q. One chromosome 15 lacks the 15q signal: an interstitial deletion of 15q.
  • [Notes] FISH confirms duplications/deletions found by other methods; detects oncogene amplification (MYCN in neuroblastoma) and translocations. Figure 2: 22q11.2 deletion detected with HIRA probe (red) and ARSA control probe (green).
  • [Lucía] If the probe does not fluoresce, it has not bound: a microdeletion in that region.

Chronic myeloid leukaemia: t(9;22) (exam case years ago)

  • CML (a blood cancer): the cells carry a reciprocal translocation between chromosome 9 and chromosome 22: a der(9) with part of 22 and a der(22) with part of 9.
  • The der(22) is the Philadelphia chromosome (Ph).
  • Notation t(9;22)(q34;q11) ([Slides]): the first breakpoint (q34) refers to the first chromosome (9), the second (q11) to the second (22). (Transcript reads “Q811” and “Q3134”; [Slides] has q34;q11.)
  • The Philadelphia chromosome carries a fusion gene of one gene from 22 and one from 9. A tyrosine kinase normally not expressed in blood stem cells is, because of the fusion, expressed at high levels in developing white blood cells, driving their proliferation: leukaemia.
  • [Notes] The genes are BCR (22q) and ABL1 (9q); the BCR-ABL1 hybrid gene.

Interphase FISH

  • Great advantage: no metaphase chromosomes needed; cells (nuclei) are put on a slide directly.
  • Detects aneuploidies (trisomies, monosomies) and translocations.
  • t(9;22): probe for 9 in green, for 22 in red; where they are fused, the signals sit together and can look yellow where they overlap (transcript “green and blue” [verify]; [Notes] Figure 3: red and green, overlap orange-yellow).
  • Trisomy 8 (another leukaemia): a centromere probe for chromosome 8; count dots per nucleus across hundreds of cells to see how many carry the trisomy.
  • “Very powerful”: molecular cytogenetics complements classical cytogenetics.
