1. Single gene (Mendelian) diseases vs Lec 9.3

Lec 9.2 single geneLec 9.3 polygenic
CauseOne mutation in one gene causes the diseaseMany genes plus environment
FrequencyRare in the populationVery common
SeverityUsually quite serious
FamiliesRun in families, following set patterns of inheritanceGenetic component not a single gene
His examplesHemophilia AType 1 diabetes (“you wouldn’t say it’s a genetic disease. Well, in a sense it is, but not only”)
  • The basic tool of medical genetics is pedigree analysis: take the family history, build the pedigree, apply Mendel’s rules (which he does not re-explain: “you’re probably filled up with… Mendel’s experiments… from your high school”). From the pattern alone you should have an idea of what is happening.
  • Mitochondrial DNA mutations follow a different pattern (section 8).
  • [Notes] A more accurate term than “Mendelian” is monogenic (covers chromosomal loci and mtDNA loci). Five basic Mendelian patterns: autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked dominant, X-linked recessive, Y-linked; plus mtDNA inheritance.
  • [Notes] Terms: locus; allele; genotype; homozygous/heterozygous; men are hemizygous for most X and Y loci. Dominant = manifested in the heterozygote; recessive = not; co-dominant = both phenotypes shown by the heterozygote (AB blood group).

2. Pedigrees

  • Taking the family history: e.g. “your son has this problem with the lungs… when did this happen… is there anyone else in your family with this?” Then you construct the pedigree.
  • Name: from French, “the foot of the crane”, because of the shape. (Transcript: “La pied du coul”; [verify] the French phrase.)
  • Drawing it quickly while talking to the family gives an overview instead of writing “one son, three daughters…“.

  • Before any molecular analysis you do not know who carries the mutation; you only see who shows the disease.
  • Consanguinity: genetic relatedness greater than the population average, e.g. spouses whose grandparents were siblings. “Used to be very common in rural areas” where the same families intermarried for generations. “Very important to account for.”
  • [Notes] Figure 1 adds: deceased, spontaneous abortion, termination of affected pregnancy, obligate carrier, carrier who may go on to manifest disease. Proband also called propositus/proposita. Relatives of first degree (parent-child, sibs), second degree (grandparent, uncle/aunt, half-sibs), third degree (first cousins).
  • [Notes] Box 1: proportion of genes in common = (1/2)^n per path, summed over paths: parent-child 1/2, sibs 1/2, half-sibs 1/4, uncle/aunt-nephew/niece 1/4, first cousins 1/8, second cousins 1/32. Coefficient of inbreeding = half the parents’ coefficient of relationship (first cousins: 1/16). Not covered in the lecture.

3. Autosomal dominant (AD)

Three questions to ask of the pedigree:

  1. Are all generations affected? Yes.
  2. Is the ratio of affected children about 50% in each mating? Roughly (his example matings: 2 of 3, 1 of 3 [transcript garbled], 2 of 4). Short generations make percentages “not very meaningful”.
  3. Are both sexes affected? Yes.
  • Dominance: one mutated allele of the two is enough to show the disease. Recessiveness: both alleles must be mutated.
  • A healthy parent must have two normal alleles; the affected parent is probably heterozygous and passes either chromosome to each child: about 50%.
  • “Autosomal”: the mutated gene is on chromosomes 1 to 22, so sex does not matter.
  • Punnett square (Reginald Punnett, early 20th century): normal mother (AA) x affected heterozygous father (Aa, lower case = mutated): 2 of 4 normal homozygotes, 2 of 4 heterozygotes with the disease = 50% risk for each child. [Notes] Figure 2: same 1 in 2.
  • [Notes] Because AD disorders are rare, affected people are almost always heterozygotes.

Example: familial hypercholesterolemia (AD)

  • Very high LDL cholesterol in blood (“hundreds, thousands”) due to mutations in the LDL receptor in the liver. [Notes] Gene LDLR.
  • Lipid deposits in soft tissues (transcript “xanthelathic”; [Notes] tendon xanthomas and xanthelasmata of the eyelids). Same in the coronary arteries: coronary disease at 20 to 30 years of age.
  • Frequency 1 in 200 to 250 people (lecturer and [Notes] agree). Late enough onset that people reproduce before diagnosis, so the mutation is frequent and homozygotes occur.
  • Homozygotes: for most AD diseases two mutated alleles are lethal (the embryo dies); in FH they are born but have heart disease very young (“heart attack at 18-20 years of age”). [Notes] Homozygous form more severe, usually appears in childhood.
  • [Lucía] Also gives a mutation in APOB-100 (the LDL ligand for the receptor) as a cause. Not said in the lecture.

4. Autosomal recessive (AR)

Questions for the pedigree:

  1. All generations affected? No.
  2. 50%? (garbled in the transcript; [Notes]: 25% per child of two carriers.)
  3. Both sexes? Yes.
  4. Consanguineous mating? Yes, in his example (first cousins; frequency varies with culture).
  • Many empty symbols are carriers with one mutated allele; disease only with both alleles mutated.
  • Parents of an affected child must both be healthy carriers (one mutated allele each). Without molecular analysis you do not know who else carries it.
  • Punnett square, Aa x Aa: 1/4 healthy non-carrier, 1/2 healthy carriers, 1/4 affected (homozygous mutated).
  • Counselling wording: risk the next child is affected = 1/4; healthy (no disease) = 3/4 (75%); of the healthy, 1/3 will not transmit the mutation and 2/3 will. Affected children always transmit it.

Consanguinity and compound heterozygotes

True homozygoteCompound heterozygote
Typical settingConsanguineous parentsUnrelated parents
MutationsThe same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, on both allelesTwo different mutations in the same gene (e.g. CFTR, one in one exon from the father, one in another exon from the mother)
FunctionBoth copies inactivatedBoth copies inactivated; “in real terms it doesn’t matter”
  • Consanguinity raises the odds of two copies of a mutation “lying around” in a family meeting in one child.
  • [Notes] Figure 3 (A) compound heterozygotes, (B) true homozygotes via first cousins; risk stays 1 in 4 in both. For rare disorders, parental consanguinity strongly indicates AR.

Example: cystic fibrosis (AR)

  • One of the most common genetic diseases in European populations or people of European descent; the mutation appeared some centuries ago in Northern Europe.
  • The gene (CFTR) codes for a chloride channel. In epithelial cells that make mucus (bronchi, glands, pancreatic duct, liver), the salt (sodium chloride) concentration is wrong, the mucus becomes thick and blocks the lumen: in bronchi, pneumonia; in pancreas, pancreatitis. Blocked bronchioles and alveoli get infections and fibrosis, hence the name.
  • Carriers: about 1 in 20 people; “probably two of you in this room are carriers”.
  • [Slides] Organs affected: airways, lungs, pancreas, sweat glands. Distribution of CFTR mutations across the exons, with ΔF508 (transcript of slide “AF508”) the most frequent: see the slide.
  • [Notes] Frequency 1 in 2,500 to 3,500 newborns in Caucasian populations; meconium ileus, CF-related diabetes, congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens in most men.
  • [Notes] Carriers may show disease-related traits: sickle-cell trait (mild anaemia, sickling under hypoxia or exhaustion); sickle-cell disease is recessive but the trait is dominant. [Lucía] same. Not in the lecture.

5. X-linked recessive (XR)

  • Women have two X chromosomes, men one. A man passes his X to daughters and his Y to sons.
  • Pedigree: like AR (few people affected, not all generations), but only (or mostly) males are affected: this “should alert you” that it is X-linked.
  • Female carriers shown with a dot.
ParentsDaughtersSons
Carrier mother x normal father1/2 normal, 1/2 healthy carriers; none affected1/2 unaffected, 1/2 affected
Affected father x normal motherAll healthy carriersAll healthy (father passes his Y)
Affected father x carrier mother (“worst case”)1/2 affected1/2 affected
  • The third case is how women can rarely be affected (his example: a carrier who only knew of an uncle with hemophilia marries a man with hemophilia). [check] He also says “one fourth, 25% of the offspring” for this cross, then “50% of the daughters… and 50% of the sons” (which is 1/2 overall). [Notes] Figure 5B: “a 1 in 2 chance that a child would be affected, irrespective of whether it was a boy or girl”; the apparent male-to-male transmission is an illusion (the son’s mutant X came from his mother).
  • Examples: hemophilia A, colour blindness (“many things”).
  • “Work out the Punnett squares of the different situations is great fun.”

Example: Duchenne muscular dystrophy (XR)

  • “One of the most frequent” XR diseases. Named after the doctor who described it.
  • Muscle weakness and wasting from a young age; he gives about 10 years, wheelchair by 20, death by about 30 from respiratory failure. [verify] these ages in a textbook (no vault source gives them).
  • Mutations in dystrophin, a protein at the sarcolemma needed for communication between the muscle fibre and the extracellular matrix. Images: dystrophin staining at the sarcolemma; normal skeletal muscle cross-section vs dystrophic muscle with inflammation and fibres of different sizes (see the slide).
  • [Notes] About 1 in 5,000 newborn males; DMD gene; dystrophin in skeletal and cardiac muscle; female carriers have increased risk of cardiomyopathy. [Lucía] Death from respiratory failure (weak diaphragm) or heart failure (cardiomyopathy).

6. X-linked dominant (XD)

  • “Very, very rare”, few diseases.
  • Resembles AD: all generations, about 50%, both sexes.
  • Telltale sign: never father-to-son transmission. An affected male passes the mutation only to daughters (all of them), never to sons. “If you see that in a pedigree, you should think there’s something fishy going on… Careful.”
  • [Notes] More affected females than males; affected females milder and more variable (X-inactivation); in some XD disorders males die prenatally (incontinentia pigmenti, Figure 6B). [Lucía] example: X-linked hypophosphatemia (X-linked rickets). Neither example was given in the lecture.

7. X-chromosome inactivation

  • One of the two X chromosomes in females becomes inactive: seen cytologically as the Barr body, very densely packaged chromatin of the inactive X at the nuclear periphery. Named after “Murray Barr, Scottish doctor” ([verify] his nationality). [Slides] X-chromosome inactivation picture: see the slide.
  • Random: either X can be inactivated; happens early in the embryo. [check] Timing: lecturer “by the blastocyst stage, more or less, chromosome inactivation is complete”; [Notes] and [Lucía] the decision begins “at around the eight-cell stage” in the preimplantation embryo.
  • About 50% of a woman’s cells inactivate each X. With a mutation, if inactivation is skewed against the healthy X (e.g. 80% of cells keep the mutated X active), a heterozygous woman can show symptoms of the disease (“for some reasons that are rare”).
  • [Notes] These are manifesting heterozygotes; female carriers are mosaics; skewing can also protect carriers. Some genes (pseudoautosomal regions) escape inactivation. [Lucía] the inactive X is only about 60% inactivated; XIST is expressed from the X that will be inactivated.

8. Mitochondrial inheritance

  • Mitochondria have many copies of a short circular mtDNA; each cell has many mitochondria, each with many copies.
  • mtDNA codes some of the proteins of the OxPhos cascade (transcript: “some of the oxidase, and MAPH, blah, blah”, [verify] which ones he named); the rest are encoded in the nucleus. Mutations in mtDNA cause mitochondrial diseases.
  • All the zygote’s mitochondria come from the egg. [check] Lecturer: the sperm cell “has actually got rid of the mitochondria… there is a lot of debate about this”; [Notes]: the sperm does contribute mtDNA but paternal mtDNA is destroyed in the very early embryo after ubiquitin tagging; [Lucía]: males don’t contribute mitochondria at fertilization. All agree: no paternal mtDNA in the child.
  • Pattern: matrilineal. [Slides] “Males NEVER pass the disease. Females can pass the disease to sons and daughters. Some women are unaffected carriers.”
  • Unaffected carrier women: the number of mtDNA molecules with the mutation is very low, and not all mitochondria passed to the embryo carry it. (A garbled sentence before this, “Or not, but it has to be via a male, as it’s called”: [verify].)
  • [Notes] Heteroplasmy (mixed normal and mutant mtDNA) vs homoplasmy; variable heteroplasmy and the mitochondrial bottleneck explain variability within families; incomplete penetrance is common; high-energy tissues (muscle, brain) mainly affected; mtDNA 16.5 kb, 37 genes.
  • Student question: is all mtDNA from the mother? “Only from the mother… none from your father.” Hence the mitochondrial Eve of human evolution studies: using mtDNA, all humans living on Earth come from (coalesce into) a “mitochondrial Eve”; so mtDNA is useful for evolutionary studies too.
