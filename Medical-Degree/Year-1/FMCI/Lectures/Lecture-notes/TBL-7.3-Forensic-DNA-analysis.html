1. History: DNA fingerprints

  • A scientist (name not in the recorded part) realized that patterns of tandem repeats reflect family relationships between people and could be used to identify individuals.
  • He first used the technique to help the police in the UK solve a crime. [verify] Transcript: “a case of rape or a crime. The first case was of their crime, a murder”; the type of the first case is unclear. He then helped solve other cases, and the technology became easier.
  • He called the patterns “fingerprints”: each person has a unique fingerprint of minisatellite repeat numbers. There are hundreds or thousands of minisatellites in the genome; for each, a person has a certain number of repeats, giving a band of a different size.
  • The method moved from minisatellites to microsatellites: also tandem repeats, but of very short units, 1 to 4 nucleotides.

2. Why microsatellites (STRs) and not minisatellites

  • Asked in class and as quiz question 1 (also: why not SNPs?). Students: “more specific”.
  • Lecturer: “one of the reasons is technical, basically”. Minisatellite fingerprinting is a complex technology; for microsatellites it is just a PCR. PCR is fast; the products are run in capillary electrophoresis and the different sizes appear as peaks.
  • Quiz answers (word cloud): PCR, faster, specific.
  • Calculating random-match probabilities cannot be done for minisatellites but can for microsatellites, the technology used today.
  • [Lucía] STRs are in non-coding regions and highly polymorphic (nine or more detectable alleles); different people carry different allele combinations. The more microsatellites analysed, the better.

3. Paternity case: exclusion vs inclusion

  • Figure (minisatellite fingerprints; see the slides): mother, child, two potential fathers (F1, F2).
  • Each band in the child must be maternal or paternal, because the child inherited one allele from each parent.
  • Bands not from the mother all match F1; F2 cannot have contributed them, so F2 is excluded.
  • Can we say with 100% certainty that F1 is the father? No.
    • Technical reasons (partial profile, contamination) were set aside: “just biological reasons”.
    • A student (Pedro) suggested we only look at part of the genome. Answer: the markers are scattered throughout the genome, so it is a fairly broad view.
    • The real reason: we do not know whether someone else in the world has exactly the same pattern (“you go to Australia… that Australian man could have been the father”).
  • So you give a probability, based on how likely the pattern is to be found again in human populations: the random-match probability. If it is, say, 1 in 3,000 million and you know the whole population, you may conclude no one else could have it; still, it is a very high probability, not a certainty.
  • Exclusion is certain; inclusion is always a probability.

4. The prosecutor’s fallacy

  • [Slides] “Prosecutor’s fallacy: Is #1 guilty? Is #1 innocent? Are #2 and #3 guilty?”
  • Case: a rape. A specimen found on the victim (minisatellites, old technology) and three suspects rounded up by the police. Suspect 1 matches.
  • The tempting conclusion: suspect 1 must be the offender. That is the prosecutor’s fallacy (a fallacy = a flawed argument).
  • Suppose the same profile occurs by chance in 1 in 10 males in that city. Then there may be 1,000 potential offenders in the city, so the probability that suspect 1 is the real one is 1 in 1,000: very low.
  • Suspects 2 and 3 can be excluded: their DNA is not in the sample.
  • To attribute the sample to one person you must calculate the probability of finding the same pattern by chance. Only if that probability is lower than anything plausible (he jokes: lower than the number of people even if there were life on other planets) can you say it must be him, and even then you give a probability such as 99.999%.
  • The pre-reading case (“How DNA was found at killing scene”) shows these limitations: people can be wrongly convicted.

5. Calculating probabilities (individual quiz)

Hardy-Weinberg underlies the calculation (“I don’t have time to explain that”; see Lec 7.7 Genetic variation).

Question 2: heterozygote

  • One STR marker with 5 alleles in the population: A 20%, B 20%, C 10%, D 30%, E 20% (sum 100%). The transcript says “30% have allele B”; with B already at 20%, the 30% must be D.
  • Probability that a random person is C/D: multiply the two frequencies and multiply by 2 “because it’s the heterozygote”: 2 x 0.1 x 0.3 = 0.06.
  • [verify] The transcript reads the answer as “0, 0, 0, 6” and D as “0, 0, 3”; 0.06 is what the stated frequencies give.

Question 3: homozygote

  • Probability of a random B/B homozygote: frequency squared, 0.2 x 0.2 = 0.04 (confirmed as the right answer). For homozygotes you do not multiply by 2.

Question 4: random match in a city

  • The evidence sample from a crime scene is usually a mixture: DNA of the victim, the culprit, and people who simply passed by.
  • A suspect matches a profile of many markers; the random-match probability is 1 in a million (10^-6); the offender is known to come from a city, so the potential offenders are the people of that city. Which statement is more correct?
  • [verify] The options and the right answer are not audible. Fragments: “the 10 million is the highest”, “in every million”, “50%“. Apply the reasoning of section 4: expected number of chance matches = city population x random-match probability.

6. The STR method (recap)

  • Example microsatellite with a trinucleotide repeat: 3 repeats = 9 nucleotides.
  • PCR primers are placed on both sides of the repeat; their sequences are the same in everyone, so they bind everyone’s DNA.
  • Product of e.g. 199 bp with 3 repeats; someone with 5 repeats gives 205 bp (6 nt longer). Alleles are named by the number of repeats (allele 3, allele 5).
  • Many allele sizes exist in the population, but each person has only two alleles (one from each parent), the same or different.
  • Capillary electrophoresis: a thin tube filled with acrylamide, a polymer with many tiny pores. An electric current moves the DNA towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments migrate faster. The products are fluorescently labelled; a detector window records light over time: baseline, a peak when a fragment passes, baseline again.
  • Two peaks (alleles 3 and 5) = heterozygote 3/5. One peak (somewhat higher) = homozygote.
  • Timing: PCR in about 1 hour, electrophoresis about half an hour.
  • [Lucía] One primer of each pair is fluorescently labelled; out of 14 STRs analysed, 3 or 4 are labelled with each of four fluorescent dyes; a laser reads all four colours and displays the PCR output by size and colour. She describes a gel; the lecturer describes capillary electrophoresis.

7. Reading a real profile and the product rule

  • Real result (see the slide): 17 markers; for each, the person’s genotype.
    • e.g. a marker on chromosome 10 heterozygous 12/15.
    • Amelogenin: sex marker with alleles X and Y (this person showed both).
    • A single peak, e.g. homozygous 28 at D21S11.
  • In the marker names, the number after D is the chromosome: markers on chromosome 10, 16, 2… so they are scattered across all chromosomes (the answer to Pedro’s concern in section 3).
  • You need a table of allele frequencies for the relevant population: e.g. US tables by ethnicity (Caucasian of European descent, African-American); for a crime in Spain, Spanish frequencies. The example table was for a marker on chromosome 8.
  • For each marker: homozygote p², heterozygote 2pq.
  • Product rule: multiply the probabilities of all individual markers to get the final probability of a random match (someone who is at the same time 12/14 at one marker, 28/30 at another, 9/9, 10/10…). Example result: 1 in 26 billion (US population). [verify] the figure.
  • Then the probability that the suspect is the source depends on the number of people in the population. This is what the judge or jury must understand.
  • [Lucía] The FBI analyses 13 STRs plus a sex marker, reducing random matches. A complete profile comes with a random-match probability calculated from allele frequencies in the population.
