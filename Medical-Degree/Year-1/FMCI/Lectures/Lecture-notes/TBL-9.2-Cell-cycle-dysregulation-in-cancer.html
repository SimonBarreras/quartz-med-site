1. Cancer: a complex, multistep disease

  • [Slides] “A complex and multistep disease: genetic and environmental factors”; “The hallmarks of cancer” (scheme: see the slide).
  • Not only proliferation of cells and inhibition of cell death: also angiogenesis, the effect of the immune system, external radiation; many factors. A step-by-step process leading to metastasis and eventually death.
  • Genetic basis: oncogenic transformation of proto-oncogenes and inactivation of tumour suppressor genes, due to many factors:
FactorLecturer’s example or point
Genetic predispositionCarrying a mutation that promotes tumorigenesis
CarcinogensExposure
Spontaneous mutationsGarbled in the transcript; [Slides] “Spontaneous”
Viruses and other pathogensHelicobacter pylori, which causes stomach cancer. [Notes] it causes chronic gastritis; inflammatory cells produce free radicals that can damage DNA
  • These lead to hyperproliferation and DNA damage, forming the primary tumour. If cells escape the primary tumour they enter the bloodstream and attach to organs: metastasis. Typical sites for breast cancer: the lymph nodes first, the brain, the liver, the bone. The new tumour masses are “what is eventually killing the patient”.

2. Tobacco smoke and lung cancer

  • Compounds: lecturer “more than 4,000 chemical compounds”, about 100 able to induce malignant transformation. [check] [Slides] “4500 chemical compounds, 100 carcinogenic”; the slides give 4,500.
  • [Notes] Carcinogenic components include tars, polycyclic hydrocarbons and nitrosamines.
  • Malignant transformation here = inactivation of tumour suppressor genes and oncogenic transformation of proto-oncogenes.
  • Lung cancer from tobacco is the result of two main factors ([Slides] “Effects of tobacco smoke”):
FactorLecturer’s explanation
DNA damageDaily exposure over years. Some compounds bind DNA covalently and induce mutations. Over many years a cell may acquire a mutation, e.g. KRAS (“very typical” in many tumours, including lung cancer), EGFR mutations (transcribed “EJPAR”), or loss of p53 (transcribed “loss of 50”) [verify]. [Slides] activation of oncogenes, inactivation of TSG
Chronic inflammationHeat burns the alveolar epithelium; tar (combustion products) gets stuck in the alveoli; macrophages come to remove it but cannot digest it all because the damage is daily; they die, more inflammation, more neutrophils and lymphocytes. “Any chronic inflammation is going to be due to cancer promotion” (wording garbled) [verify]
  • Why inflammation promotes cancer: inflammatory cells (macrophages, lymphocytes) produce cytokines and growth factors, meant to recruit inflammatory cells and make them proliferate. Cancer cells “learn” to express the receptors for these cytokines on their membrane and respond to the proliferative effect. “They learn how to survive in these adverse conditions.” [Lucía] the same; also ROS produced by macrophages and leukocytes damage our own cells. [Notes] chronic inflammation produces large amounts of reactive oxygen species that damage DNA, and cytokines and growth factors that stimulate tumour cell proliferation.
  • Histology ([Slides] healthy lung, lung from a heavy smoker, lung cancer; see the slides):
    • Normal: alveoli, pneumocytes in the septa.
    • Heavy smoker: thickened alveolar septa (“like a scar”, the tissue protecting itself) and dark macrophages digesting tar. Not cancer.
    • Lung cancer: many cells in mitosis, invading the lung.
  • Late detection: once there are symptoms (coughing blood, pain), the tumour has usually spread; most lung cancers are detected late, and cure is then very difficult. Some programmes offer heavy and past heavy smokers an annual CT scan to detect growing nodules.
  • [Notes] Tobacco relates to cancers by direct contact (pharynx, larynx, stomach, lungs) or indirect contact (pancreas, bladder); alcohol and tobacco act synergistically for lung and head and neck tumours. [Lucía] “Its common effects are lung and bladder cancer.”

3. Oncogenic viruses: papillomavirus (examinable slide)

  • The lecturer: “This was included also in this type of material” (the study material).
  • Papillomavirus: sexually transmitted, infects and colonizes the epithelium of the cervix; stays inside the nucleus and divides when the cells divide.
  • Two harmful viral proteins:
Viral proteinTargetConsequence
E6Binds and inactivates p53Loss of p53’s protective function: more mutations ([Slides] “Cells unprotected from DNA damage”)
E7Binds and inactivates RbE2F active all the time, inducing proliferation ([Slides] “E2F: Excessive proliferation”)
  • Not a mutation, not a problem in the gene or promoter sequence: inactivation of the protein.
  • Excessive proliferation plus loss of p53 = “the perfect combination for cancer development”.
  • p53 described by the lecturer as “the caretaker of the genome”. [check] [Notes] calls p53 “the gatekeeper of the genome”. The slides do not decide.
  • Infection does not mean cancer; it increases the risk. Some infected women never develop cancer; other factors contribute. “This is why the vaccine is very important.”
  • [Notes] HPV can cause cervical, penile, anal and head and neck cancer. Hepatitis C virus is the second example in the study material (chronic hepatocyte infection, hepatocarcinoma); not discussed in class.

4. Accumulation of mutations: familial adenomatous polyposis

  • Aggressive tumours usually accumulate many mutations; cancer is not a single mutation in one gene, though it usually starts with one.
  • Studied in familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP): an inherited condition; [Slides] germline mutations in the APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) gene.
  • Polyps (benign) form in the colon; as cells accumulate more mutations, an aggressive tumour penetrates the connective tissue, reaches the bloodstream and metastasizes.
  • Because carriers are known, researchers sequenced polyps at each stage. Order as transcribed: loss of APC, then p53, then KRAS activation through activating mutations, then loss of other tumour suppressor genes (see the slide for the exact sequence) [verify].
  • Images: normal colon mucosa with its infoldings versus a colon full of polyps. Polyps increase progressively; at some point removing the colon “as a whole” may be needed because of the risk of malignancy.
  • Message: as cancer progresses, different mutations accumulate.

5. Genomic instability

  • “Progression implies higher genomic instability”: more mutations, chromosome alterations, alterations of all kinds of genes.
  • [Slides] karyotype of a breast cancer cell from a metastatic tumour (see the slide): e.g. five copies of chromosome 1, unclassifiable chromosomes, multiple extra chromosomes. [Notes] (Genetics class notes, Lec 9.1) cancer cells often show extreme aneuploidy, with multiple chromosomal abnormalities.
  • Many such cells are not viable and die: this is why tumours contain many apoptotic cells. Others survive by unknown means, and these are usually the more aggressive ones, found in advanced, highly metastatic tumours; they resist chemotherapy and radiotherapy, “maybe because this is kind of a natural selection process”.
  • Paradox: many genetic alterations, yet survival in all kinds of adverse conditions. “This is very difficult to treat; this is the key message.”

6. BRCA1 and BRCA2 (not examined)

  • Not for the exam; needed for clinical case 1.
  • DNA damage response recap: proteins track DNA and find lesions; this activates p53, which stimulates production of DNA repair proteins. One repair mechanism is homologous recombination; BRCA1 and BRCA2 are involved in DNA repair through recombination. [Notes] (Genetics class notes, Lec 7.2) BRCA1 and BRCA2 are part of the multi-protein homologous recombination complex, “mutated in breast cancer”; the HRR complexes are largely the same as in meiotic recombination (Lec 7.8).
  • [Slides] “BRCA1/2 mutations and genomic instability”.
  • Discovery of BRCA1 credited to a woman, name transcribed “very clear king” (Mary-Claire King, outside knowledge), who the lecturer says received the Asturias prize last year [verify].

7. Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC)

  • Women inherit a mutated copy of BRCA1 or BRCA2 and have a high increase in risk of both cancers.
  • General population (no BRCA1/2 mutation): breast cancer risk about 12%, ovarian cancer risk 1 to 2% (transcribed “baby brain cancer”, ovarian). With BRCA1 or 2 mutation: a “huge increase” in both (figures in the slide table: see the slide).
  • Carriers need monitoring and prevention.

  • Patient: “I feel perfectly healthy, but since my mom and aunt both died young, and now I carry the same BRCA1 mutation, what should I do next?”
  • Task: as a board of oncologists, decide your advice.

The two options

OptionPoints from the discussion
Surgery (risk-reducing removal of breasts and ovaries)She is very young; we do not know if she wants children; “if you remove the breast you have to remove the ovaries as well”. Maybe not necessary at this age
Surveillance (close monitoring)Breast: mammography. Ovary: blood tests for circulating tumour markers (“this is done for ovary cancer”) and ultrasound (ecography), so a growing tumour is detected early. Preventive drugs such as tamoxifen are possible but “not very common”

At 35 to 40 the risk “increases exponentially” (wording garbled) [verify]; surgery then decreases the risk “very much, almost completely”.
  • The risk between 29 and 35 is not so high, so she can wait; removing breasts and ovaries “is kind of tough”. The recommendation is based on population studies. In the end, it is the decision of the woman and the doctors; ask whether she wants children (some women decide on surgery right away).
  • Fertility options raised in the discussion: freezing oocytes, preimplantation genetic options. A student’s remark that in some cases the uterus is kept and another structure removed is garbled (“coloquial ducts”) [verify].

Consequences of early removal of the ovaries

  • Early surgery causes hormonal dysregulation; loss of oestrogens has consequences in other diseases.
  • Students’ search results included premature menopause, cardiovascular risk, osteoporosis, dementia, Parkinson’s disease, infertility. The lecturer: the main two are:
RiskLecturer’s explanation
Cardiovascular diseaseEarlier atherosclerosis, heart attacks: oestrogens protect the vascular bed
OsteoporosisOestrogens protect bone from losing its mineral part
  • In men: testosterone is converted into oestrogens by the enzyme aromatase in the vascular system, bone and fat, so oestrogens also protect men against vascular events and bone demineralization.
  • After removing the ovaries, the woman must be treated with oestrogen therapy (also an option if she chooses early surgery).

9. Tumour angiogenesis and metastasis (examinable slide, excluding the video)

  • [Slides] “Without blood vessels: 1-2 mm3”; tumour angiogenesis (recruitment of vessels) allows tumour growth and cell escape into the bloodstream; tumour vessels disorganized (VEGF).
  • At about 1 to 2 mm3, oxygen diffusion no longer reaches the core; cells cannot keep growing.
  • Angiogenic switch: the tumour produces factors such as VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) that act on nearby vessels, stimulating proliferation of endothelial cells and pericytes; new vessels enter and vascularize the tumour, supplying oxygen and nutrients.
  • Critical point: a tumour that cannot overproduce these factors stays at 1 to 2 mm3 and does not grow.
  • Second consequence: tumour cells are now near vessels, so they easily enter the circulation. People with cancer may have many circulating tumour cells; the bloodstream is hostile and few survive, but some anchor in an organ (e.g. the liver) and grow: metastasis.
  • [Notes] the main stimuli of VEGF expression are hypoxia and hypoglycaemia; the main angiogenic factors are VEGF and FGF; without vascularization the tumour becomes necrotic or quiescent; most circulating tumour cells are destroyed by the immune system or do not survive outside the primary tumour. [Lucía] hypoxia and hypoglycaemia stimulate VEGF expression (HIF-1).
  • Normal versus tumour vasculature (electron micrographs; see the slide):
NormalTumour
Hierarchy: arterioles, capillaries, venules; normal flowNo distinguishable capillaries, arterioles or venules; deformed vessels, abnormal morphology, from hyperstimulation by VEGF and other factors
  • [Notes] the new vessels are aberrant, tortuous, with excessive ramifications and microhaemorrhages.
  • Therapy: because tumour vasculature differs, drugs can target tumour vessel cells without affecting normal vasculature: anti-VEGF antibodies or other drugs blocking excessive production of vascular factors. Used usually not alone: in combination with chemotherapy. [Notes] bevacizumab, an antiangiogenic monoclonal antibody against VEGF, indicated in combination with chemotherapy.
  • Video (not to study, per [Slides]): VEGF family (seven subtypes, three receptors); VEGF makes endothelial cells proliferate (extracellular kinases and MAP kinase pathways), induces proteins that break down the basement membrane (MMPs, uPA and its receptor, tPA), increases permeability, helps new endothelial cells survive; drugs: bevacizumab (Avastin, binds VEGF), sunitinib (Sutent, small-molecule inhibitor of VEGF and PDGF receptors). The video then moved to cetuximab (anti-EGFR) and the lecturer stopped it: “this was a different topic”.

10. The immune system and immunotherapy

  • Does the immune system defend us from cancer? Yes, an important role. Proof: people on immunosuppressors (AIDS, liver or kidney transplant recipients) have a high risk of developing cancer. [Notes] incidence is higher in congenital immunodeficiency and in immunosuppressed patients (HIV, transplants).
  • Cancer still develops because tumours learn to avoid the immune system.
  • Immunotherapy aims to re-stimulate the immune system so it recognizes and kills malignant cells. “A revolution”, “a breakthrough in oncology”: it does not benefit all patients, but some clinical trials found cures of advanced or very malignant tumours.
  • Video (narration; summary):
TopicContent
SurveillanceProteins are broken into peptides, presented on the cell surface by MHC (HLA); T cells scan them with the T cell receptor (TCR), “a barcode scanning machine”. Mutated proteins (e.g. from smoking) give abnormal peptides recognized as foreign
EvasionTumours build defences: keep immune cells out, or weaken T cells. Checkpoints on the tumour surface bind T cells and inhibit killing: PD-1 (transcribed “ED1”) and CTLA-4. Secreted factors turn T cells into T regulatory cells that deactivate other T cells
Enhancing immunityCancer vaccines from neoantigens (mutated tumour proteins); adoptive T cell transfer (T cells grown and educated in the laboratory, or modified); cytokines (IL-2, 7, 12, 15); agonist antibodies (anti-OX40, anti-GITR, anti-4-1BB)
Removing tumour defencesAntibodies neutralizing inhibitory factors (anti-IL-10, anti-TGF-beta, transcribed “PGF-beta”; inhibitors transcribed “IDL”, possibly IDO (outside knowledge) [verify]); approved by the FDA: anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4
Melanoma trialUntil recently 50% of patients with advanced melanoma died within 12 months; with antibodies against both CTLA-4 and PD-1, 70% survived more than 3 years
Side effectsNot very common but potentially life-threatening; treatable if found early: diarrhoea (intestinal inflammation), endocrine disease (thyroid dysfunction, diabetes)
  • Nobel Prize: the lecturer: last year’s Nobel Prize in Medicine went to the researchers who discovered T regulatory cells, which tumours manipulate to inhibit the immune system. [Slides] Mary Brunkow, Fred Ramsdell and Shimon Sakaguchi, Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2025, for discoveries on peripheral immune tolerance; regulatory T cells are the immune system’s security guards; tumours induce T regulatory cells, which block the immune cells that fight cancer.
  • [Notes] tumour antigens are presented on MHC class I to CD8+ T cells and, after phagocytosis, on MHC class II to CD4+ T cells; tumours evade by decreasing MHC class I or antigen presentation, and secrete immunosuppressive factors such as TGF-beta.
  • [Lucía] the tumour types that respond best to immunotherapy are lung cancer and melanoma, because a high mutation load gives more antigens for T cells.

11. Focus on breast cancer (skipped in class)

  • From [Slides] only (“Epidemiological data in Spain”):
    • About 35,000 new cases every year; first in incidence among women.
    • 5-year survival after diagnosis >90%.
    • 1 out of 8 women in Spain will develop breast cancer in their lifetime.
    • If detected early, the possibility of cure is about 100%.
    • Histology: normal breast, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), invasive carcinoma (see the slide).
  • [Lucía] in DCIS the glands are enlarged with many cells, but the tumour is still within the gland; in invasive carcinoma glands and ducts can no longer be seen. Not all tumours express oestrogen receptors; tamoxifen can only treat oestrogen receptor-expressing tumours.

12. Clinical case 2: HER2-positive breast cancer

  • First confirm that the tumour expresses HER2: by immunohistochemistry or by FISH (“as we explained yesterday”). [Slides] FISH image: Her-2 in red, chromosome 17 centromere in green; gene amplification of Her-2 in breast cancer. [Notes] about 25 to 30% of breast tumours have multiple copies of Her-2 (amplification, not mutation).
  • Point of the case: real-life treatment of a HER2 tumour is not as easy as just giving anti-HER2 antibodies.
  • [Slides] the case:
StepTreatment
Diagnosis (2013)41-year-old woman, T3N2M0, stage IIIA, HER2 positive
Preoperative chemotherapy2 cycles of epirubicin plus cyclophosphamide, then 2 cycles of weekly paclitaxel
SurgeryMastectomy and axillary lymph node dissection; pathological response to chemotherapy not complete
ThenTrastuzumab and tamoxifen; no post-mastectomy radiotherapy
