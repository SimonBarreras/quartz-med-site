Exam flags from the lecturer

  • Identify each vertebra just by looking at it: “That’s your task.”
  • The spinal functional unit: three joints (one anterior symphysis, two posterior synovial facet joints), the same from C2 down (section 1).
  • Ligament scheme, always the same: “anterior, posterior, yellow, inter, supra” (section 4).
  • Facet joints have no ligaments, only capsules (asked twice).
  • Range of movement by region: cervical = highest; thoracic = mainly rotation; lumbar = flexion-extension (repeated at the end).
  • Half of neck rotation occurs at C1-C2, the other half from C2 to C7.
  • Occipital-C1 = “yes” movement; C1-C2 = “no” movement.
  • Imaging: MRI shows soft tissue; X-ray and CT only bone. Think of ligaments when you look at an X-ray (C1-C2 case, section 5).
  • Not to memorize: the range-of-motion numbers on his slide (“Please do not memorize these numbers”).

1. The spinal functional unit

  • Between two vertebrae: three joints, one anterior and two posterior.
    • Anterior: the intervertebral disc, a solid joint, a symphysis.
    • Posterior: two synovial joints: facet joints = zygapophyseal joints.
  • Same scheme all through the spine; the only difference is between C1 and C2 (section 5).
  • Axial view at the level of the disc (lumbar unit): the disc, the two posterior synovial joints, and between them the big foramen for the spinal cord or spinal nerves (lumbar level: spinal nerves, no spinal cord).
  • Tripod: when one joint moves, the other two move. Bending forward: the disc is compressed and the facet joints separate. Bending backward (transcript “extend our knee” [verify], context = spine): the facet joints are compressed and the spinous processes approach; the anterior disc opens. If one joint does not work, the others do not move as they should: that is when problems come.
  • Functional X-rays in flexion and extension: the distance between vertebral bodies changes; in extension the disc opens anteriorly.

2. Intervertebral disc (symphysis)

  • Attaches one vertebral body to another through a thin layer of hyaline cartilage above and below the disc, attached to the vertebral body. [Notes] Function: union of vertebrae, load transmission.
  • Two components:
Annulus fibrosusNucleus pulposus
PositionExternal ring, contains the nucleusJelly sphere inside the ring
Structure (lecturer)Concentric layers with oblique fibres in alternating directions: gives strength, keeps the nucleus insideMainly water (80%); comes from the notochord
Vessels / nervesAvascular; innervated only in the outer third([Notes] no arteries or nerves)
[Notes]Fibrous concentric layers; internal vertical, intermediate oblique, external horizontal; stabilizes, contains the nucleusCollagen II, few cells, ECM with 80% water
  • [check] Fibre orientation of the annulus: lecturer “oblique fibres in alternating directions”; [Notes] internal vertical, intermediate oblique, external horizontal.
  • Height of the disc: increases from cranial to caudal; the biggest disc is L5-S1. Relative to the vertebral body the disc is bigger in the cervical spine, because the cervical spine has the highest range of movement. Height determines range of movement. [Notes] Same.
  • Mechanics: a ball between two plates; the annulus keeps the ball in place; the nucleus is like a balloon full of water: incompressible but deformable, absorbs compression forces. The disc attaches the vertebral bodies, absorbs shocks and allows motion. [Notes] Functions as a liquid: incompressible, transmits pressure equally in all directions, maximally deformable (distributes loads).
  • Height during the day: about 1 cm shorter in the evening: discs act like sponges, lose water when compressed (standing, sitting) and rehydrate lying down.
  • Ageing: young vs adult disc: with age the nucleus dehydrates and the annulus loses its mechanical properties and shape. Normal for everyone; the degenerated disc no longer handles compression as before.
  • Herniated disc (clinical): the annulus breaks, the nucleus escapes, compressing neural structures (spinal cord or spinal nerves). MRI: healthy discs show water (white) in the nucleus; the herniated one has lost it. Patients report pain down the leg; if conservative treatment fails, surgery removes the rest of the nucleus to decompress the nerves; nothing is restored. Disc prostheses have some good results in the cervical spine, not as good in the lumbar spine.
  • Imaging: spine MRI (cervical, thoracic, lumbar): you see soft tissue, so it is an MRI.

3. Facet (zygapophyseal) joints

  • Synovial: a facet of the upper vertebra with a facet of the lower vertebra, with a joint space and fluid.
  • No ligaments, but a strong capsule. [check] [Notes] “Articular capsule, thin and rigid. No specific ligaments.”
  • Inclination of the facets determines the range of movement:
RegionFacet orientation (lecturer)Movement
CervicalMost obliqueHighest range: flexion-extension, lateral flexion, some rotation
ThoracicMore verticalMainly rotation
LumbarOblique but “in a coronal place” [verify] the planeMostly flexion-extension
  • Exercise: with the pelvis fixed, turning the trunk moves the thoracic spine; bending forward moves the lumbar spine.
  • Uncovertebral joints: only in the cervical spine, two small joints between vertebral bodies formed by the uncinate processes (“like two horns”); allow more movement. Not in thoracic or lumbar spine.

5. Craniovertebral junction (occiput, C1, C2)

  • The functional unit changes here.
LevelJointsTypeMovement
Occiput-C1 (atlas)Two synovial jointsBicondylar (condylar)Flexion-extension: the “yes” movement
C1-C2 (atlas-axis)Two lateralPlane (gliding)With the median joint: rotation, the “no” movement
One median (atlanto-axial): odontoid process of the axis with the anterior arch of the atlasPivot, held by a ligament around the odontoid
  • [Notes] Atlanto-occipital: synovial, bicondylar, axes converge anteriorly; occipital condyles + superior articular facets of the atlas; anterior and posterior atlanto-occipital membranes. Atlanto-axial rotation combines rotation of the atlas on the odontoid with sliding of the flat lateral joints.
  • Half of neck rotation occurs at C1-C2, the other half from C2 to C7.
  • Median joint: two synovial spaces: anterior (anterior arch of atlas and odontoid) and posterior (odontoid and transverse ligament). [Notes] Same.
  • Ligaments, from posterior to anterior in dissection:
    • Tectorial membrane (continuation of the posterior longitudinal ligament). Cut it to see:
    • Cruciform ligament: the transverse ligament (the most important part, keeps the odontoid in place) with a superior and an inferior expansion (makes the cross).
    • Cut the superior expansion to see the apical ligament: odontoid process to the foramen magnum (occipital bone).
    • Two small ligaments from the odontoid to the occipital condyles reinforce stability ([Notes] alar ligaments, from each side of the apex of the odontoid to the medial part of each occipital condyle).
    • [Notes] Superior expansion reaches the anterior border of the foramen magnum; inferior expansion reaches behind the body of the axis. [Atlas] Plate 47: tectorial membrane, cruciform ligament (superior longitudinal band, transverse ligament, inferior longitudinal band), alar ligaments, apical ligament of dens.
  • Clinical X-ray: the space between the anterior arch of C1 and the odontoid is bigger than expected: no fracture, but a transverse ligament injury. The odontoid moving backwards compresses the spinal cord at C2: “game over” (also no breathing).

6. Rib cage joints

  • Breathing in deeply: the ribs move upwards and outwards, to let air into the lungs (“if anyone doesn’t feel his ribs moving… we’re going to the emergency room”).
  • Movement between ribs and spine through two joints, both synovial (capsule, ligaments, fluid, space):
JointBetweenNotes
Costovertebral (joint of the head of the rib)Head of the rib and the vertebral body (the thoracic facets)[Notes] The two facets of the rib head articulate with its own vertebra and the vertebra above; radiate ligament; simple capsule
CostotransverseTubercle of the rib and the transverse processUsed as a surgical reference: to remove a vertebral body (tumours, metastasis), the surgeon cuts through the costotransverse joint, a safe route to the anterior vertebral body. [Notes] Costotransverse ligaments (proper, lateral, superior); costotransverse foramen for the intercostal nerve
  • [Notes] The ribs rotate around their longitudinal axes.
  • Sternocostal joints: 1st rib = synchondrosis; 2nd to 7th = synovial. (He also points out the clavicle with the sternum on the image.) [Notes] Sternocostal articulations: the 7 superior costal cartilages with the sternum; interchondral joints (VII to XI, ±V and VI) synovial.
  • Sternum: two symphyses, manubrium-body and body-xiphoid. [Notes] Manubriosternal and xiphosternal joints: symphysis.
  • Ribs move through the spine and through the sternocostal joints.
