Exam flags from the lecturer

  • Fibre directions and layer order: “This is one of the most important things in this class.” He gave three body mnemonics for the abdominal layers and said outright: “when I ask you in the exam what is the direction of the fibres of the external intercostal muscles, you can put your hands in your pockets and you will remember that” (sections 2 and 7).
  • The inguinal canal table: “This is the table you need to memorize.” What is needed: ceiling, floor, posterior wall, anterior wall, and the two rings (entrance and exit) (section 11).
  • “Also draw the rectus sheath, both in coronal view but also axial”, and “remember the arcuate line is the line that differentiates the upper part from the lower part” (section 9). Earlier, on the same figure: “You need to draw this to understand it. That’s my recommendation.”
  • Three anatomic references for the dermatomes, repeated twice: nipples T4, xiphoid process T6, umbilicus T10, with L1 the groin added for the abdomen (sections 5 and 10).
  • Only the external intercostal elevates the ribs; the other four depress them (section 2, but see the [check] on the innermost).
  • Admin: the inguinal canal summary table is not in the slide deck the students have. “I will put them in a bit today, as well as last class”: expect updated slides for this class and for IOSI Muscles of the back.
  • Practical: he refers throughout to what the students will see in the cadaver lab (which layers have to be cut to expose the next).

1. The five muscles of the thoracic wall [Slides p2-4]

  • Muscles of the pectoral region [Slides p3]: pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, subclavius, anterior serratus. Not covered here: “we will study them in the upper limb chapter.”
  • Five muscles form the thoracic wall: three between the ribs (external, internal and innermost intercostal) and two inside the thoracic cage (subcostal and transversus thoracis).
  • On the naming: a student asked why it is not “external, intermediate, internal”. His answer: “the people who decided the names decided that the middle layer is internal and the deepest one is innermost.”

  • [check] Action of the innermost intercostal. [Slides p11] says elevate ribs; the lecturer said twice that it has the same origin, insertion, direction and function as the internal intercostal, that is depress, and summarised “all of them but one depress ribs; only the external intercostal elevates”. Both versions given; worth settling before the exam, since the summary rule he wants learnt depends on it.
  • Other slide details: at the costal cartilage the external intercostal forms the external intercostalis ligament, an aponeurotic band [p5]; the internal intercostal likewise forms the internal intercostalis ligament [p7].
  • Innermost intercostals are in contact with the parietal pleura and the endothoracic fascia [p11], because they are the deepest layer, “next to covering the lungs”.
  • Dissection notes he gave: in the cadaver the most anterior part of the intercostal space has aponeurosis over the internal muscles, which has to be cut to see them. To see the transversus thoracis you must cut the ribs and open the thoracic cage, because it lies just behind the sternum: “if you don’t cut the ribs you won’t find it.”

2. Breathing demonstration (standing) and the mechanics

The class stood up, put one hand on the sternum and the other on the lower ribs:

ObservationExplanation he gave
The hand on the sternum moves forward when breathing inOn inspiration the sternum moves forward
The hand on the lower ribs moves outwardThe lower ribs move outward
Both are produced by the external intercostals elevating the ribs
Blowing out hard (“as if you were putting out 50 candles”) hardens the bellyForced expiration: the ribs go down and the abdominal wall hardens; the internal intercostals and the abdominal muscles do this
Quiet expirationPassive. “There’s no muscle contraction”: the external intercostals simply relax. A student asked explicitly whether the subcostal and transversus thoracis are used in quiet breathing out; answer: they are used when you force it (blowing out candles, inflating a balloon), not in normal quiet breathing
  • [verify] He also named a second muscle that relaxes in quiet expiration, saying “another muscle, you already know, which is the…”: the transcript then repeats a fragment (“leventor”) about twenty times and the name is lost. The levatores costarum are the rib elevators covered in IOSI Muscles of the back, which fits, but the recording does not settle it.

3. Neurovascular bundle of the intercostal space [Slides p17-19]

  • The bundle runs between the internal and the innermost intercostal muscles [Slides p17]. He asked this in the two-minute quiz and repeated it for the abdominal wall (section 7).
  • Order of the main bundle, from top to bottom: vein, artery, nerve (VAN), running just below the costal groove of the upper rib [Slides p18]. He gave a Spanish mnemonic for the order, VAN, and glossed it as “vein, artery, nerve… from top to bottom” (the Spanish phrase itself is garbled in the transcript [verify]).
  • Accessory (collateral) bundle: at the bottom of the space, just above the lower rib, and in the reverse order: nerve, artery, vein.
  • Why the reverse order, as he drew it out of the class: the bundle at the top is the main one; the accessory is a collateral branch, and the arrangement keeps the nerve protected and leaves space to puncture the intercostal space safely. The most important structures are the nerves and arteries, and the two sets are kept separate.
  • Arterial supply [Slides p19]: posterior intercostal arteries from the thoracic aorta; anterior intercostal arteries from the internal thoracic artery. Veins run parallel to the arteries, and cutaneous branches arise from the intercostal vessels to supply the superficial fascia [Slides p18].
  • Colour convention he reminded them of: veins blue, arteries red, nerves yellow.
  • [Slides only] Lymphatic drainage [p20]: parasternal nodes and intercostal nodes, both to the bronchomediastinal trunk; diaphragmatic nodes. Not lectured.

4. Innervation of the thoracic wall [Slides p21-22]

  • The spinal nerve emerges and divides; the anterior ramus forms the intercostal nerve. The posterior rami supply the intrinsic (autochthonous) back muscles; the anterior rami supply the intercostal and the migrated muscles (IOSI Muscles of the back).
  • [Slides only] [p22]: the anterior branch of T1 contributes to the brachial plexus; the intercostobrachial nerve is the lateral cutaneous branch of the second intercostal nerve and gives cutaneous innervation to the medial surface of the upper arm; the lower intercostal nerves innervate the muscles, skin and peritoneum of the abdominal wall.

  • Definition he asked the class for: a dermatome is the area of skin innervated by one nerve.

  • Student question: the yellow area at the top of the figure is the supraclavicular nerves, which come from the cervical region. “But we are talking today about thorax.”
  • [verify] In one passage the transcript gives the dermatome list as “T12, T10, umbilical”, which contradicts the rest of the lecture and the slide; read it as T6 xiphoid, T10 umbilicus as everywhere else.

6. Layers of the anterior abdominal wall [Slides p24-26]

Order from the surface, as he listed them: skin, fat (superficial fascia), then three muscle layers, then the transversalis fascia; in the midline the rectus abdominis and pyramidalis, wrapped by the rectus sheath.

  • Superficial fascia: one fascia, two layers [Slides p26]: superficial layer = Camper, deep layer = Scarpa.
  • The three flat layers correspond one to one with the intercostal layers: external oblique to external intercostal, internal oblique to internal intercostal, and transversus abdominis in place of the innermost.
  • Rectus sheath, as he defined it in passing: the junction of the fasciae and aponeuroses of these muscles around the rectus abdominis; it is “what forms the six-pack”.

7. The three flat muscles [Slides p27-35]

MuscleOrigin [Slides]Insertion [Slides]Innervation [Slides]Action [Slides]Fibre direction
External oblique [p27-28]External face of ribs V-XIILinea alba, pubic tubercle, iliac crest6 lower thoracic nervesCompresses and holds viscera, trunk flexion and rotationInferomedial: same as the external intercostal, “up to down, lateral to anterior”
Internal oblique [p30-31]Thoracolumbar fascia, anterior 2/3 of the iliac crest, anterior 2/3 of the inguinal ligamentLower borders of ribs X-XII, linea alba, pubis6 lower thoracic and 1st lumbar nervesSameSuperomedial: perpendicular to the external oblique
Transversus abdominis [p32-33]Internal aspects of costal cartilages VII-XII, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, lateral 1/3 of the inguinal ligamentLinea alba, internal oblique aponeurosis, pubic crest via the conjoint tendon6 lower thoracic and 1st lumbar nervesCompresses and holds viscera, rotation and lateral flexionHorizontal, the deepest layer. He stressed it is not like the innermost intercostal: the fibres are horizontal
  • Additional slide detail: the internal oblique’s superoposterior fibres go to the costal cartilages and its inferoanterior fibres to the aponeurosis [p31].
  • The external oblique forms the inguinal ligament [Slides p28]: its aponeurosis runs from the anterior superior iliac spine to the pubic tubercle. The lacunar ligament is the medial expansion to the pectineal crest [p28].
  • Clinical aside: the femoral artery, vein and nerve leave the pelvis for the lower limb below the inguinal ligament. “When you palpate your pulse in the groin, you are feeling those vessels coming out below the inguinal ligament.”
  • Conjoint tendon: a tendon formed between the transversus abdominis and the internal oblique that inserts on the pubic crest.
  • Function compared with the back: the back muscles mostly extend; these flex the trunk and help bend it, besides compressing and holding the viscera.

7b. The three function demonstrations

DemonstrationWhat works
Pull the belly button towards the spineAll three layers compress the abdomen, the transversus abdominis most of all
Twist to the rightThe right external oblique and the left internal oblique: “two muscles, one diagonal”
Sit and lift the feet off the floorThe rectus abdominis and its tendinous intersections
  • Student question, why the opposite internal oblique contracts when twisting: think of the fibre directions, “one is stretching and the other one is contracting”. The internal oblique on the other side contracts to avoid over-twisting, retaining the thoracic cage: it acts as a counterpoint.
  • Why three different fibre directions at all: so the trunk can fold in every direction, giving flexion and rotation; when you twist, “you need one moving, the other one retaining”.
  • Dissection note: in the cadaver lab you cut the external oblique to see the internal oblique, and the deepest layer is the transversus abdominis.

8. Transversalis fascia, rectus abdominis, pyramidalis [Slides p36-41]

  • Transversalis fascia [Slides p36]: covers the deep surface of the transversus muscle and is continuous posteriorly with the parietal pelvic fascia. It lies behind the muscles of the abdominal wall.
  • Rectus abdominis [Slides p38]: origin symphysis and pubic crest, insertion xiphoid process and costal cartilages V-VII, 6 lower thoracic nerves, compresses and holds viscera, trunk flexion; interrupted by 3 or 4 tendinous intersections. (The lecturer described it in the other direction, from the xiphoid and costal cartilages down to the symphysis and pubic crest.) The tendinous intersections are what makes a six-pack, eight-pack or four-pack, and vary between individuals.
  • Pyramidalis [Slides p40]: origin pubis, insertion linea alba, XII thoracic nerve. It tenses the linea alba. Not everyone has it: “when you are dissecting the cadaver, if you don’t see it, it’s normal.”
  • Linea alba, as he defined it: the line of connective tissue in the midline of the abdomen connecting all these muscles.

9. The rectus sheath and the arcuate line [Slides p42-43]

Upper three quarters [Slides p42], from anterior to posterior:

PositionContents
AnteriorExternal oblique aponeurosis + anterior half of the internal oblique aponeurosis
MiddleRectus abdominis muscle
PosteriorThe other half of the internal oblique aponeurosis + transversus abdominis aponeurosis
  • [check] The slide line for the posterior layer reads “aponeurosis of the internal oblique and posterior half of the transversus” [p42], which splits the transversus rather than the internal oblique; the lecturer consistently said the internal oblique aponeurosis splits into an anterior and a posterior half and that the whole transversus aponeurosis goes posteriorly. Both given.
  • Below the arcuate line: all the aponeuroses pass anteriorly, and posteriorly there is only the transversalis fascia [Slides p42]. His phrasing: “above the arcuate line there is aponeurosis behind the muscles; below it there is only fascia.”
  • The arcuate line is the end of the upper three quarters of the sheath, the transition at the inferior quarter [Slides p42].
  • The aponeuroses forming the rectus sheath join in the midline to form the linea alba [Slides p42].
  • Exchange on which part is better protected: the lower part anteriorly, because below the arcuate line all the aponeuroses lie anterior to the muscle.
  • Aponeurosis vs fascia, as he defined them when asked:
Definition he gave
AponeurosisThe end of the muscle; the same as a tendon, but sheet-like rather than cord-like. Thick and strong, because it converts muscle contraction into movement
FasciaThe connective tissue covering the muscle or organ. Thinner
  • The muscle is continuous with its aponeurosis or tendon, and the fascia covers the muscle. A student asked where the fascia ends: where the muscle and the aponeurosis end.

10. Innervation and dermatomes of the abdominal wall [Slides p44-45]

  • Motor innervation T7 to L1, and the nerves run between the transversus and the internal oblique [Slides p44], the same plane as the intercostal bundle.
  • Dermatomes [Slides p45]: T6 xiphoid, T10 umbilicus, L1 groin and pubis. The new reference is L1: “when you palpate the groin, the sensation is carried by L1.”

11. The inguinal canal [Slides p47-60]

He called it “the scariest part of this class” and said “we don’t have time for this” before going through the rings and walls at speed.

  • Definition [Slides p47]: a slit that runs downward and inward, just above and parallel to the lower half of the inguinal ligament.

Deep inguinal ring (entrance) [Slides p50]Superficial inguinal ring (exit) [Slides p52]
What it isEvagination of the transversalis fascia above the inguinal ligament: “a hole in the fascia that gets into the canal”Outgrowth of the external oblique aponeurosis: a hole in that aponeurosis
PositionLateral to the inferior epigastric vesselsAbove the pubic tubercle and the medial end of the inguinal ligament, where the external oblique inserts
Continues asInternal spermatic fascia; it runs medially and caudallyExternal spermatic fascia
PillarsMedial pillar: the part of the external oblique aponeurosis inserting on the pubic crest and pubic symphysis. Lateral pillar: fibres of the same aponeurosis going to the pubic tubercle
  • His way of remembering which is which: “the names are telling you: deep = entrance, posterior; superficial = exit, anterior.”

11b. The four walls (the table to memorize) [Slides p54-57]

WallFormed by
AnteriorExternal oblique aponeurosis, reinforced in its lateral third by the internal oblique
PosteriorTransversalis fascia, reinforced in its medial third by the conjoint tendon (junction of transversus and internal oblique)
FloorInguinal ligament (he adds the lacunar ligament at its medial end)
Ceiling (roof)Internal oblique and transversus fibres
  • The weakest portion of the canal is the middle third of the posterior wall [Slides p55]; the lecturer said “the weakest portion is the middle aspect of the posterior wall”.
  • His rule for the table: “two muscles, internal oblique and transversus”, which between them account for the reinforcement of the anterior wall, the conjoint tendon posteriorly, and the ceiling.
  • Dissection note: to see the posterior wall you must cut the anterior wall and get inside the canal; then you see the transversalis fascia and the conjoint tendon.

11c. Contents [Slides p58-60]

  • Spermatic cord in males, round ligament in females.
  • Genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve.
  • Ilioinguinal nerve, which crosses part of the canal and exits through the inguinal ring [Slides p58].
