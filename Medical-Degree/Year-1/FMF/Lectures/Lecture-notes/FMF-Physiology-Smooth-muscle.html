1. Structure and innervation

  • Varicosities instead of synapses: places along the axon where neurotransmitter is released; no postsynaptic specialization, no narrow cleft. The transmitter spreads to nearby areas; smooth muscle cells with receptors there respond. Not one axon to one fibre.
  • Single-unit smooth muscle: a varicosity per fibre is not needed; a few varicosities activate one, two or three nearby fibres which act collectively. Multi-unit: isolated fibres with fewer connections, not compact.
  • [Lucía] Single-unit (visceral): fibres coupled by gap junctions, acting like one long fibre. Multi-unit: each fibre contracts independently.
  • Disposition is very variable: compact walls (e.g. the media of arteries); digestive tract with longitudinal and circular layers (rows of cells, not one big tissue).
  • Nuclei and length: a skeletal fibre (up to e.g. 40 cm) needs hundreds of nuclei to stay alive; smooth muscle cells have one nucleus, are much shorter, and long distances are covered by cells placed one after another.
  • Receptors are concentrated in membrane areas called caveolae (transcript “capillae”), surrounded by sarcoplasmic reticulum; instead of skeletal triads, the reticulum lies near the caveolae.

2. Patterns of activity

PatternExample
Contracted most of the day, relaxed brieflySphincters (pylorus, cardia, bladder sphincter)
Relaxed most of the day, contracts a few timesUrinary bladder (detrusor), gallbladder (transcript “suplectus” [verify]; contracts with meals)
Rhythmic contraction-relaxationStomach / digestive tract (moving contents along)
Always some tone, higher or lowerBlood vessels (blood pressure control), airways
  • (Skeletal muscle: type 1 fibres for sustained effort, type 2 for short efforts; most skeletal muscles are relaxed much of the time, e.g. during sleep.)
  • Such different patterns need a different excitation-contraction coupling.

3. Calcium acts through myosin phosphorylation

  • Calcium is the shared element, but it acts differently.
  • Skeletal (recap): calcium binds troponin, which moves tropomyosin and exposes myosin binding sites on actin.
  • Smooth muscle thin filaments are just actin: no troponin, no tropomyosin; binding sites are always available.
  • Smooth muscle myosin is a different subtype: it binds actin only when phosphorylated (on the light chain).
  • Calcium + calmodulin activate myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), which phosphorylates the light chain and starts contraction.
  • A phosphatase (myosin light chain phosphatase) removes the phosphate; if the kinase is no longer activated, contraction stops. Calcium must be maintained to sustain contraction.
  • Once phosphorylated, the cross-bridge cycle is the same as in skeletal muscle (ATP binding to unbind, hydrolysis to recock).
  • Because calcium must activate a kinase that phosphorylates myosin, contraction is much slower than in skeletal muscle.
  • [Lucía] Unphosphorylated smooth muscle myosin has very little affinity for actin; phosphorylation/dephosphorylation in equilibrium.

4. Sources of cytoplasmic calcium

  • Skeletal: only from the reticulum: action potential into the T-tubule, DHPR pushes RyR, calcium released.
  • Smooth muscle, several mechanisms (possibly combined in one fibre):
    • Reticulum release (via second messengers, e.g. IP3, from metabotropic receptors);
    • Extracellular calcium through receptor-operated (ligand-gated) calcium channels opened by a neurotransmitter;
    • Voltage-gated calcium channels: depolarization (e.g. an action potential in the fibre) opens them. (Recall: voltage-gated calcium channels also trigger transmitter release at presynaptic terminals.)
    • Receptors may act directly (ionotropic) or via second messengers (metabotropic).
  • [Lucía] Calcium entering through ligand-gated channels can depolarize and open voltage-gated channels.

5. Modulation independent of calcium: Rho and NO

Rho

  • Rho (activated by second messengers) activates Rho kinase, which inhibits the myosin light chain phosphatase (acting on its regulators): more phosphorylated myosin for the same calcium, i.e. more contraction.
  • So with the same calcium, contraction can be higher or lower. Only in smooth muscle; in skeletal muscle calcium alone decides.

Nitric oxide (NO)

  • Example drugs: sildenafil-type “blue pills” (Viagra, commercial name; transcript “the agra”); apart from erectile uses they treat pulmonary hypertension: they relax smooth muscle.
  • NO acts through cyclic GMP, leading to PKG1 (protein kinase G), with five actions:
    1. Activates an inhibitor of the reticulum calcium channels: less release from the reticulum.
    2. Inhibits metabotropic receptors that release calcium via IP3: less release (indirect).
    3. Activates the myosin light chain phosphatase: less phosphorylation, less contraction.
    4. Opens potassium channels: membrane potential more negative, voltage-gated calcium channels less likely to open: less calcium entry.
    5. Directly inhibits voltage-gated calcium channels: less calcium entry.
  • Summary in three: less calcium release from the reticulum, less calcium entry from outside, phosphatase activation. A very powerful inhibitor of smooth muscle contraction, hence a good drug target.
  • [check] [Lucía]‘s list differs slightly: mechanism 1 inhibits IP3 activation via a G-protein receptor; 2 lowers ER calcium release; 3 phosphatase; 4 potassium channels; 5 direct block of voltage-gated calcium channels. She also writes Viagra “contains NO” (the lecturer only said these drugs act on this pathway).

6. Calcium sensitization and desensitization

  • Curve of calcium concentration vs myosin light chain phosphorylation can shift left or right (Rho, NO): the cell becomes more or less sensitive to calcium.
  • Does not happen in skeletal muscle: there the same calcium always gives the same result.
  • This, plus the many calcium sources, explains the very different activity patterns of section 2.
  • [Lucía] Active phosphatase: more calcium needed (shift right); inactive: less needed (shift left).

7. Membrane potential and force

Three patterns on his graph (membrane potential above, force below):

  1. Action potentials (calcium via voltage-gated channels or reticulum) produce a progressive force increase, as in skeletal muscle, but always slow.
  2. Mixed: rhythmic oscillations of membrane potential with occasional action potentials; contraction follows the rhythm (e.g. much of the lower digestive tract). Smooth muscle does not need action potentials: small changes in membrane potential change calcium entry through voltage-gated channels.
  3. Contraction without any change in membrane potential: metabotropic receptors and second messengers release calcium from the reticulum (skeletal muscle has only nicotinic ionotropic receptors).
  • Second messenger rule: IP3: contraction; cAMP: relaxation.
    • Alpha-adrenergic receptors: contraction, via IP3. Alpha agonists raise blood pressure.
    • Beta-adrenergic receptors: relaxation. Beta agonists lower blood pressure or improve breathing (inhaled asthma drugs relax airway smooth muscle). [check] The transcript says beta receptors “use IP3”; by his own rule relaxation goes with cAMP.
  • Smooth muscle receptors are spread over the membrane, so it responds not only to varicosities but also to hormones and substances in the blood or extracellular space.

8. Long contraction with little energy

  • Advantages of the complex mechanism: lots of regulation (many transmitters, drugs, hormones) and sustained contraction with minimal energy.
  • Even type 1 skeletal fibres spend a lot of energy to stay contracted (think of the food needed after a long run).
  • Smooth muscle: a high initial calcium peak to start contraction, then an intermediate calcium level, just enough for a few myosin heads to bind from time to time and replace those that unbind. Smooth muscle myosin has a very slow turnover (slow ATP use).
  • Easy in smooth muscle because binding sites are always exposed; only the number of phosphorylated myosin heads is regulated.
  • Useful for sphincters.
  • [Lucía] Example durations: sphincters about 23 hours a day.

9. Adaptability to stretch

  • Skeletal muscle: sarcomeres perfectly aligned; force depends strongly on starting length.
  • Smooth muscle: thick and thin filaments not strictly parallel; stretching makes them more parallel. So it keeps similar force over a wide range of lengths.
  • Urinary bladder: can contract with little urine or when full. Limit: from fully stretched it contracts to about half its size, then the fibres readjust; so after a very long wait you may need to urinate again about half an hour later.
  • [Lucía] No sarcomeres; filaments dispersed.

10. Speed and force

  • Even the slowest skeletal muscle is about an order of magnitude faster than the fastest smooth muscle.
  • Skeletal: much force, fast. Smooth: less force, slower, but more adaptable, better regulated, lower energy cost.

11. In-class quiz

  • Common element in excitation-contraction coupling: calcium (not action potentials: some smooth muscle contracts without them; not troponin: absent in smooth muscle).
  • Muscles around the eye: levator palpebrae (skeletal, voluntary) and the smooth Müller’s muscle (transcript “carousel muscle of Miller”), not under voluntary control: why the eyes open more when afraid. Pupillary sphincter (constrictor) and dilator: both smooth, not voluntary. Similar pairs elsewhere: an internal smooth and an external skeletal sphincter (transcript garbled [verify] which). The pylorus is also a sphincter.

12. Growth

  • Smooth muscle fibres can divide (skeletal fibres usually do not, beyond some new cell generation): e.g. chronic hypertension thickens the arterial media with more smooth muscle cells.
  • They can also grow in size: instead of more nuclei, they increase their DNA: tetraploid or octoploid.
  • [Lucía] Same (hypertrophy with increased ploidy, one nucleus).
