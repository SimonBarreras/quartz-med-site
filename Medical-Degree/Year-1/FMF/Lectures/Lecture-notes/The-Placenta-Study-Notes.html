The Placenta: Study Notes

fmf medicine Sep 27, 2026 · @simon

1. Definition and the two components

The placenta is the ephemeral materno-fetal organ that enables nutrient and gas exchange between mother and fetus. Together with the umbilical cord it forms a transport system between the two.

The mature placenta has two parts:

ComponentOriginWhat it is
Fetal: villous (bushy) chorion, chorion frondosumTrophoblast + extraembryonic mesodermThe chorionic villi at the embryonic pole
Maternal: decidua basalisEndometriumThe decidua deep to the conceptus

One-line summary for the group: fetal villi float in pools of maternal blood (a hemochorial placenta); the two bloods never mix, they exchange across the placental membrane.

2. Development: decidua and villous chorion

The decidua (maternal side)

The decidua is the endometrium of the pregnant uterus. It has three named regions, defined by position relative to the conceptus:

RegionPositionFate
Decidua basalisDeep to the conceptus (between it and the myometrium)Becomes the maternal component of the placenta (decidual plate + septa)
Decidua capsularisSuperficial, overlying the conceptusStretches and thins over the smooth chorion; by month 3 it fuses with the parietalis
Decidua parietalisAll the rest of the uterine liningFuses with the amniochorionic membrane, obliterating the uterine cavity

[image: Decidua basalis, capsularis and parietalis at the end of months 2 and 3]

Slide 6. Left, end of month 2: basalis (under the bushy chorion), capsularis (over the sac), parietalis (the rest); the uterine cavity still exists. Right, end of month 3: capsularis has fused with parietalis and the cavity is gone.

Chorionic villi (fetal side)

Villi develop in three stages. The key idea: each stage adds one layer to the core.

StageWhenCoreCovering
Primary villusEnd of week 2Cytotrophoblast (finger-like projections pushing into the syncytiotrophoblast)Syncytiotrophoblast
Secondary villusEarly week 3Extraembryonic mesoderm grows into the cytotrophoblast coreCytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast
Tertiary villusEnd of week 3Mesenchyme differentiates into blood vessels (villous capillaries)Cytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast

[image: Villus development days 4 to 17 and cross-sections of primary, secondary and tertiary villi]

Slide 12 (Carlson, 2019). Top: timeline from implantation (day 7) to anchoring and floating villi (day 17). Bottom: A primary (cytotrophoblast core), B secondary (mesoderm core), C tertiary (capillaries in the core).

  • Chorion = extraembryonic mesoderm (chorionic plate) + cytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Cytotrophoblast cell columns break through the syncytium and spread over the endometrium as the outer cytotrophoblastic shell, which anchors the chorion to the endometrium.
  • Anchoring villi reach and attach to the decidua basalis via the shell; floating (free) villi branch off them and hang in the intervillous space.
  • Intervillous space: the lacunae (from week 2) that fill with maternal blood from eroded maternal sinusoids / spiral arteries.
  • By the end of week 4, tertiary villi surround the entire chorionic sac.

[image: Outer cytotrophoblast shell anchoring villi to the endometrium around the intervillous space]

Slide 13. Follow a villus from the chorionic plate (bottom) up to the outer cytotrophoblast shell (top), which is glued to the endometrium. The red space between villi is the intervillous space, filled by maternal vessels.

Chorionic vessels and the first circulation

  • Week 3: blood islands appear first in the extraembryonic mesoderm around the yolk sac, then in the connecting stalk and chorion. They differentiate into blood cells (centre) and endothelial cells (edge): vasculogenesis.
  • Signals on the slide: FGF2/FGFR turns mesoderm into hemangioblasts; VEGF via VEGFR2 (Flk1) drives hemangioblasts; VEGF via VEGFR1 (Flt1) drives tube formation; later branching into arteries and veins.
  • Also in week 3, the embryonic (intraembryonic) mesoderm forms its own vessels.
  • Chorionic plate vessels join vessels in the connecting stalk (future umbilical cord), which join the embryo’s vessels.
  • By day 22 (start of week 4) a primordial cardiovascular system is formed and functional: heart, dorsal aortae, cardinal veins, vitelline vessels (yolk sac) and umbilical vessels (chorion).

Spiral artery remodelling and immune tolerance

  • Endovascular extravillous trophoblasts (eEVTs) invade maternal spiral arteries and remodel them into wide, low-resistance vessels that flood the intervillous space.
  • Cells at the decidual-placental interface: SCT (syncytiotrophoblast), VCT (villous cytotrophoblast), EVT, decidual NK cells (dNK, the most abundant), decidual macrophages (dM), dendritic cells, T cells, decidual stromal cells.
  • Why the mother does not reject the fetus: trophoblast has a unique HLA profile (HLA-C, -E, -F, -G on the slide; notably HLA-G) that binds receptors on local immune cells (KIR, NKG2, ILT families), producing tolerance (Lin et al., 2022).
  • Beyond the slides: trophoblast lacks classical HLA-A and HLA-B, and poor spiral artery remodelling is linked to pre-eclampsia. Good if someone asks “so what?”

From leafy sac to discoid placenta

  • Week 3: villi cover the whole chorionic sac (fluffy appearance).
  • End of month 2: villi persist and branch only at the embryonic pole (facing the decidua basalis) = villous/bushy chorion. Villi on the abembryonic side (facing the capsularis) degenerate = smooth chorion (chorion laeve).
  • Months 4 to 5: decidua is eroded to enlarge the intervillous space, leaving wedge-shaped placental (decidual) septa that divide the maternal surface into cotyledons. Septa do not reach the chorionic plate, so the intervillous space stays continuous.
  • Amnion: the amniotic cavity expands and obliterates most of the chorionic cavity. The amnion covers the umbilical cord and fuses with the smooth chorion = amniochorionic membrane, which then fuses with the decidua parietalis.

[image: Placental septa dividing the placenta into cotyledons; spiral artery jet into the intervillous space]

Slide 20. Left top: maternal surface with cotyledons. Left bottom: fetal surface with cord and chorionic vessels. Right: decidual septa hang from the decidual plate but stop short of the chorionic plate; a spiral artery jets blood into each compartment.

3. Gross anatomy of the full-term placenta

A term placenta is a disc with two very different faces. Typical figures (textbook, not on the slides): about 500 g, 15 to 25 cm across, 2 to 3 cm thick.

FeatureFetal surfaceMaternal surface
LookSmooth, shiny, grey-blueRough, dark red, spongy
Covered byTransparent amnion (peels off) over the chorionic plateThin layer of decidua basalis that tore away at delivery
LandmarksUmbilical cord insertion; chorionic vessels radiating from it under the amnion (arteries usually cross over veins)Cotyledons (lobes) separated by grooves where the placental septa were
Derived fromChorionic plate (fetal)Decidua basalis (maternal) + villous chorion

[image: Full-term placenta: maternal surface with cotyledons, fetal surface with cord and vessels, membranes]

Slide 23 (Moore). A: maternal surface, cotyledon outlined, grooves between. B: fetal surface, vessels radiating under transparent amnion. C: amnion and smooth chorion lifted, false knot in the cord. D: fetal surface with artery and vein labelled. This is what you will hold in the lab.

At the rim, the placenta continues as the membranes: amnion (inner) + smooth chorion (outer), i.e. the amniochorionic membrane. The slide photo also shows a false knot in the cord (a vessel loop or Wharton’s jelly bulge, not a true knot).

Umbilical cord

  • Transverse section: 2 umbilical arteries + 1 umbilical vein, embedded in Wharton’s jelly (mucoid connective tissue), covered by amnion.
  • Arteries carry O2-poor blood from fetus to placenta. The vein carries O2-rich blood from placenta to fetus. (Named by direction relative to the fetal heart, not by oxygen.)
  • The vein has the wider lumen and thinner wall; the arteries are thick-walled, often constricted.
  • Single umbilical artery is a recognised variant shown on the slide; it is associated with fetal anomalies (beyond slides: roughly 1% of cords).

[image: Umbilical cord cross-section with two arteries, one vein, Wharton’s jelly; normal vs single umbilical artery]

Slide 24. Right: histology of the cord, 2 small thick-walled arteries, 1 larger vein, Wharton’s jelly, amnion on the surface. Bottom left: normal cord vs single umbilical artery.

4. The placental membrane (barrier)

The placental membrane separates maternal blood (intervillous space) from fetal blood (villous capillaries). It thins with gestation to make exchange easier.

TimeLayers, maternal to fetal side
~10 weeks: 4 layers1. Syncytiotrophoblast 2. Cytotrophoblast 3. Connective tissue (villous mesenchyme) 4. Fetal capillary endothelium
Mid-gestation onward: 2 layers1. Syncytiotrophoblast 2. Endothelium

[image: Placental membrane at 10 weeks with 4 layers versus mid-gestation with 2 layers]

Slide 26. Top: villi mature from thick (left) to thin and highly branched (right). Bottom: at 10 weeks the barrier is syncytium, cytotrophoblast, connective tissue, endothelium; by mid-gestation only syncytium and endothelium.

Why it thins: the cuboidal progenitor cytotrophoblast layer becomes thin and regionally discontinuous, and fetal capillaries move up against the syncytium.

Inside a mature villus (cross-section)

Syncytiotrophoblast (outside) > scattered cytotrophoblast cells > basal lamina > fetal mesenchyme with Hofbauer cells (fetal macrophages) > fetal microvessels.

[image: Mature chorionic villus longitudinal and cross-section with transfer lists]

Slide 19. a: spiral artery and uterine vein opening through the cytotrophoblastic shell; anchoring, stem and free-floating villi in the intervillous space. b: villus cross-section with Hofbauer cells and fetal microvessels, plus what moves mother to fetus and back.

The syncytiotrophoblast (ST) is a unique epithelium

  • It is a syncytium: no lateral cell borders.
  • Its apical (maternal-facing) membrane is amplified by microvilli.
  • Transporters on both membranes: GLUTs (glucose), LAT and SNAT (amino acids), FATPs (fatty acids, helped by lipoprotein lipase), nucleoside transporters.
  • Nutrient transport is regulated by maternal hormones and nutrient supply, blood flow, fetal demand, and placental mTOR signalling.
  • Roles: main site of O2, nutrient and waste exchange; a selective barrier; a highly effective barrier to infection; and the main endocrine cell (section 6).

5. Placental circulation and what crosses

Two separate circuits meet at the villus surface without mixing.

Fetal circuit: fetal heart > umbilical arteries (O2-poor) > chorionic vessels in the chorionic plate > stem villi > capillaries in branch villi (exchange) > back to the umbilical vein (O2-rich) > fetus.

Maternal circuit: uterine arteries > endometrial spiral arteries (through the cytotrophoblastic shell) > jet of blood into the intervillous space toward the chorionic plate > bathes the villi > drains back through endometrial veins in the decidual plate.

Memory hook for non-transferable: large or bound molecules (IgM is a pentamer, heparin is a large charged polysaccharide) and whole bacteria. Viruses and Toxoplasma are the exceptions that do get through.

6. Functions of the placenta

The placenta does the job of five fetal organs at once.

#FunctionActs asKey points
1RespiratoryLungsO2 moves from maternal HbA to fetal HbF, which has higher O2 affinity; CO2 goes back
2ExcretoryKidneysBicarbonate, H+ ions, lactic acid, urea, creatinine to the mother
3NutritionGutGlucose, amino acids, lipids, micronutrients (raises maternal vitamin requirements)
4ImmunityImmune systemTransfers maternal IgG (passive protection); ST barrier to infection; HLA-mediated tolerance
5EndocrineEndocrine glandshCG, progesterone, estrogen, hPL, all from the syncytiotrophoblast

Endocrine timeline

  • hCG rises fast from implantation and peaks late in the 1st trimester (around weeks 8 to 10), then falls to a low plateau. Its job: keep the corpus luteum alive so it keeps making progesterone. It is what pregnancy tests detect.
  • Corpus luteum is the main progesterone source in the 1st trimester; the placenta takes over around the end of the 1st trimester (~13 weeks on the slide).
  • Progesterone and estrogen (mainly estriol) rise steadily until term.
  • hPL (human placental lactogen) appears later in the 1st trimester and rises to term; it increases maternal insulin resistance so more glucose is left for the fetus.

7. Clinical correlations: Rh disease and twins

Hemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis)

  • Setting: mother Rh-negative, baby Rh-positive.
  • Fetal RBCs (RBC antigens) reach the mother, usually at delivery, and she makes anti-Rh antibodies (isoimmunization).
  • In a later Rh+ pregnancy, those antibodies are IgG, so they cross the placenta and destroy fetal RBCs. The first baby is usually spared.
  • Prevention: at-risk women receive anti-D immunoglobulin, which clears fetal cells before the mother can sensitise.

[image: Dizygotic twins with separate or fused placentas; monozygotic twins split at stages A, B, C]

Slide 33. Left: dizygotic twins, placentas separate (A) or fused (B), always two chorions. Right: monozygotic split at A (2-cell), B (inner cell mass: common placenta, common chorionic cavity) or C (bilaminar disc: common amniotic cavity).

The logic: the chorion forms from trophoblast (~day 4 to 5), the amnion forms around day 8. Split before a membrane forms and each twin gets its own; split after and they share it. Beyond slides: a split after ~day 13 risks conjoined twins. Days are textbook approximations; the slide only gives the 13-day limit.

8. Dissection lab: a 5-minute walkthrough

Work from outside in, and fetal side before maternal side.

  1. Define it (1 sentence): an ephemeral materno-fetal organ made of villous chorion (fetal) + decidua basalis (maternal); hemochorial, so maternal blood bathes fetal villi.
  2. Membranes: lift the edge. Show the amnion peeling off the smooth chorion (amniochorionic membrane). Explain that the smooth chorion is where villi degenerated (facing the decidua capsularis).
  3. Fetal surface: smooth, shiny, amnion-covered. Point to the cord insertion and the chorionic vessels radiating out.
  4. Umbilical cord: if cut, count the vessels: 2 arteries + 1 vein in Wharton’s jelly. Say which way each carries blood. Mention single umbilical artery and false knots.
  5. Flip to the maternal surface: rough, dark red, lobed. Show cotyledons and the grooves left by placental septa (months 4 to 5). This surface is decidua basalis over villous chorion.
  6. Development in 3 lines: primary (week 2) > secondary (week 3) > tertiary villi (end of week 3); villi cover the whole sac at week 4; only the embryonic pole keeps them by month 2.
  7. Barrier: 4 layers at 10 weeks, 2 layers (syncytiotrophoblast + endothelium) by mid-gestation.
  8. Functions: lungs, kidneys, gut, immune, endocrine (hCG, progesterone, estrogen, hPL).
  9. Close with a question to the group: “Why does IgG cross but IgM not, and why does that matter for Rh disease?”

Likely questions from the group

  • Do maternal and fetal blood mix? No. They are separated by the placental membrane; only small leaks (e.g. at delivery) happen, which is how Rh sensitisation starts.
  • Which vessel carries oxygenated blood? The umbilical vein.
  • Where do the hormones come from? Syncytiotrophoblast.
  • What anchors the placenta? Anchoring villi via the outer cytotrophoblastic shell into the decidua basalis.
  • Can identical twins have two placentas? Yes, if they split in the first ~3 days.
