Exam flags from the lecturer

  • “An important message to retain”: whatever the situation, the centre of the cell always has charge neutrality; any excess of positive or negative charge sits on the inner side of the membrane (section 2). “This is the explanation for any neuron, for any muscular cell.”
  • “An important point”: the potential is created by diffusion; electric forces stop the process. “When you are faced the first time with the membrane potential, you will believe that it is due to electric forces… No” (sections 4 and 5).
  • Goldman equation: “pay attention to the order of outer and inner” (section 7).
  • At rest, permeability order K > Na (and Cl): “only at rest. That’s important because we are going to introduce the action potential.”
  • The action potential will be analysed in detail in the workshop.

1. Electricity in biology: ions, not electrons

  • Recap of the previous session: in biophysics electric current is carried by ions, not electrons. Ions are much heavier, so they are much harder to move through the water of the intracellular and extracellular media.
  • Membrane potentials matter for neurons and the nervous system, and for muscle cells.
  • Model cell: for its electric behaviour, the only relevant part is the membrane, separating intracellular and extracellular media.
  • Ions considered: positive (sodium, potassium), negative (chloride); also large negatively charged molecules (proteins). Calcium is relevant only for muscle.
  • [Lucía] Previous session’s key ideas: electric forces are conservative; field lines from positive charges (source) to negative (sink); superposition principle; electric potential decreases along the field direction.

2. Charge neutrality and shielding

  • One positive ion alone: no forces. Several positive ions: they repel each other and end up on the inner side of the membrane; the centre has zero charge.
  • Positive and negative ions together: each ion feels many small attractive and repulsive forces in all directions; if the numbers of positive and negative charges are equal, the total force on each ion is zero. The forces are always there, but cancelled.
  • So the only way to have ions inside a cell in equilibrium is charge neutrality: total positive balanced by total negative.
  • Shielding: add two extra (unbalanced) charges to a neutral cell. The effect of all the other, balanced charges cancels, so for the extra charges it is as if they were behind a shield and only the two extra charges exist. They repel each other and move to the inner side of the membrane.
  • Result: the centre stays neutral; any excess (positive or negative) accumulates on the inner face of the membrane. Charges move only if there is a net force; at the centre there is none.
  • From here on he does not draw all the ions, but there are always a huge number of positive and negative ions in equal numbers.
  • [Lucía] Equilibrium requires total charge Qt = 0 (resting cell).

3. Impermeable vs selectively permeable membrane

  • Clip 6: an impermeable membrane has no holes: a barrier that prevents ions crossing.
  • Now open a passive hole (no pump, no active mechanism; not the sodium-potassium pump): a discontinuity of the membrane that lets only positive ions cross.
  • In reality pores are selective for an element (potassium, sodium, chloride), but here it is enough that one charge type crosses.
  • Thermal motion: the body is at about 310 K (37 °C). Ions, like water molecules and proteins, are vibrating, colliding and moving randomly. The cause of this movement is thermal energy, not electric forces, which are cancelled.
  • An ion that happens to approach the hole in the right direction escapes to the outside.
  • The probability of crossing depends on temperature (faster vibration) and on the number of ions (more ions, more chances).
  • [Lucía] If the membrane is impermeable to an ion, it bounces back; if permeable, an ion hitting a pore crosses. At 0 K nothing would change.

4. Diffusion creates a charge distribution across the membrane

  • Start: total charge zero inside and outside. After a while, say 5 positive ions are outside.
  • Inside, 5 negative charges (out of perhaps 10^20 ions) are now unbalanced; they move to the inner face of the membrane. Outside, the 5 positive ions end up on the outer face.
  • New situation: negative charges on the inner side, positive on the outer side. Away from the membrane both media stay neutral.
  • The driving force is diffusion, not electrostatic force. Electric forces appear afterwards, because of the charge distribution, and they are what stops the movement: “Actually they will stop moving because of the electric forces.”
  • Once the hole is open, ions outside can also cross inwards: two probabilities, one in each direction. The winner is the side with higher concentration.
  • [Lucía] The concentrations themselves do not change: very few ions cross relative to the total, so no water flow (not osmosis).

5. Equilibrium: the Nernst potential

  • Assume the concentration inside is higher: net ion flux to the outside. Does it continue forever? No.
  • At first (charge neutrality) there is no electric force; only diffusion pushes.
  • After some time, positive charges outside and negative inside: an ion trying to leave is repelled by the outside positive charges and pulled back by the inside negative charges. An electric force pointing inwards appears.
  • While small, diffusion still wins and more charges accumulate; the electric force grows until it balances diffusion. Then crossing stops (net).
  • Electric force means energy can be extracted: there is an energy difference between inside and outside, i.e. a voltage across the membrane. Defined as V = V(inside) - V(outside).
  • In a neuron it can be about 70 mV, large for a membrane: compare the roughly 3 V of a phone battery (a factor of about 50). He mentions it being close to the limit the membrane can withstand [verify].
  • Two kinds of forces: diffusion (depends on number of ions and temperature) and electric (depends on the voltage and the charge of the ion). “There are no more magnitudes.”
  • Balancing them (derivation not given; the logarithm not demonstrated):
    • Nernst potential: the voltage equals k_B T / q (Boltzmann constant times temperature, divided by the ion’s charge) multiplied by the logarithm of the ratio of the concentrations outside and inside. Named after Nernst (transcript “NAPS”). The exact form is on his slide. In the workshop he corrected it: outside concentration on top, inside on the bottom (see FMF Biophysics Action potential scenarios); “log” means natural logarithm.
    • [Lucía] Formula only as an image; she notes that with equal concentrations the log is 0 and the Nernst potential is 0; k_B T/q about 26.7 mV.
  • “Ideal” value: valid only for a membrane permeable to one ion. It is never found in a real cell, because several ions act at once. The time to reach it would also be very long (“two years”; for chemistry, seconds) [verify] meaning.

6. Nernst potentials in a real neuron

  • His table: concentrations inside and outside of sodium, potassium, chloride and other negatively charged molecules (values for a non-human neuron, but close). Positive and negative charges balance inside and outside.
  • The large negative molecules cannot cross passively; only sodium, potassium and chloride have passive pores.
  • Sodium is high outside, potassium high inside: maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, always working.
  • With T = 310 K, k_B, and q = 1.6 x 10^-19 C:
IonNernst potential
Na+about +60 mV
K+about -90 mV
Cl-about -70 mV (transcript once “minus 7”)
  • Student question: should chloride’s be positive? No: its charge q is negative, which reverses the sign.
  • Meaning: a neuron with pores only for sodium would end at +60 mV; only for potassium, at -90 mV.
  • +60 and -90 mV are the limits of what diffusion alone can produce.
  • [Lucía] Same three values.

7. Which ion wins: permeability and the Goldman equation

  • The ion that controls the membrane potential is the one with the most open pores.
  • Permeability (p): the number of open pores for an ion (not probability). Zero when all closed; very large when many open.
  • At rest: p(K) > p(Na), and chloride; sodium and chloride permeabilities are small but not zero. “Only at rest.”
  • So the resting potential is close to K’s -90 mV, but sodium and chloride pull it: about -70 mV.
  • Goldman equation (for a neuron only; “you cannot apply that for muscular cells”):
    • Same prefactor k_B T / q (q now the positive elementary charge, 1.6 x 10^-19 C) times a logarithm of a ratio of sums, each term a permeability multiplied by a concentration (Na, K, Cl).
    • The transcript is garbled; he read the terms as “inside, outside, inside, outside” and then “but here this is outside”, i.e. one term has inside and outside the other way round. Together with “pay attention to the order of outer and inner”, copy the exact formula from his slide. [verify]; [Lucía] has it only as an image.
    • With physiology-textbook permeabilities you get about -70 mV.
  • [Lucía] p(Cl) is so small it can be left out; p(Na) = 0.002 p(K); result -70 mV.

8. Small perturbations vs threshold

  • Diagram: voltage against time, with the Na and K Nernst potentials as limits; the neuron starts at -70 mV, where it is polarized.
  • Input from other neurons can give (A) a small depolarization (e.g. -60 mV) or (B) a hyperpolarization (more negative).
    • A: electric forces are now smaller than at equilibrium, so diffusion wins, pushing potassium out; back to -70 mV.
    • B: electric forces are bigger, ions come in; back to -70 mV.
    • Small perturbations disappear (are damped). He calls it “an unstable situation” in the transcript, but describes a return to rest [verify] wording.
  • Crossing the threshold: many voltage-sensitive sodium channels open suddenly; p(Na) becomes much larger than p(K) and p(Cl); the membrane potential approaches the sodium Nernst potential (+60 mV).
  • Speed depends on permeability: many channels, very fast transition; fewer, slower.
  • Then the sodium channels close and the original situation returns.
  • Summary he gave: (1) a cell at charge neutrality has constant voltage inside and outside; (2) opening a pore for one ion creates a membrane potential, set by diffusion (temperature, concentrations), which is the limit for that ion; (3) with Na, K, Cl all active, the equilibrium depends on which wins: K at rest, Na when the sodium channels open.
  • [Lucía] To switch the membrane potential the cell changes permeability (opens channels); diffusion then drives the change. Close Na gates with high p(K): towards -90 mV; raise p(Na): towards +60 mV.
