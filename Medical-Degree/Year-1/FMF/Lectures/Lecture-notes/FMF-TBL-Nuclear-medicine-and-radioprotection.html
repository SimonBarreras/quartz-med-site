Part 1: Exam-style questions

1. Radiopharmaceuticals and type of radiation

  • A radiopharmaceutical has two components: the radioisotope (produces the radiation) and the pharmaceutical (takes the radioisotope to the organ to explore).
  • The isotope depends on the intention:
IntentionEmission
TreatmentAlpha and beta-minus particles
PET-CTBeta-plus (positrons)
Gammagraphy (gamma camera)Gamma photons
  • Physicist: where the energy is deposited.
    • Alpha and beta-minus: energy delivered very close to where the isotope decayed. The maximum distance a charged particle travels inside the patient is the range.
    • Photons (gamma): the distance before absorption “can go to infinity”: they can cross the whole patient and reach a detector outside. So photons are better for diagnosis.
    • For usual isotopes: alpha range of the order of micrometres; electrons of the order of millimetres (transcript “milliliters”).
    • Particles produced in accelerators, synchrotrons for new-generation treatments have huge energies: even carbon ions (heavier than alpha particles) can have a range of the order of 10 cm. So “charged particles for therapy, photons for diagnostics” is a simplification.
  • [Lucía] The penetration length of charged particles is called the range (PDF p. 17).

2. Theragnosis

  • The same pharmaceutical is labelled with different isotopes: one emits radiation used for diagnosis, the other damages tissue (therapeutic isotope).
  • Aim of combining them: check before treating that we will treat the right thing, before doing any damage to the patient.

3. Renal scintigraphy (Tc-99m MAG3)

  • Question: select the single incorrect answer. Incorrect: B, “it is used exclusively to assess the morphology of the kidneys and bladder”.
  • The renogram can show morphology, but its intention is to assess excretion (obstruction, reflux): whether the kidneys have good vascularization, whether the parenchyma processes the radiopharmaceutical, and whether excretion is appropriate.
  • Analysed with activity-time curves (shown in class), which change with the patient’s excretion.
  • Technetium produces gamma photons, so images are acquired with a gamma camera.
  • [Lucía] Tc-MAG3 and Tc-DTPA; dynamic studies; images over 20 to 30 minutes, one every 2 minutes (PDF p. 43).

4. FDG

  • Question: select the incorrect option. Incorrect: B, “neurons consume lipids”. Neurons consume glucose (about 99%).
  • FDG is the most widely used radiopharmaceutical nowadays, mainly for PET imaging.
  • Tumour cells increase their metabolism: they have more glucose transporters in the membrane, so FDG enters.
  • The patient’s blood glucose influences FDG distribution in cells.

5. Theragnosis: the diagnostic part

  • Identify the incorrect statement. Incorrect: the one saying the purpose is to administer (a treatment; wording garbled) [verify]. Correct statement: C.
  • Purpose of the diagnostic part: know the exact distribution of the radiopharmaceutical; whether the patient is a good candidate for therapy; the exact extent of the disease (sometimes wider than expected).

6. Radiation protection of workers

  • A question on technetium and the gamma camera precedes this one; its discussion is unintelligible [verify].
  • Radiation protection question: correct answer C (described as “probably the longest one”): prevent and optimize exposure (not avoid; minimize), monitor the dose and conduct surveillance.
  • Why the others are wrong:
    • Monitoring alone is not protection: a worker monitored every day who applies no protection measures can exceed the limits.
    • B is wrong because of “avoid”: if we wanted to avoid all exposure, we would have stopped using radiation long ago; we accept receiving radiation for a benefit.
    • Not all workers have yearly health surveillance: only category A; it is an extra measure for the most exposed, not protection itself.

7. Stochastic and deterministic effects; dose quantities

  • Answer: A.
StochasticDeterministic
Meaning”Probabilistic”: we are not sure the effect will appearAppears for sure once a threshold is exceeded
DoseHigher dose, higher probabilityHigher dose, higher severity
SeverityAlways the same (cancer); not related to the doseRelated to the dose (e.g. skin, eye lens)
  • Dial painters: a homogeneous group of women doing the same work, painting dials (transcript “lions for the airplanes”) with radioactive radium paint, studied around the Second World War (as stated) [verify]. Despite the homogeneous exposure, disease appeared in some and not in others: whether the effect appears is a matter of luck.
  • Natural background: you always receive a small dose every day (air, soil, nature).
  • Three doses:
DoseTakes into accountUnit
Absorbed (physical) doseOnly energy per kilogram; what detectors measureGray (transcript “race”; [Lucía] Gy)
Equivalent doseType of radiation (weighting factor WR): e.g. X-rays produce less damage than heavier particles such as protonsSievert
Effective doseTissue type (liver, brain, bone: some tissues much more sensitive)Sievert
  • Equivalent and effective doses try to reflect the biological effect; still energy per kg, but a different unit marks the difference. All reference values (limits) are expressed in sieverts.
  • The effective dose best reflects the probability of damage.
  • [Lucía] stochastic effects have no threshold; deterministic effects (e.g. erythema) appear at higher doses; WR has no units; WT is the tissue weighting factor (PDF p. 18).

8. Range versus exponential attenuation (shielding)

  • Question: the range of monoenergetic electrons is 5 mm; what happens with layers of 5 mm? The aim is to distinguish range (charged particles) from the behaviour of photons.
  • Photons: the number crossing a material decreases exponentially, characterized by a penetration length. Even after three layers some photons may cross.
  • Charged particles: a maximum distance, the range. One layer at the range stops essentially all electrons (range refers to most, not exactly all); with a second layer, “nothing will cross”.
  • Conclusion: charged particles are easy to shield; photons much more difficult; neutrons (garbled) [verify].
  • The graph on the slide was wrong (“I corrected that”); the corrected graph will be in the PDF on ADI.

9. Activity and energy release

  • A source (isotope transcribed “Etium-19” [verify]) with an activity of 2,000 Bq. Becquerel = one decay per second.
  • For an emitter, the energy released per unit of time depends on two factors: activity (decays per second) x energy per decay. The higher either, the higher the energy delivered.

Part 2: Clinical case

10. Presentation

  • A real patient followed step by step (“pay attention because I will ask you something later”).
  • 44-year-old patient (“fairly young”), consulted (department transcribed “diagnostic department”) for constipation and rectal tenesmus for a month and a half. No asthenia, no weight loss, no other symptoms.
  • Colonoscopy (after the usual preparation): a lesion 20 cm from the anal verge (as transcribed); biopsy: rectal adenocarcinoma. “Not so frequent in young people, but could happen.”

11. Justification of the next test (Elena)

  • Question: is a test with radiation justified at this stage?
  • If you request a test with radiation, you are responsible for the justification. First look at the guidelines, check that the case matches, and do the test they recommend: this way justification is always fulfilled. If the guidelines recommend something else (possibly without radiation), follow them.
  • For a really special case (e.g. a young patient), you can do an individual justification: you decide, but think, because you are responsible. Check you have all the information: ask the patient for a CD or report from another hospital.
  • The nuclear medicine physician: usually guidelines are followed, but sometimes a personalized approach is used; you should know exactly what to perform to avoid damage.

12. Staging: which test

  • Aim: know the exact extent of the disease: only the rectal lesion, or other lesions.
  • A student: a plain X-ray might lead to a CT anyway, so go directly to CT. Accepted.
TestDiscussion
UltrasoundNot enough information
X-rayCannot explore all tissues appropriately
EndoscopyNot needed: already done with a successful biopsy
CTFirst test after diagnosis; must cover the whole body or at least thorax, abdomen and pelvis. Guidelines suggest CT first
FDG PET-CTComplementary if there are doubts; more sensitive for small lesions. Some years ago not all hospitals had it; now most do, and in many cases it is done directly to avoid a double CT
  • In this patient: CT first.

13. CT findings and CT physics

  • Axial slices, with a soft tissue window and a lung window.
  • Findings:
    • Rectum: a thick-walled lesion: the adenocarcinoma (bladder, pelvic bone and muscles also visible).
    • Liver: several hypodense lesions: metastases (unexpected).
    • Lungs: two very small nodules. Small lung nodules may be benign; but disease only in the liver versus also in the lungs changes the next step completely. How to check? FDG PET-CT: it shows how active (glucose-consuming) the lesion is, which CT does not.
  • Windows: the Hounsfield scale goes from -1,000 to 1,000 (speaker) [check] [Lucía] -1,000 to +3,000 (PDF p. 28). The eye cannot distinguish so many values, so images are viewed by window: in the lung window the lung parenchyma is visible and soft tissue is saturated white; in the soft tissue window the lungs are completely black and soft tissue structures can be distinguished. [Lucía] the eye distinguishes only 30 to 40 grey values.
  • CT principle (physicist): unlike a plain film (one projection), a source and detector on opposite sides rotate around the patient, giving many projections and sections. The X-rays reaching the detector depend on the path length and the tissue (bone lets fewer through than soft tissue); from the projections the absorption coefficient is recovered.
  • Noisy image: if too few photons reach the detector (below its threshold), the image is noisy. To increase the number of photons, increase the current, not the voltage. The voltage changes the average energy per photon (and so the absorption coefficient), and the noise then behaves in a more complex way.
  • [Lucía] kV is the only variable affecting beam energy; mA (tube current) and mAs increase intensity (number of photons) but not energy (PDF p. 26).

14. FDG PET-CT

Maximum intensity projection (MIP)

  • The image on the left is a PET image: a maximum intensity projection, the functional image (FDG distribution only; the CT is needed for the slices).
  • Radiopharmaceutical: 18F-FDG, recognized by its distribution. FDG is used for most tumours, except those detected through receptors; adenocarcinoma is detected with FDG.
  • Physiological uptake:
    • Brain: completely black: consumes mainly glucose.
    • Heart: may or may not be seen depending on what it is “eating”: on lipids it looks like muscle (light grey); on glucose completely black like the brain; here a mixture. “The brain only consumes glucose, but the heart is a little bit more clever.”
    • Kidneys and bladder: elimination.
  • Pathological uptake:
    • Primary tumour just behind the bladder.
    • Metastatic lymph nodes not evident on CT (normal size; uptake shows tumour involvement).
    • Liver: many more lesions than on CT; almost the whole parenchyma.
    • Sacrum: an uptake judged inflammatory at the time (bone normal on CT). Anecdote: she followed this patient for years, until he died last month (the case was prepared three or four years ago). Years later he had sacral pain he attributed to falling while playing with his children: a bone metastasis at that site. On retrospective review, only the uptake had been there. It responded well to chemotherapy and disappeared.
  • The MIP cannot assess small lung nodules: slices are needed.

Optimization (Elena)

  • Second principle after justification: optimization: the lowest dose that gives an appropriate image. Responsibility of the imaging department (nuclear medicine physicians, radiologists): protocols on the machines, proper kV and mAs for CT.
  • In nuclear medicine the main factor is the administered activity: the patient’s dose is proportional to the activity in the syringe at the start.
  • FDG is dosed by weight (activity per kg, not a fixed dose): patients are weighed on arrival. Different activity for FDG and PSMA.
  • PET-CT is a hybrid technique: both components use ionizing radiation (passage garbled).
  • Narrow range: the minimum activity compatible with a good image; lower dose means lower quality (more noise). Hence written protocols.
  • Anecdote (cyclotron): Pamplona’s hospital has a cyclotron, so FDG is always available. In Madrid she has none: she orders the activity needed each day (a little extra for urgent PET-CTs). If activity falls short (late patients, late transport, complications), and the remaining activity is enough for a quality image, they inject and acquire more slowly; if not, they ask for more FDG or reschedule. Missing a lesion is a big responsibility.

PET physics

  • A positron-emitting radiopharmaceutical; the positron meets an electron; annihilation gives two photons of 511 keV, detected by the ring of the PET-CT. “The PET detects photons, not positrons.”
  • The positron is antimatter: it does not remain long; it attracts its antiparticle (the electron) and they annihilate; only energy remains as two photons with a well-defined energy, which makes detection easy.
  • Drawback (physicist): the positron is emitted where the radiopharmaceutical is, but annihilates a small distance away (of the order of some millimetres), and the PET locates the annihilation. Result: a “cloud” instead of a point, blurring. Techniques now try to recover the emission point.
  • So spatial resolution of PET is worse than CT (CT can go below a millimetre; impossible for PET). Borders are not sharp, but the contrast of glucose-consuming cells against surrounding tissue is very high, and that is the information wanted.
  • [Lucía] annihilation produces two 511 keV photons in opposite directions (PDF p. 15).
  • Tumour cells have increased glucose metabolism: more glucose transporters and glycolytic enzymes, hence increased FDG uptake.

Slices

  • Axial, sagittal and coronal slices and PET-CT fusion.
  • Lung nodules: visible on CT but no FDG uptake: not metastatic, and they stayed negative throughout the disease.
  • Primary tumour with very high uptake; all the liver metastases visible.
  • Question: what does FDG emit? A student said gamma photons; answer: beta-plus (positrons): PET = positron emission tomography; gamma photons are for the gamma camera.

15. Treatment

Chemotherapy and response

  • Multidisciplinary tumour board: given the extent, systemic treatment (chemotherapy) for the rectal tumour and the metastases.
  • Response assessment CT: hypodense liver lesions reduced (good morphological response; not always the case, hence the response CT). Rectum: very good response. Metastatic lymph nodes: complete response. Liver lesions remain.

Liver surgery?

  • A student: surgery, since the liver can regenerate. Right, but it needs time, and a sufficient volume of liver is needed to keep liver function.
  • The disease involved the whole right hepatic lobe: a right hepatectomy was needed, but the left liver was too small to keep liver function.
  • Needed: a treatment that controls the disease and damages some normal right liver so the left (remnant) liver grows.

Hepatic radioembolization

  • A nuclear medicine procedure (“maybe we didn’t explain widely in the class”): administration of radioactive microspheres through the hepatic artery or one of its branches.
  • Rationale: hepatic tumours (HCC, cholangiocarcinoma, both primary, or metastases) are mainly nourished by the hepatic artery; normal liver parenchyma mainly by the portal vein. Not black and white, but mostly. So intra-arterial microspheres treat mainly the tumours and a little normal liver.
  • Procedure: in the operating theatre, an interventional radiologist inserts a catheter from the femoral artery to the common hepatic artery or a branch, toward the area to treat; microspheres loaded with yttrium-90 (transcript “E290”), a beta-minus emitter, intended to destroy tumours.
  • Risk: if the catheter is badly positioned, microspheres can flow to other organs: lungs, duodenum, stomach: severe damage where they deposit (beta-minus destroys tissue).
  • So the treatment is first mimicked with a diagnostic radiopharmaceutical: technetium-99m MAA (also microspheres), through the same artery. Two arteriographies: first the diagnostic, then the treatment. Tc produces gamma photons, so the planning study is imaged by SPECT (not PET).
  • Arterial anatomy varies a lot between people (“many, many variants. You cannot imagine”): the CT before treatment may guess territories, but the arteriography can bring surprises, so it is planned for each patient. The interventional radiologist draws the arterial tree and the distribution of disease to choose the artery.

Physicist: delivering radiation to a tumour

  • If surgery is not possible, radiation can kill tumours. How to deliver energy only to the tumour?
    • External radiotherapy (with systems that protect a lot).
    • Introducing radioactive material inside the patient: e.g. small radioactive seeds placed through needles in organs such as the prostate (via a catheter, left for some time and removed); or radioembolization.
  • Why the spheres stop: at each bifurcation the branches have a smaller diameter. The spheres are small but larger than a capillary, so they get jammed along the vessels; the hope is that they stop inside the tumour. Hence careful planning: release them as close to the tumour as possible (released e.g. in the heart, they would travel all around the body).
  • Yttrium-90 decay: 99.99% of decays are beta-minus (therapy). A tiny fraction goes through a chain via zirconium (transcript “sycronium”) that can emit a very high-energy photon which spontaneously splits into an electron-positron pair: so a few positrons are produced, which allows imaging where the dose was deposited.
    • Fraction: physicist “0.1%, 0.01%”; physician “one every two million… more or less” [check] (speakers differ; no source settles it).
  • In nuclear medicine the ideal isotope has a high-probability therapeutic branch plus some gamma or positron emission for post-treatment imaging. In external radiotherapy you program the radiation and know its track; in nuclear medicine you trust the patient’s physiology to move the radiopharmaceutical (in radioembolization, the catheter delivers it), so post-treatment imaging is valued.
  • Hemodynamics: predicting what fraction of particles goes into each branch at a bifurcation is complex (fluid behaviour); particles move with random “jumps” and the result resembles a Gaussian distribution (statistics next year). Hemodynamics of the systemic and pulmonary circulation will be studied next course; interventional radiologists need these parameters.

SPECT imaging of the MAA

  • After the planning arteriography, the patient goes to nuclear medicine for imaging of the MAA distribution.
  • Gamma camera: two big square detectors, one above and one below the bed, designed for planar images; rotating it gives many projections, and reconstruction (similar to CT) gives a 3D image (SPECT). Inside the head: a scintillation detector (transcript “simulation”) and photomultiplier tubes: similar components to PET, different geometry.
  • Images: SPECT alone (black and white), CT, and SPECT-CT fusion in a colour scale, showing MAA distribution in the liver.
  • Two intentions: high activity to the tumours, and some to the normal right liver (purple/grey on the colour scale); none to the left liver (the future remnant). Absorbed doses to tumours and normal liver are calculated. The progressive radiation damage lowers right liver function and the left liver responds with contralateral hypertrophy.

Radiation protection in the procedure (Elena)

  • Y-90 in a plastic box: no lead needed because the radiation is mainly beta, absorbed by very little material; staff are protected while the Y-90 is in the box, more exposed when it goes into the patient.
  • X-rays are used at the same time (screen showing contrast in the liver; the X-ray tube head above the patient): staff wear an individual lead apron under the sterile gown; interventional radiologists also wear protective glasses; she wears them too.
  • Contamination: the microspheres are liquid in a vial: double gloves, so contaminated outer gloves can be removed and work continues.
  • The physician mentions a new, “fancy” administration box in Madrid.

Post-treatment PET and outcome

  • About one in every two million decays emits a positron (physician’s figure; see the [check] above), so post-treatment PET is possible: uptake only in the right liver, none in the left, as intended.
  • Nuclear medicine physicians and radiophysicists calculate the absorbed dose to tumour and normal liver before (simulation) and after (real distribution) treatment; this predicts the outcome (very low absorbed doses: the patient may progress or not respond).
  • Outcome: complete response of the lesions; the left liver (blue) grew a lot and the right (pink) shrank.
    • A successful right hepatectomy needs a remnant of more than 35 to 40% of the liver.
    • Left liver before treatment: 14% (insufficient); after: 44.6%.
    • The right hepatectomy (the surgery suggested at the start of the case) was then performed, with the enlarged left liver keeping liver function.
