Exam flags from the lecturer

  • The three principles (justification, optimization, dose limitation) are “the main umbrella over any application with radiation”. For patients, dose limitation does not apply (section 7).
  • “That’s the main message”: ionizing radiation is useful in many applications, but we must work with it carefully.
  • Training in radiation protection is compulsory for every student and hospital worker who may be involved; that is why these two sessions are in first year.
  • “Justification is dynamic”: what is justified today may not be tomorrow, so stay updated with guidelines.

1. Context and regulation

  • Many doctors are exposed: radiologists, but also cardiologists, neurologists, and surgeons who use X-rays to check during surgery.
  • Occupational exposure: radiation received because you are doing your job.
  • Main Spanish regulation: published in 2022 (with roots about 20 years earlier and updates) on the protection of health against the risks derived from ionizing radiation. [verify] exact name and number. It applies to all uses of ionizing radiation, not only medical.
  • It divides the population into workers and public, and requires both to prevent exposure as far as possible and to monitor it.

2. Types of risk

  • Irradiation (external exposure): an external source (photons, particles, gamma rays, X-rays) irradiates the body from outside.
  • Contamination: working with unsealed radioactive material (e.g. liquid radiopharmaceuticals in nuclear medicine) that can be spread and get on or into you.
  • Identify which risks you are exposed to before choosing measures.

3. Protecting against irradiation: distance, time, shielding

  • Distance: dose falls with the square of the distance (inverse square law). Source at 1 m gives, say, 100; at 2 m: 25 (factor 4); at 3 m: about 11 (factor 9). “Really easy and really efficient.”
  • Time: dose is proportional to exposure time. A surgeon controlling the X-rays (often by a foot pedal) should use the minimum compatible with what is needed, for themselves, the patient and the staff. If you are not needed in the room, leave: your dose is zero.
  • Shielding: choose the material for the type of radiation. For X-rays, lead (efficient but heavy): lead aprons and screens. They are less effective for other radiation (e.g. in nuclear medicine). TV series show lead aprons; people forget distance and time, which are very efficient too.

4. Protecting against contamination

  • Distance, time and shielding are not useful: if your hand is contaminated, you carry the source with you.
  • Use work clothes and gloves: they do not prevent contamination but make it easy to remove (take them off, clean yourself before leaving).
  • Measure: detectors to check work surfaces, hands and even feet before leaving any area with contamination risk. Awareness is the best way to stop the spread.
  • Student question: can there be contamination risk without irradiation risk? Yes, rarely: isotopes of very low energy that cannot even penetrate gloves or clothing (laboratories handling very low activity). They are the hardest to detect because detectors are less sensitive to them.

5. Signalling of areas

  • International radiation trefoil symbol on the doors of areas with risk; the colour indicates the level of risk:
ColourMeaning (as given)
GreyThe lowest level: a person working there full time all year could exceed the limit for the public (so not everyone may work there)
GreenUp to 6 mSv per year (he says about “two-thirds of the limit of the worker” [verify]; 6 is less than a third of 20)
YellowWorking there all day for a year (about 2,000 hours) could exceed the limit for exposed workers: more care, limited stays (no desks; enter to do the job and leave)
  • Background of the sign: plain white = irradiation risk only; dots = contamination risk; both can be present.
  • Operating room example: sign on the door, lights that flash when radiation is on, and a shielded door.

6. Workers: classification and monitoring

Classification

  • Exposed vs non-exposed workers: those with any probability of being exposed at some time.
  • Exposed workers: category A (likely to receive about 6 mSv per year or more: higher control) and category B.
  • His hospital: transcript “we are like 200… 400 exposed… 40 category A”; earlier “more than 2,000 people”. [verify] numbers.
  • Category A in a hospital: interventional radiology (complex procedures with high X-ray use, staff in the room; transcript “angiography or a mathematician” [verify]); people working with sealed sources in therapy (transcript “write therapy” [verify], presumably brachytherapy); staff preparing radiopharmaceuticals in the PET department and PET technicians.

Training and procedures

  • Training and information (compulsory; refreshed every 2 to 4 years) and written procedures.
  • Example: mobile X-ray units (low power, usable anywhere, e.g. ICU): when the button is pressed only the patient and the operator stay; everyone else at least 3 m away; lead aprons.
  • So five preventive measures: distance, time, shielding (plus contamination measures), training, procedures.

Monitoring

  • Workplace monitoring: fixed detectors with alarms (e.g. lights, door locked if radiation too high), portable real-time detectors (like a Geiger counter), passive dosimeters placed around rooms. Example CT room map: outside readings at natural background, showing the shielding works; some high-intensity X-ray rooms read 2 to 3 mSv per month [verify] units.
  • Personal dosimeter (compulsory for exposed workers): does not protect and has no display or alarm; it records the accumulated dose, changed and read monthly; annual totals checked against limits and used to improve procedures. Records kept essentially forever.
  • Pregnant workers: the worker may be exposed, but the fetus is a member of the public: on (voluntary) declaration of pregnancy the limit changes from 20 to 1 mSv; an extra abdominal dosimeter estimates fetal dose, plus measures to avoid any dose.
  • Category A: yearly medical examination by the occupational health doctor, who receives their doses and declares them fit or not.
  • Limits mentioned: public 1 mSv/year, workers 20 mSv/year.

7. Patients: justification and optimization

  • For patients, no dose limitation (forget the 1 and 20 mSv): so special care with the other two principles.

Justification

  • European regulation: all medical exposures shall be justified in advance: balance potential risk against benefit; only if justified is the test done.
  • Risk is harder for a clinician to judge than benefit.
  • First level: follow guidelines from scientific societies (someone has already done the justification); stay updated.
  • He uses a Spanish Society of Radiology list of about 100 things not to do (the opposite of a recommendation list, with justification and references). Examples:
    • Trauma (e.g. car accident): do not do plain radiography; go straight to CT (higher dose, but plain films lose time and often do not give the information).
    • No routine preoperative chest X-ray (in the pre-anaesthesia work-up) unless there is a specific reason.
  • Student question (emergencies): in his hospital there is no CT dedicated to the emergency department, so the full radiology department is available; may differ in big hospitals. Contrast CT dose depends on parameters (kV, mAs) [verify].
  • Individual justification: outside the guidelines, for this patient today. The requesting doctor is responsible, and it must be recorded in the patient’s record. Example: an X-ray in a pregnant patient with a serious, life-threatening problem; the patient must be informed and agree. Responsibility is shared between requester and radiologist; if they disagree, the radiologist decides.
  • Tools to understand risk: a Spanish leaflet used at the Clínica translating each test into mSv, equivalent chest X-rays and days of natural background:
    • chest X-ray: about 0.02 mSv, about 3 days of background;
    • skull X-ray: about 3 chest X-rays; spine films more (bone needs more radiation): months of background;
    • CT: hundreds of radiographs, years of background.
    • (Not a dose limit, just a way to grasp the risk.)
  • Gather previous information: past tests elsewhere, CDs of images; avoid repeats.
  • Questions for individual justification: Has the patient already had it? Do I need it (will it change management, or am I just reassuring myself; e.g. suspected pneumonia: start antibiotics, re-evaluate in 1 to 2 weeks)? Do I need it now? Am I ordering too many tests at once instead of step by step? Have I given radiology all the information (they may suggest a better test)? Is there an alternative without radiation? (“I am not a physician”, so the clinical examples are only illustrative.)

Optimization

  • Mainly in the hands of radiologists, nuclear medicine physicians and radiotherapy staff: do everything with as low a dose as possible.
  • Hard to know if you used the minimum: good images make everyone happy regardless.
  • Main tool: diagnostic reference levels (DRL). Built from surveys of hospitals in a region (Spain uses European surveys): each reports its dose for a test (e.g. chest CT); the 75th percentile of the distribution is the reference level (75% of hospitals are below it). Published by the European Commission.
  • Each year, device by device, a representative sample of patients’ doses is compared with the DRL. Below: acceptable (still improvable). Above: investigate with physicists, radiologists and technicians (too many series, too high kV or mAs…).

8. Effects to avoid

  • Justification and optimization aim to minimize the probability of stochastic effects, assuming that deterministic effects are never produced.
  • Deterministic effects in patients from X-rays have been published in the past; machines and procedures have improved, but if a very hard case used a lot of radiation, the patient should be followed up for such effects.
  • [Lucía] Deterministic: death of many cells; threshold dose; severity increases with dose; after high doses. Stochastic: sublethal DNA damage in one or few cells (germ cells: hereditary disorders; somatic: cancer); no threshold; probability (not severity) increases with dose; special caution in children and women of child-bearing age. Units: absorbed dose Gy; equivalent and effective dose Sv.
