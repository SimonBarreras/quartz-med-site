Exam flags from the lecturer

  • “What I want you to understand”: PET needs a positron that annihilates, producing two photons detected one in front of the other; without both signals there is no image (section 1).
  • “I want to call your attention on something”: function changes before anatomy; PET detects disease before anatomical change (section 3).
  • Nuclear medicine physicians must know the normal (physiological) distribution of each radiopharmaceutical, or they will call normal uptake pathological: “something that is very important” (section 2).
  • Patient preparation for FDG PET (section 5): fasting, glucose control, no exercise, warmth, drugs; PET-CT is scheduled, not an emergency test.

1. How PET detects positrons

  • PET = positron emission tomography, using radiopharmaceuticals that emit beta-plus particles (positrons). (Previous class: gamma emitters for gamma camera and SPECT.)
  • The PET ring does not detect positrons: a positron leaving the nucleus annihilates with a nearby electron within about 2 mm, producing two high-energy photons in opposite directions. (She refers to Javier’s biophysics explanation.)
  • The ring of detectors registers only pairs detected one in front of the other (in opposite detectors). Without both signals, no image.
  • Difference from the gamma camera: the gamma camera’s two detectors register whatever gamma rays reach them; PET requires the annihilation and the paired detection.
  • PET-CT: CT image first, then PET acquisition; the patient is the source; result: PET (function only), CT (anatomy), and the fused (hybrid) image with the PET in colour over the CT. The PET alone cannot clearly separate structures; the CT shows exactly where the uptake is.
  • [Lucía] Annihilation gives two 511 keV photons; PET needs attenuation correction and quantification (SUV); attenuation artefacts must be corrected in reconstruction.

  • Heart and endocarditis: to see infection in the heart, its glucose uptake must be suppressed: a diet full of lipids beforehand switches the heart from glycolytic to lipid metabolism, so cardiac uptake becomes very low and the infection becomes visible.
  • Normal adrenal uptake must not be called pathological: know the physiological distribution.

3. Function changes before anatomy

  • Before an organ’s anatomy changes, its function changes. E.g. in Alzheimer’s, before MRI shows atrophy of the temporal lobes, neurons die and function falls, visible on PET.
  • Oncology: most oncology patients in Spain get a PET (many PET-CTs in Spanish hospitals). A lymph node of normal size on CT but with increased uptake is pathological (tumour).

4. FDG: mechanism and uses

  • Tumour cells have more glucose transporters and more glycolytic enzymes. FDG enters through the transporters and is trapped inside the cell; she says it is “not detected by the hexokinase” [verify] ([Lucía]: the glycolytic enzymes incorporate FDG and it is retained). After the fluorine-18 decays, the molecule is processed by the enzymes and eliminated.
  • Uses of FDG PET:
    • staging tumours;
    • assessing treatment response early (uptake falls before CT changes);
    • detecting recurrence;
    • guiding biopsy to the most active part of a tumour (a big tumour may have a necrotic centre that gives no useful information);
    • caution: uptake is also increased in many benign conditions.
  • Dementia: normal brain FDG is high; frontotemporal dementia: reduced frontal and temporal uptake; Alzheimer’s: e.g. reduced parietal uptake. The pattern indicates the type of dementia.
  • [Lucía] FDG measures glucose metabolism, an indirect measure of proliferation; GLUT-1 and hexokinase increased (HIF-1, anaerobic conditions). Very well-differentiated tumours can have very low uptake (false negatives).

5. Patient preparation for FDG PET

  • PET-CT is scheduled and prepared, not an emergency test: normal cells should take up FDG normally and tumour cells more, so they can be distinguished.

  • Glucose limits: Spain is stricter; in the USA the limit is 200 to perform a PET-CT. She used to give rapid insulin and wait (to avoid muscle uptake); now she injects FDG with levels around 150 and images are usually acceptable.
  • Uncontrollable cases: a glottic tumour patient coughing constantly showed intense neck muscle uptake, anatomically normal muscles on CT.
  • [check] [Lucía]‘s preparation table: “type II diabetic: 6 h fasting + metformin”; “type I diabetic: breakfast + insulin”. This year’s lecturer: stop metformin two days before; no insulin on the day, first appointment, low-carb diet. Lucía also lists hydration the day before and on the day.

6. Timing and working with radioactive patients

  • Half-lives: FDG (F-18) 109 minutes; Tc-99m 6 hours (“we are very calm” with technetium). Most PET radiopharmaceuticals are around 100 minutes.
  • Wait about one hour after injection for incorporation, then image (at 60 minutes; transcript “eight minutes after… but not three hours after” [verify], probably 80 minutes). Too late: decay gives poor images.
  • Hence “people running through the corridors”: schedules must be tight; if one patient’s glucose must be lowered, another patient takes their slot so activity is not wasted.
  • Staff exposure: personal dosimeter and ring dosimeters (they record but do not alarm), so protection relies on shielding: the radiopharmaceutical inside a shield, injected through a line without touching it; a clock records the exact injection time.

7. Recognizing variants

  • Quiz images: a pelvic kidney and a horseshoe kidney (both kidneys joined): normal function, abnormal position; not pathological. The truly abnormal finding was a lung nodule with high uptake on the axial CT slices: lung cancer, the reason for the study.

8. Acquisition protocols (recording ends)

  • What is imaged depends on the question: for e.g. ovarian cancer, not whole body (no metastases expected in the feet). Whole-body protocols for diseases that can involve any bone or the skin: melanoma, multiple myeloma (recording ends here).
  • [Lucía] Protocols: head and neck (arms down), body (arms up), whole body, base of skull to mid-thigh. SUV (standardized uptake value) for semiquantitative analysis. Examples: lung cancer nodes of normal size with uptake; tuberculosis; neck and oesophageal cancer.
