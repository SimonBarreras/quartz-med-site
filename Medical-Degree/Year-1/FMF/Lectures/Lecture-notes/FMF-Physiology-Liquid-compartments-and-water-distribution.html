1. Introduction

  • [Slides] Opening quote: “What Medicine really is: hate the sickness and love the sick”, Jérôme Lejeune, 1961. The lecturer: a doctor who discovered the cause of Down syndrome; “an amazing life, which has nothing to do with water”; read about him if you have time.
  • [Slides] Part 2 quote: “Water is the driving force of all nature”, Leonardo da Vinci.
  • Plan: the body’s compartments, all the water (liquid) in the body, the composition of the compartments, how water moves.

2. Body water

  • We are mainly made of water: roughly 60% of the body. It changes through life: as we get older, the proportion of water decreases ([Slides] “Body water proportion”: see the slide).
  • Intake and output: the largest intake and output of the body is water, not proteins or fats. We always need intake to compensate output, even when fasting.
  • The amounts depend on lifestyle: a sedentary person has less turnover than someone exercising. ([Slides] “Daily intake and output of water”: numbers on the slide, “just to get an idea”.)

3. Compartments

CompartmentLecturer[Slides]
Intracellular fluid (ICF)Roughly two thirds of body water28 L
Extracellular fluid (ECF)Roughly one third14 L: interstitial 11 L, plasma 3 L
Transcellular fluidThird compartmentAlso called third space
  • Blood is both: plasma (ECF) plus the blood cells, which contain intracellular fluid. [Slides] “Blood volume”.
  • Layout: the cell membrane surrounds the ICF; then the interstitial fluid; then the capillary membrane, which separates blood from the rest.
  • Changes of input and output occur mainly at plasma level. They translate into changes of the interstitial fluid (all ECF), which then create changes in the ICF.

Transcellular fluid (third space)

  • Neither intracellular nor extracellular. [Slides] not connected to the bloodstream; important in pathological conditions.
  • Normal examples: fluid inside the eye; fluid in the joints (cracking your joints moves liquid from one side of the joint to the other); urine in the bladder.
  • Normally not taken into account. A change in its volume usually means a pathological condition.
  • Clinical example, pleural effusion: between the parietal and visceral pleura there is normally nothing (a virtual space: present but empty). Liquid there is a pleural effusion, which is transcellular fluid.

4. Units ([Slides] “Physical principles”)

  • [Slides] Mole = 6.022 x 10^23; molecular weight = sum of atomic weights (e.g. H2O = 2 x 1 + 16 = 18 g/mol).
  • Molarity = moles per litre of solution; molality = moles per kg of solvent. In the body the solvent is water and 1 L of water = 1 kg, so molarity and molality are the same.
  • Osmolarity = osmoles per litre. What matters for us is osmolarity: it relates to the osmotic power (pressure) of a compound, the basis of fluid exchange. Each dissolved particle is one osmole: 1 mole of NaCl gives two particles (Na+ and Cl-, ions, no longer a molecule), each one osmole.
  • In the body we use milli-units (mOsm, mEq).
  • Milliequivalents = millimoles x ionic charge: about charge, not number of particles.

  • Two equilibria across a membrane: of osmoles and of charges (anions and cations must be even on both sides). On the composition chart: up and down = osmolarity; left and right = equivalents (garbled) [verify].
  • Osmotic pressure and molecular weight: the lecturer said osmotic pressure “will depend on the molecular weight”, “the heavier the compound… the more osmotic power”, and used it to explain why proteins matter for osmotic pressure (section 5).   • Electrolyte = charged; non-electrolyte = not charged.

  • Calcium: in a garbled passage the lecturer seems to list calcium with the intracellular ions; [Slides] put Ca2+ in the ECF [check].
  • Bicarbonate: one of the most important pH buffers. Depending on bicarbonate there is acidosis or alkalosis, measured on plasma pH:

    • Measured via pH and bicarbonate.
    • The lecturer: bicarbonate “is divided into water and CO2”; CO2 is an acid and is easily eliminated each time you breathe.
  • Proteins: not ions as such, but in the body they are uncharged or negatively charged, so they appear among the anions. Very important for osmotic pressure (very large molecules). Mainly intracellular, but there are also many in the plasma and very few in the interstitium.
  • Sodium: ECF sodium compared with ICF sodium is about 10 times more (garbled: intracellular sodium “14”) [verify].
  • Clinical relevance: the ions are the first thing analysed in a blood sample: e.g. hypernatraemia = excess sodium, hyponatraemia = low sodium; “the same thing with potassium”.

Plasma versus interstitial fluid: the Donnan effect

  • Ions move freely across the capillary wall, so interstitium and plasma should be evenly distributed ([Slides] “Interstitial and plasma are similar”).
  • But proteins are very large and do not cross the capillary membrane, so they stay mainly in the plasma; they “attract” more ions. Result: a slight difference between interstitial fluid and plasma: the Donnan effect (related to protein concentration).
  • You never measure interstitial concentrations; you measure plasma, every day.

6. Diffusion versus transport

  • ICF and ECF are separated by the lipid bilayer, which does not let things travel freely: they need channels.
DiffusionTransport
MechanismPassiveActive: requires energy (ATP); a change in the shape of the protein
ExampleAquaporins (water)Na+/K+ pump
  • Aquaporins: roughly holes in the bilayer that let water travel freely; not ions, not proteins (these need specific channels). Important for osmotic pressure.
  • Na+/K+ pump ([Slides]): 2 globular proteins (alpha and beta); 3 binding sites for Na+; 2 for K+; ATPase activity. Net effects: maintains Na+ and K+ concentrations, maintains cellular volume, creates a negative electrical voltage (basis of nerve function). The lecturer: the pumps maintain the difference between extracellular and intracellular composition. [Lucía] the pump extracts 3 Na+ for every 2 K+ it introduces; always working.
  • Anecdote (the “NA-K” car): his physiology teacher (physiology was in second year then; the teacher has since passed away) kept a very old Talbot because its Navarra licence plate read NA-K, like the sodium-potassium pump. When the car finally died, that was the end of the “sodium-potassium” (passage partly garbled).
  • Clinical consideration: [Slides] activators of the pump: insulin; inhibitors: digoxin (foxglove plant).
    • The lecturer: in the heart, the contractile myocardial cells depend on calcium; the cardiac action potential is based on calcium, unlike other action potentials (mainly sodium and potassium).
    • Digoxin blocks the Na+/K+ pump; through the sodium-calcium exchanger this gives more intracellular calcium. Pumps are linked: acting on one has an effect on the other.

7. Compartmental separation

  • [Slides] ICF and ECF are theoretically isolated by the bilayer membrane; water diffuses through aquaporins.
  • Water diffuses freely and moves to equalize osmotic pressure (and charge).
  • As a doctor you can only act on the ECF, in fact only on the plasma, to create a change in the other compartments. The first thing that happens is water movement.
  • Plasma to interstitium: water and ions move freely; proteins do not, unless smaller than the pores of the capillary membrane: 6 to 7 nm ([Slides] pores 6-7 nm; “only proteins can’t travel through endothelium”; in part 2 “up to 7 nm (smaller than albumin)”).
  • Two exceptions:
LiverBrain
Capillary wallFenestrations (“windows”): huge holes connecting plasma and interstitiumBlood-brain barrier: very tight junctions
WhyThe liver cleans the plasma of all kinds of compounds, so it must extract large compounds freelyThe brain must be completely isolated and protected
ConsequenceVery little movement except via specific channels. Clinical: how much of a drug crosses the BBB determines how many neurological side effects it has

8. Osmosis and osmotic pressure

  • [Slides] Osmolarity = osmoles of solute particles per unit volume; a semipermeable membrane implies osmosis (osmolarities tend to be even); osmosis is the water exchange.
  • Osmotic pressure (pi) = the force that pushes water from one side to the other (or gathers it). Water moves freely to keep osmotic pressure even on both sides.
  • Osmotic vs oncotic pressure: osmotic pressure takes into account all the osmolarity of the solution (plasma, interstitial or transcellular); oncotic pressure is the part created by the proteins ([Slides] colloidosmotic or oncotic pressure). Slightly different concepts, but in the body “we won’t be separating both of them”.
  • Four factors of osmotic pressure ([Slides]): temperature, solute concentration, number of particles (n), Staverman’s coefficient (sigma).
    • Temperature is not a problem in us: fairly constant, and fever affects both sides of the membrane equally.
    • Concentration x number of particles = osmolarity. So osmotic pressure depends on osmolarity and Staverman’s coefficient.
  • Staverman’s reflection coefficient: [Slides] a unitless constant specific to the permeability of a membrane to a given solute; from 0 to 1 (1 = cannot cross the membrane; 0 = travels freely). Different for sodium, chloride, bicarbonate, and proteins (which normally do not cross).
  • [Slides] ECF and ICF have the same osmolarity, despite different compositions; water flowing freely allows this.

The U-tube experiment

  • U-shaped tube with water on both sides and a membrane that lets water but not the solute through. Add glucose (C6H12O6) to one side: higher osmolarity there, so water moves from the lower to the higher osmolarity side.
  • The resulting difference in height between the two sides is the pressure.

mmHgBig pressures: arterial blood pressure (e.g. 120/80 mmHg = normal: systolic 120, diastolic less). Historically a column of mercury: how much mercury is needed to collapse the artery

9. Aquaporins

  • [Slides] 13 different proteins, AQ0 to AQ12 (the lecturer first said 14, then corrected to 13); 6 membrane-spanning alpha-helical domains, with both carboxyl and amino terminals on the cytoplasmic side; in groups of 4; in the lipid bilayer; let water flow freely. They work by diffusion.
  • Clinical importance: diabetes insipidus.
    • Two types of diabetes: mellitus (“tastes like honey”) and insipidus (“no taste”), named after the taste of urine. Both were first described by the symptom of constant urination (“10, 15, 20 litres a day”), the first symptom of diabetes.
    • Mellitus is about sugar. Insipidus is a mutation in one aquaporin, AQ2, causing problems in the kidney: water cannot be reabsorbed. In the kidney water normally moves back and forth across cells, which lets it excrete urea and other substances; with the mutation, water goes out and never comes back.
    • [Slides] mutations in the AQ2 gene cause hereditary nephrogenic diabetes insipidus; up to 20 L of urine per day; unable to concentrate urine (no response to vasopressin).

10. Plasma osmolarity

  • Very important to be “normal”, and it drives IV fluid therapy: you need to know the patient’s osmolarity and the effect of the fluid you give.
  • Formula (has to be known and calculated) ([Slides]):
    • Plasma osmolarity = 2 x [Na+] + [glucose]/18 + [BUN]/2.8
    • [Na+] x 2 because sodium goes with Cl- or HCO3- (as NaCl or NaHCO3). Glucose molecular weight 180 and BUN (blood urea nitrogen) 28, each divided by 10 because glucose and BUN are in mg/dL while osmolarity is mOsm/L.
    • The lecturer read the formula aloud as “the concentration of sodium plus…” without the factor 2; the slide has x2 [check].
  • Normal value: transcribed “295 to 295” mOsm per litre [verify] (the range is not in the slide text).
  • The lab gives you the number; you still need to know how it is calculated.
  • Main determinant is Na+: e.g. sodium 145 weighs far more than glucose/18 or BUN/2.8. [Slides] urea’s Staverman’s coefficient is fairly low (so it diffuses); when Na+ goes up, plasma osmolarity goes up and water moves ICF to ECF.
  • Sodium diffuses easily, but the Na+/K+ pump compensates and keeps sodium mainly extracellular and potassium intracellular.

11. Changes in plasma osmolarity

Low plasma osmolarityHigh plasma osmolarity
Water movesECF to ICFICF to ECF
CellsSwellShrink (“crenation”, seen e.g. in red blood cells)
Main dangerThe brain[Slides] in the brain, increased blood volume and potentially haemorrhage
  • Brain in low osmolarity: the brain is in a closed cavity and cannot increase its volume. Swelling elsewhere matters less. If brain cells swell, pressure rises on the vessels entering the skull; vessels collapse, blood does not reach the brain: neurological consequences. Lecturer: headache, seizures, loss of consciousness, even death. [Slides] headache, vomiting, obnubilation, seizures, coma (and even death).
  • Causes:
    • Low osmolarity: the lecturer said it is “not that often” (a large intake of water without ions while the kidney keeps working; passage garbled). [check] [Slides] the most typical cause is hyponatraemia, “the most common electrolyte alteration in inpatients”.
    • High osmolarity: dehydration; too much salt intake (the easiest way).
  • Why hypertensive patients must avoid salt: salt raises plasma osmolarity; water moves from the intracellular to the extracellular compartment; blood volume increases, which increases blood pressure. You cannot “control” blood volume when taking too much salt, because water compensates.

12. IV fluid therapy

  • [Slides] IV fluid therapy is basic; the most common inpatient therapy; knowledge of osmosis is crucial; it can potentially be lethal; euvolaemia is key.
  • The lecturer: the first thing done for almost every patient, either to correct volume or simply to have an IV access (a line to give drugs).
  • Volaemia = the amount of blood in the vessels; euvolaemia = normal volume. Hypovolaemia: e.g. dehydration, bleeding patients.
  • Bleeding patients do not die from lack of red blood cells straight away (they can live with anaemia, less haemoglobin); to survive, volume is the most important thing: with low volume “everything starts failing”.
  • [Slides] “A typical protocol”: see the slide (transcript garbled).
  • Percentages: fluids and drugs in bags are expressed as percentages: 1% = 10 g/L. Example: lidocaine (a local anaesthetic) at 1% or 2%. Normal saline 0.9% NaCl = 9 g/L.
  • Normal saline calculation ([Slides]; Spanish “suero fisiológico”; the most widely used fluid therapy): NaCl molecular weight 58 g/mol; 9 g/L = 0.155 mol/L = 155 mmol/L; pi = [Na] x n x sigma = 155 x 1.87 x 1 = 290 mOsm/L (the asterisk on 1.87 is explained on the slide: see the slide). So it does not change plasma osmolarity.

Glucose (dextrose)

  • Glucose is essential (the brain works with glucose), but it is not normally given alone straight away.
  • 5% glucose (usually named dextrose): [Slides] 50 g/L, molecular weight 180 g/mol, = 0.278 mol/L = 278 mmol/L: theoretically isotonic (that is why 5% is used).
  • But glucose is transformed into H2O + CO2; CO2 is eliminated by breathing, so the patient effectively gets water: a hypotonic solution.
  • Therefore glucose is given as glucose-saline solutions, to provide glucose plus the ions and avoid a hypotonic effect.

13. Microcirculation (introduction; to be continued with the cardiovascular system)

  • Capillaries: a very small section each but a huge total surface. At any moment the volume of blood flowing through arteries, capillaries and veins is the same, so in capillaries the speed is extremely low, allowing water exchange in every capillary.
  • Along the capillary (arterial end to venous end) there are different pressures on each side. Example: oxygen and CO2 equilibrate between blood and interstitium; water, through the pores, crosses freely; everything except proteins crosses. This slow equilibration is microcirculation, important to bring nutrients to the tissues and remove what must be eliminated.
  • [Slides] water and small nutrients cross the endothelium freely; pores up to 7 nm (smaller than albumin); proteins stay on the vascular side, creating colloidosmotic (oncotic) pressure; the two pressures are hydrostatic and oncotic; net filtration pressure (figure: see the slide).
